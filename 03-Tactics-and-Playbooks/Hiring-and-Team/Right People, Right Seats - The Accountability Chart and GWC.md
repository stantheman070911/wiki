# Right People, Right Seats - The Accountability Chart and GWC

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Tactics and Playbooks Index|Tactics and Playbooks]]

## One-line summary
"Right people in the right seats" is two independent tests, not one — right people share your core values, right seats means the role fits their Unique Ability — and separating them turns a vague personnel worry into a solvable, diagnosable problem.

## Context
Everyone repeats Collins's phrase; almost nobody has a procedure for it. Wickman's contribution is decomposition:

> Core Values + People Analyzer = **Right People**
> Unique Ability + Accountability Chart = **Right Seats**

Two tests, two tools, two different remedies. Confusing them is why founders keep good people in wrong roles and wrong people in right ones for years.

## Diagnosing the two failures

**Right person, wrong seat.** Someone who shares your values but isn't operating in their Unique Ability — promoted into a seat too big, outgrown a seat too small, or placed where their real strength is unused. Usually they're there because they've been around a long time and you like them, and you believed the promotion was a favour. It wasn't: *you were hindering his or her growth and the growth of the company.* The remedy is to move them. If there is genuinely no seat — and usually there is — you have to make the hard call. *You don't have the luxury of keeping people around simply because you like them.*

**Wrong person, right seat.** Competent, but doesn't share the values. This is the one that quietly poisons a culture, and it is why core values have to be defined before this exercise is worth running.

## The Accountability Chart

Not an org chart of who exists — a structure of **what seats the business needs to move forward in the next six to twelve months.** Three ground rules, all about detaching from the present:

1. **Look forward.** Not backward, not at today; the present distorts judgement.
2. **Detach from the existing business, your current role, and your ego.**
3. **Elevate above the business, look down, and decide for the long-term greater good.**

**Three functions, always.** Every organisation — startup or giant — runs on sales and marketing (generates business), operations (delivers and looks after the customer), and finance and administration (manages money in and out, plus infrastructure). *All three must be strong*, and Wickman walks the three failure cases: strong sales + weak operations loses customers out the back door; strong sales + strong operations + weak finance is *"$10 million in and $10 million out, or worse, $10 million in and $10.2 million out"*; weak sales with the other two strong is talented people waiting for something to happen.

**One name per seat.** *When more than one person is accountable, nobody is.* Leadership teams doing this the first time routinely find two or three names in a box — which is not a formatting problem, it's *"a root issue for your company's lack of growth or chaos."* Reduce to one.

**One name, two seats is fine.** The reverse is not. A founder starts in every seat; a bookkeeper can also do shipping. The constraint is time to do both well, and that constraint moves as you grow.

**The Integrator.** Because the three functions cannot operate independently, and because strong function heads generate *"great healthy friction and tension"* between them, someone must blend that friction into energy. Call it CEO, president, or GM — the Integrator is *"the glue that holds the company together."* Where there is also a **Visionary** (typically the founder, living in ideas, relationships, and culture), the Visionary/Integrator pair is EOS's most-copied structural idea.

## GWC — the right-seat filter

Once the chart says who is accountable for what, one filter decides whether the person fits: **Get it, Want it, Capacity to do it.**

- **Get it.** They genuinely understand the role, the culture, the systems, the pace, and how the job fits together. Not everyone does, and it cannot be taught quickly.
- **Want it.** They actually like the job, at fair compensation, for the responsibility it carries. *"In many instances, a manager feels the need to motivate, overpay, or beg a person to want it, when the reality is they don't."* Ego, your hopes, or their ignorance of what the job entails can all counterfeit it.
- **Capacity.** Time plus the mental, physical, and emotional capacity to do it well. A role needing 55 hours and a person willing to give 40 is a capacity failure, not an attitude failure. *"This is the Peter Principle at its finest."*

**A "no" on any of the three means it is not the right seat.** You can occasionally convert a no with time and money; usually you don't have the time to wait out the learning curve. And critically: *just because they currently have the job doesn't always mean that they get it, want it, and have the capacity.*

The diagnostic use: when you clearly articulate a seat — roles, responsibilities, expectations, measurables — and present it, the person either steps up or never will. If they don't, exactly one of G, W, or C is missing. Find which.

## Delegate and Elevate

Everyone has 100% of their working time, whatever number of hours that is for them. When a job requires 120%, that person *"is holding the organization back and hitting the ceiling"* — and burning out.

The move is to **delegate the excess 20% and elevate toward your Unique Ability.** Worked example: with operations and finance covered, a founder still spending 120% leading the team, managing sales, selling, and making marketing materials should keep leading and selling (the Unique Abilities) and hand off sales management and marketing production.

The precondition is uncomfortable and non-negotiable: *when you delegate and elevate, it's vital that you have the right person in the right seat. If you don't, you'll never feel completely comfortable letting go.* If the only reason you haven't let go is the person occupying the seat, that is the decision you're actually avoiding.

The monkey rule makes it enforceable: picture every direct report's problems as monkeys. **If someone walks into your office with a monkey, they need to walk out with it.** If they can't or won't, you've hired the wrong person.

Tyler Smith of Niche Retail is the textbook case — from printing and packing orders in his own basement, to delegating packing to his wife, then shipping, then an operations manager, a bookkeeper, a CFO, a COO, and a head of stores and marketing, across warehouses of 2,000, then 10,000, then 80,000 square feet. Each ceiling was broken by delegating and elevating rather than working harder.

## What this costs

Wickman's own tracking over eleven years: **80% of client leadership teams change within the first two years** of implementing EOS — half by removing someone, half by adding. Ronnisch Construction removed two of four leadership team members (both talented; both self-rated 4/10 on commitment), then turned over **40% of all employees** — and grew 70% in a subsequent year.

The honest reading is that this process is a filter, and filters remove people. Decide before you start whether you're willing to act on what it tells you, because running it and ignoring the result is worse than not running it.

## Application

1. Define core values first. Without them, "right people" has no test and the whole exercise collapses into taste.
2. Draw the Accountability Chart for **six to twelve months from now**, with names left off until the boxes are settled. Then place names.
3. Find every box with two names and resolve it to one this quarter. That list is usually your accountability problem in its entirety.
4. GWC every person on your leadership team, yourself included, and write which of the three is missing where it is. "They're not working out" is not an actionable diagnosis; "she doesn't want it" is.
5. Calculate your own percentage. If you're above 100%, name the specific 20% to delegate and the person receiving it. If no such person exists, that hire is your next Rock.
6. Introduce the monkey rule explicitly to your direct reports so returning problems isn't read as a rebuke.
