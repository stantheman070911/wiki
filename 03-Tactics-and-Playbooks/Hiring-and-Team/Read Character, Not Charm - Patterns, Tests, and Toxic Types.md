# Read Character, Not Charm - Patterns, Tests, and Toxic Types

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Tactics and Playbooks Index|Tactics and Playbooks]]

## One-line summary
People never do something just once — character is a compulsive pattern laid down before anyone could observe it — so judge whoever you hire, partner with, or work for on their repeated actions rather than their charm, intelligence, or reputation.

## Context

Greene's Law of Compulsive Behavior. Character is built in layers: genetic predisposition; the attachment schema stamped in the first years (Bowlby's free/autonomous, dismissing, enmeshed-ambivalent, disorganized — which shape how a person uses relationships to modulate stress); habits set in youth from the strategies those first layers made available; and finally a **fourth layer of disguise**, developed in adolescence once people notice their own flaws and learn to cover them — the timid type presenting as outgoing or domineering.

Two consequences matter for anyone hiring or partnering.

**First, weak traits are the durable ones.** Generosity, empathy and resilience *"require awareness and practice to truly become habits that can be relied upon,"* and life tends to wear them down — confidence without self-awareness becomes grandiosity. **The weakest parts of a character produce the compulsive behaviour precisely because they require no effort to maintain.**

**Second, the corollary that does all the work:** *people never do something just once.* They will excuse it as losing their head in the moment; they will repeat it. Often against their own interest — which is exactly what reveals the compulsion. Cassius Severus, the Roman orator, escalated his insults of officials until his books were burned and he was banished to Crete; there he continued, mailing diatribes back to Rome and insulting Cretan officials until the Senate moved him to an unpopulated rock in the Aegean, where he spent his last eight years. *"Like a dog that returns to his vomit is a fool that repeats his folly."*

## The illusions to strip first

We judge on what is most apparent, and what is apparent is usually the disguise:

- **Presented weaknesses read as strengths.** Brimming self-confidence turns out to be arrogance that cannot listen. Frankness turns out to be boorishness. Prudence turns out to be timidity afraid of the slightest criticism.
- **Charm blinds.** We want to like people, so we don't look deeper.
- **Reputation substitutes for the person.** We assume success implies generosity, intelligence, and desert. But *"successful people have just as many character flaws as anyone else"* — some are simply good at using others to mask incompetence.
- **Stated beliefs substitute for character.** *"A person can be a progressive liberal or a loving Christian and still be an intolerant tyrant at heart."* People bring the character they have to the position or creed they occupy.

## Where character shows

| Signal | What to read |
| --- | --- |
| **Actions over time** | The strongest indicator. Look for the same decisions repeating across a life — disappearing under stress, not finishing important work, turning belligerent when challenged, or rising when handed responsibility. Then research the past and re-read old incidents as instances of the pattern. *If you ignore the pattern it is your own fault.* |
| **Small matters** | Late on simple assignments → late on large projects. Irritated by small inconveniences → crumbles under large ones. Inattentive to small details → inattentive to important ones. |
| **Treatment of underlings** | Watch for a discrepancy between the persona and the behaviour toward people with no power. Jeb Magruder was impressed by Bob Haldeman's honesty and sharpness in a 1969 interview — then watched him turn near-hysterical and insulting at the staff when no golf cart was available. He wanted the job and ignored it, to his later cost. |
| **Stress and crisis** | Normal self-control drops and insecurities surface — fear of failure, anxiety about reputation, absence of resilience. Some rise instead. There is no way to know until the heat is on, so pay extra attention when it is. |
| **Power** | Lincoln: *"If you want to test a man's character, give him power."* On the way up, people play the courtier. At the top the restraints fall away. Lyndon Johnson, once Senate majority leader, made a point of talking only to the assistants of senators who had crossed him, and would walk off the floor during their speeches so others followed. **Power does not change people so much as reveal them** — the signs were there earlier. |
| **Choice of partner** | Rarely intellectual; it reflects attachment schema. Someone chosen to dominate, or to rescue, or to fill a parent role. Even outwardly incompatible choices have an internal logic — e.g. deep fear of abandonment leading someone to pick a partner they believe will cling regardless. |
| **Play** | Competitiveness that can't be switched off (including while driving) reveals insecurity when it appears in off hours. Watch how they lose — graciously, or with a flash of resentment in the body language. Do they bend rules? |

## Strong and weak character

**Strong character has a tensile quality — like good metal, it bends without breaking.** It emanates from personal security rather than bluster: takes criticism and learns, persists rigorously, stays open to new methods without abandoning principles, keeps its head in adversity, handles chaos without anxiety, keeps its word, organizes and finishes, and can subordinate personal interest to the group because its status doesn't feel at risk.

**Weak character** is easily overwhelmed and therefore unreliable; slippery and evasive; and — worst — *cannot be taught*, because learning implies criticism. They appear to accept instruction and revert to what they think best. You will hit that wall permanently.

> **Weak character neutralizes every other good quality.** A highly intelligent person of weak character produces good ideas and then crumbles under pressure, resents criticism, puts their own agenda first, or drives others out. *"Someone less charming and intelligent but of strong character will prove more reliable and productive over the long run."*

This is Greene's reading of Warren Buffett's method: look past the numbers to the CEO, and gauge resilience, dependability, and self-reliance above all. Franklin Roosevelt's choice of Eleanor is the personal version — he could have chosen on looks and chose openness to experience and determination instead.

## Five tests you can actually run

Greene's suggestions for gauging strength rather than waiting for it to reveal itself:

1. **A good-natured joke at their expense.** Gracious response, or a flash of resentment?
2. **Share strategic information or a rumour.** Does it travel? Do your ideas come back repackaged as theirs?
3. **Criticize directly.** Taken to heart and used, or met with overt resentment?
4. **Give an open-ended assignment** with less direction than usual, and watch how they organize thought and time.
5. **Assign something difficult or unfamiliar** and observe how they handle the anxiety.

Alongside these, the plainest question: **what have they actually completed?**

## The toxic types

Each presents a weakness as a virtue, which is why they're hard to spot until you're enmeshed. The defence is recognizing the type early and disengaging.

| Type | The lure | The reality |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Hyperperfectionist** | Works harder than anyone; explosive only in service of high standards. | Control, not standards. Cannot delegate; micromanages until the team is resentful and secretly resistant — exactly what they fear. Everything flows through them, so the group is disorganized and factional. Pattern of initial success, burnout, spectacular failure, and blaming others. |
| **The Relentless Rebel** | Hates authority, loves the underdog — appeals to the adolescent in all of us. | Recognizes no rules or precedent; convention is for the weak and stodgy. The opposition is compulsive, not principled. |
| **The Personalizer** | Sensitive, thoughtful, a little sad — you want to help. | The sensitivity runs only inward. Everything is taken personally and brooded over long after you've forgotten it. A childhood sense of never having received enough, carried into a life of resentment and expecting to be given things without asking. |
| **The Drama Magnet** | Unusual energy, animated, witty, great stories. | They learned as children that lasting attention came from entangling parents in troubles big enough to hold them. They live for confrontation, are always the victim in the telling, and their aim is to get hooks into you. |
| **The Big Talker** | Impressive ideas and projects; they need a backer. | Check the record for anything finished. Ambivalence: they crave attention and power but fear the effort and responsibility, and the anxious side wins — they slip away at the last moment, after you have committed, and blame society for not realizing their vision. |
| **The Sexualizer** | Refreshingly unrepressed energy; blurs work and pleasure. | Compulsive rather than free, usually rooted in early abuse. Every relationship is read as potentially sexual; sex functions as self-validation. |
| **The Pampered Prince/Princess** | A regal, calm, faintly superior air — pleasant to be near someone who seems destined for a crown. | You find yourself doing favours and unpaid work without understanding why. They are masters at getting others to pamper them, and revert to baby-like sulking when they don't get it. |
| **The Pleaser** | Nobody has ever been so nice and accommodating. | Niceness as defence, not affection — often learned deflecting punishing parents, along with practised, expert lying. The doubts start small (a missed commitment, sloppy work) and end in sabotage and talking behind your back. |
| **The Savior** | They recognized your need and arrived with the books, the strategy, the diet. Seductive at first. | Doubts begin the moment you assert independence. Often children who had to parent their own parent; the satisfaction comes from rescuing, and they have a nose for anyone who might need saving. |
| **The Easy Moralizer** | Eloquent outrage at injustice; conviction that attracts followers. | Cracks in the veneer — poor treatment of employees, condescension to a spouse, a private vice. Impulses punished in childhood become self-loathing projected outward; they resent people who are less repressed. The adult tell is **a complete absence of nuance**: people are good or evil, no middle ground. |

## The self-facing half

The law applies to you first. Your character cannot be removed — *"it is too deep"* — but awareness lets you interrupt the negative patterns and build new habits deliberately. Greene's practical program: examine your own past for the elements that formed you, look with rigour at your current relationships as the clearest evidence, **associate as much as possible with people of strong character so you assimilate their habits**, and periodically shake yourself up by doing the opposite of what you'd normally do — flexibility being itself a sign of strength.

Cesare Pavese, quoted at the chapter's close, states the stakes: *"What one does, one will do again… The agonizing thing in life is that it is our own decisions that throw us into this rut."* His example is exactly the trap Perkins attacks elsewhere in this vault — *"let us slave away and save every penny till we are thirty, then we will enjoy ourselves"* — because at thirty they will have a bent for avarice and hard work, and will never enjoy themselves.

## Application

- **Make character a screening criterion above charm, intelligence, and reputation** for anyone you hire, partner with, invest in, or work for. Write it into the scorecard rather than leaving it to impression.
- **Reconstruct the pattern before you decide.** Ask for a chronology, not a highlight reel: what has this person actually finished, and what happened at each exit? References should be asked about behaviour under stress, not competence.
- **Watch the small and the unwatched.** How they treat the receptionist, how they handle a small delay, whether they're late on trivial things.
- **Run at least two of the five tests** during any serious hiring or partnership process — the direct criticism and the open-ended assignment are the highest-yield.
- **Treat a promotion as a measurement instrument.** Give responsibility in a bounded way and observe what surfaces before you give it unbounded.
- **Name the toxic type out loud when you spot one**, and disengage early. The cost of these relationships is paid in the environment around them, not just in their own output.
- **Do the self-audit.** List the patterns that keep recurring in your own history, then deliberately do the opposite of your default once in a while to test whether the pattern is you or a habit.
