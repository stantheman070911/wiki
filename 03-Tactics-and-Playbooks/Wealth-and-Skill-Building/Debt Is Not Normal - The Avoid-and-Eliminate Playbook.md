# Debt Is Not Normal - The Avoid-and-Eliminate Playbook

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Tactics and Playbooks Index|Tactics and Playbooks]]

## One-line summary
Debt is the single largest obstacle to building wealth, and the only correct posture toward it is elimination by interest rate, funded by an aggressive cut to non-essential spending — which conveniently builds the exact platform you will later use to invest.

## Context
Collins' argument starts from a cultural claim rather than an arithmetic one: debt has been successfully marketed as a normal feature of adult life, and that normalisation is why most people never reach financial independence. His test of the marketing's power: *would the average new car cost $32,000 without E-Z financing, or a four-year degree $100,000+ without readily available student loans?* Easy credit does not merely let you buy the thing — it sets the price of the thing.

The consequence is that the first move on the path to wealth is not investing. It is getting to zero.

## Why debt costs more than its interest rate

Collins lists the damage beyond the payment:

- **Your lifestyle is already diminished.** Even on purely consumerist terms, interest has pre-spent a portion of every future paycheque.
- **You are enslaved to your current income source.** Debt must be serviced, so your ability to make choices congruent with your values is constrained. This is the direct inverse of [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Wealth-and-Skill-Building/F-You Money - The Three-Part Formula and the 25x Target|F-You Money]].
- **The psychological load is real** — shame, guilt, helplessness, the emotional profile of any addiction, made worse by being a prison of your own construction.
- **It distorts your time horizon.** Attention fixes on past mistakes, present pain, and looming disaster; the brain shuts down on the topic in the vague hope it resolves itself "later."

*"If your lifestyle matches — or god forbid exceeds — your income, you are no more than a gilded slave."*

## The interest-rate triage

If you already carry debt, whether to prepay is a capital-allocation question with a rough threshold:

| Interest rate | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Under 3% | Pay it off slowly; route the money to investments instead |
| 3–5% | Either. Do whatever feels most comfortable |
| Over 5% | Pay it off ASAP |

Collins adds a caveat against over-optimising: if debt control has historically been a problem for you, there is a lot to be said for simply getting it out of your life and moving on, numbers notwithstanding.

## The elimination playbook

1. **List all your debts.**
2. **Eliminate all non-essential spending — all of it.** The $5 coffees, $20 dinners and $12 cocktails are the fuel. "The more you pour, the sooner you stop burning."
3. **Rank the debts by interest rate.**
4. **Pay the minimum on everything, and throw all remaining money at the highest rate first.**
5. **Roll down the list** as each one dies.

And three deliberate refusals:

- **Don't pay a service to help.** Credit counsellors have no magic formula; only you can do the work, and their fee adds to the cost.
- **Don't chase consolidation,** even at a lower rate. The goal is a rate of zero, not a rate of 12% instead of 18%. Time spent on clever structuring is time not spent paying.
- **Don't pay smallest-balance-first for the psychological win.** Collins explicitly rejects the snowball method — *"better to adapt yourself and your attitudes to the numbers than to adapt the strategies to your psychological comfort levels."* This is the sharpest disagreement between his approach and most consumer debt advice, and it is a real trade: he is choosing arithmetic optimality over adherence.

## The three "good debts," examined

**Business loans.** Sometimes genuinely productive, but the history of commerce is littered with companies ruined by debt. Those who use it well use it with great care.

**Mortgages.** The classic "good debt," and the one Collins is most sceptical of. If financial independence is the goal, you seek *the least house that meets your needs, not the most house you can technically afford.* A house is an expensive indulgence, not an investment — the true cost includes taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, landscaping, remodelling, furnishing, and the opportunity cost on all the equity capital tied up in it. *"The more and greater things you allow in your life, the more of your time, money and life energy they demand."*

**Student loans.** The most dangerous, because they survive bankruptcy, follow you to the grave, and can garnish wages and even Social Security. Collins' 1968–72 state-school degree cost $1,200 a year all-in, coverable by a summer job; his daughter's 2010–14 state-school years averaged $40,000. Against six-fold general inflation over the period, four-year state-school cost rose **33-fold** — because easily obtained loans flooded the system with money. The second-order damage is that spiralling cost warped higher education from the pursuit of learning into job training that must justify the debt incurred.

## Application

1. **Reclassify debt from "normal" to "crisis."** Until it is gone, it is your top financial priority — ahead of investing, ahead of savings goals beyond a thin emergency buffer.
2. **Run the triage table on every balance you hold** and sort into prepay-now, prepay-slowly, and ignore.
3. **Cut non-essential spending to fund the attack, and keep the cut after the debt dies.** This is the whole trick: the lower-spending lifestyle you build to kill debt *is* the high-savings-rate platform that funds investing. When the debt is gone, you only redirect the same cash flow.
4. **Buy the least house that meets your need,** and price the full carrying cost, not the mortgage payment.
5. **Before signing student debt, size it against the realistic post-graduation income** — and treat "it survives bankruptcy" as the deciding fact, not a footnote.
6. **Never buy stocks on margin.** In the 1929 crash, margin buyers were wiped out entirely rather than merely halved.
