# The 4 Percent Rule - Withdrawal Rates and the Flexibility That Makes Them Safe

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Tactics and Playbooks Index|Tactics and Playbooks]]

## One-line summary
The 4% withdrawal rate is a floor so conservative that it usually leaves a fortune untouched, the real safe range runs 3–7% depending on stock allocation and inflation adjustments, and what actually determines your security is not the percentage you pick but your willingness to spend less in a bad year.

## Context
The rule comes from the **Trinity Study** (1998, updated 2009): three professors ran every 30-year period they had data for, across portfolios with different stock/bond mixes and different withdrawal rates, both with and without annual inflation raises.

The financial media seized on exactly one cell of that table — 4%, a 50/50 portfolio, inflation-adjusted — because it was the one that could be reported as near-certain. **96% of the time the portfolio survived the full 30 years.** It failed in only two of the 55 starting years measured: 1965 and 1966.

Collins' complaint is that reporting only the safe cell hid the more interesting finding. In the large majority of runs, 4% left *enormous* sums on the table.

## What the full table actually says

- **Under 3%** annually, with inflation raises, is *"as near a sure bet as anything in this life can be."*
- **Under 4% with 75% stocks / 25% bonds** is the setting if you want both a sure thing and your annual inflation raises.
- **Up to ~6%** works if you give up the inflation raises, on a 50/50 mix.
- **Up to 7%** is viable per the study's own authors — *provided you stay alert and flexible*, cutting withdrawals when the market dives.
- **Past 7%,** in Collins' phrasing, "your future will include dining on dog food."
- **Stocks are what make the portfolio survive.** This is the finding most people miss: a conservative portfolio is not a safer one over 30 years.

The remaining-balance tables are the part worth internalising. On $1,000,000 at a 4% withdrawal rate, the *median* ending value after 30 years:

| Allocation | Straight 4% | 4% with inflation raises |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100% stocks | $15,610,000 | $10,075,000 |
| 75/25 | $10,743,000 | $5,968,000 |
| 50/50 | $7,100,000 | $2,971,000 |

Most of the time, taking only 4% means leaving buckets of money to your heirs. That's excellent if it's your goal — and it means you underspent your life if it wasn't.

## The condition everyone omits: fees

The Trinity numbers assume you keep the market's return. Wade Pfau's decomposition, which Collins quotes:

> the 50-50 portfolio over 30 years with 4% inflation-adjusted withdrawals had a 96% success rate without fees, 84% success rate with 1% fees, and 65% success rate with 2% fees.

Which yields the blunt conclusion: **using the Trinity projections with a portfolio built from anything other than low-cost index funds is invalid.** A 2% advisory fee converts a 4-in-100 failure rate into a 35-in-100 one. It is the single largest lever on retirement survival in the entire study, and it is entirely under your control.

## The mechanics of pulling the money

The withdrawal itself is administratively trivial — a standing transfer, or dividends and distributions routed to checking, or manual transfers as needed. Collins' family simply logs in and moves money when the current account gets low, and tracks spending by category in a plain spreadsheet so they can see what to cut if they need to.

His sequencing principles, which generalise past the specific account names:

1. **Spend non-investment income first.** Any earned money in "retirement" reduces the draw and buys the portfolio more time to grow.
2. **Off-load leftover legacy holdings first, ugliest first,** slowly enough to manage capital gains. Dump anything sitting at a loss immediately and use the loss to offset gains elsewhere.
3. **Then draw from the taxable account,** letting the tax-advantaged accounts keep compounding.
4. **Judge the withdrawal against the whole portfolio, not the account you're drawing from.** If the taxable account is a third of your assets, a 4% total draw is 12% of that account — and that's fine.
5. **Site each fund where its tax treatment is best:** bonds in the tax-deferred account (tax-inefficient), stocks in the Roth (last money spent, most growth, best thing to inherit) and in taxable (more tax-efficient).
6. **Reinvest distributions inside tax-advantaged accounts, but pay taxable-account distributions straight to your bank** — reinvesting a taxed distribution only to withdraw the same sum weeks later is pure friction.

## What not to do: set it and forget it

*"I think it is nuts to just set up a 4% withdrawal schedule and let it run regardless of what happens in the real world."*

Collins' argument is symmetry with working life. If your salary were cut 50%, you would obviously adjust your spending; if markets halve your portfolio, the same reasoning applies. And it runs the other way too — in strong years he'll spend more than 4%, knowing the wind is at his back. He reassesses once a year, at the same time he rebalances.

His own rate has run **north of 5%** in recent years, with reasons attached: a kid in college (a large, ending expense), accelerated travel (*"at my age I am more worried about running out of time than money"* — and an easy expense to cut), and Social Security coming online later. He expects it to drop under 4%.

> 4% is only a guide. Sensible flexibility is what provides security.

The variables that actually set your safe rate are therefore behavioural, not financial: can you tighten your belt, can you find supplementary work, are you willing to relocate somewhere cheaper. *"If you are locked into certain income needs, unwilling or unable to ever work again and your roots go too deep to ever seek out greener pastures, you'll need to be much more careful."*

## Application

1. **Set your target as 25× annual spending** (the inverse of 4%) and treat it as the entry price, not a precise threshold.
2. **Choose your rate from the 3–7% band by flexibility, not by optimism.** Rigid life, rigid costs, no work option → 3–4%. Genuinely adjustable spending and a willingness to move or earn → you can carry 5–6%.
3. **Strip fees before you trust any withdrawal projection.** Recompute your plan's survival odds at your actual all-in cost; if it's above 1%, fix that before anything else.
4. **Split your spending into fixed and discretionary** in advance, so that "cut back in a downturn" is a list you already have rather than a decision made in a panic.
5. **Review annually alongside rebalancing** — same date, same sitting. Adjust the draw up in good years and down in bad ones.
6. **Never plan on a specific market return.** Collins uses 11.9% for illustration and repeats twice, in capitals, that it must not be treated as a forecast.
