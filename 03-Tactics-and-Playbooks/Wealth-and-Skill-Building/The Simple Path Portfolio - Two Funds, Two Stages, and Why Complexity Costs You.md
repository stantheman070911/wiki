# The Simple Path Portfolio - Two Funds, Two Stages, and Why Complexity Costs You

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Tactics and Playbooks Index|Tactics and Playbooks]]

## One-line summary
A broad low-cost stock index fund while you are accumulating, plus a bond index fund once you are living off the pot, beats almost every professionally managed alternative — because complexity in finance exists to pay the seller, and every layer of fees is a permanent compounding drag you can simply refuse to pay.

## Context
Collins' organising claim: *"Complex investments exist only to profit those who create and sell them. Further, not only are they more costly to the investor, they are less effective."*

The evidence he leans on is a Vanguard study starting in 1998 that followed all 1,540 actively managed equity funds then in existence. Over the next 15 years, **only 55% survived, and only 18% both survived and beat the index.** 82% failed to outperform — and 100% charged fees to try. Longer studies are harsher: Barras, Scaillet and Wermers looked at 2,076 actively managed US stock funds over 1976–2006 and found only 0.6% showed any skill at beating the index, a result *"statistically indistinguishable from zero."*

So the portfolio below is not a compromise for lazy people. Collins is explicit: *"I'd happily put in more effort for more return. More effort for less return? Not so much."*

## The three considerations and the three tools

Before choosing anything, settle three questions: **which stage are you in** (accumulation or preservation), **what risk level can you actually hold**, and **is your horizon long or short**. Collins' guidance on the third is that almost everyone underestimates it — a 20-year-old faces perhaps 80 investing years; a healthy 60-year-old still faces 30.

Then there are only three tools:

| Tool | Job |
| --- | --- |
| **Total stock market index fund** (his: VTSAX) | The wealth builder, and the inflation hedge |
| **Total bond market index fund** (his: VBTLX) | Income, a smoother ride, and the deflation hedge |
| **Cash** | Routine expenses and emergencies; king in deflation, eroded by inflation — hold as little as your comfort allows |

A crucial framing on the middle column: **stocks and bonds are hedges against the two opposite macro disasters.** Deflation destroys through collapsing prices (the 1929 Big Ugly Event: down 90%, not recovered until the mid-1950s); hyperinflation destroys through a collapsing currency. Stocks are a reasonable inflation hedge because you own businesses whose assets and products reprice; bonds pay you back money that buys more in a deflation. That is *why* the second fund is there — not "diversification" as a slogan.

## The two portfolios

**Wealth Accumulation Portfolio — 100% stocks.**
One fund. Add to it relentlessly. Ignore it otherwise. *"Put all your eggs in one basket and forget about it."* This is aggressive by design: you have decades, you are adding new money (which itself smooths the ride), and drops are the "stocks on sale" that new money buys.

**Wealth Preservation Portfolio — Collins' own, roughly:**

- ~75% stocks
- ~20% bonds
- ~5% cash

Tune it by temperament: more bonds for a smoother ride and a lower long-run return, more stocks for growth. Rebalance about once a year — Collins uses his wife's birthday, "random and easy to remember" — plus any time the market makes a 20%+ move. Do the selling inside tax-advantaged accounts wherever possible so rebalancing doesn't trigger capital gains.

**The honest caveat he includes against his own advice:** some studies show that holding 10–25% bonds slightly outperforms 100% stocks *and* is slightly less volatile. He still recommends 100% stocks in accumulation, on the grounds that the projected difference is tiny, unpredictable in advance, and not worth the added complexity — but he says *"you'll get no argument from me"* from anyone who takes the 80/20 route.

## The two design choices he defends against the consensus

**No international fund.** Three reasons: added currency risk; added accounting risk (weaker regulatory regimes, and even the US produces Enrons); and roughly double the expense ratio. Plus, he argues, it's already covered — the largest US companies in the index are international businesses, many earning 50%+ overseas, and world markets have become increasingly correlated as economies knit together, which erodes the diversification case. If you disagree, hold a total-world fund and skip the domestic one rather than layering both.

**Target Retirement Funds are a legitimate one-decision answer.** A TRF is a fund of index funds that rebalances toward conservatism automatically as the target date approaches. Collins gives them his stamp of approval — pick an earlier date for a more conservative glide path, a later one for more aggressive. He prefers the two-fund approach only because expense ratios are lower, it avoids the international sleeve, and it lets him site bonds in the tax-advantaged account. **If you won't reliably rebalance, the TRF is better than the theoretically better portfolio you won't maintain.**

## If you can't buy the specific funds

The fund ticker is not the point; the *portfolio* is. Collins' fallback ladder:

1. A different share class of the same index portfolio (investor shares, ETF, institutional) — same holdings, different minimums and costs. Watch ETF trading commissions and spreads, which can offset a lower expense ratio.
2. Any low-cost total-market or S&P 500 index fund from another provider — competitive pressure means nearly every major company now offers one. Participate in an employer plan even if its menu is mediocre, *at least up to the match*, and roll it out when you leave.
3. Outside the US, a total-world index fund — but check that it includes the US market, since many funds marketed as "international" are explicitly ex-US and designed to supplement a domestic holding you don't have.

## Why the fees matter more than they look

The average actively managed fund charges roughly **1.25%**; a total market index fund around **0.05%**. Collins' way of making 1% feel real: *once you are living on 4% of your assets, a 1% fee is a full **25% of your income.***

The extraction layer has three legitimate business models, and all three work against you:

- **Commissions ("loads").** A typical 5.75% front load means $10,000 invested puts $9,425 to work. Stack a 1% recurring trail and a 1.5% expense ratio and you've surrendered 8.25% of capital at the start. Insurance-wrapped investments — annuities, whole and universal life — pay commissions as high as 10%, buried where you never see them.
- **AUM fees (1–2% of assets).** On a $100,000 stake over 20 years at the historical average, a 2% fee turns $947,549 into $660,623 — **$286,926 gone**, because you lose the fee *and* everything the fee would have earned. It also carries its own conflict: an advisor paid on assets has a standing reason to advise against paying off your mortgage or funding your kid's tuition.
- **Hourly fees.** The cleanest of the three and the one advisors like least, precisely because clients notice writing the cheque. You are less likely to be cheated, but you still can't tell whether the advice is good.

*"By the time you know enough to pick a good one, you know enough to handle your finances yourself."*

## The five rules of not being conned

Collins treats fraud as a design constraint of the financial world, not an edge case — a field with access to people's life savings is a magnet. His rules:

1. **Everybody can be conned.** The moment you decide it can't happen to you, you have become the most attractive kind of target. *"That's the first rule."*
2. **You are most likely to be conned in your area of expertise.** Con artists target people to whom the scam naturally appeals, and people are least cautious where they feel competent. Many of Madoff's victims were financial professionals.
3. **Con artists don't look like con artists.** They look like the safest, most trustworthy, most comforting people imaginable. You will welcome them warmly.
4. **99% of what they say is true.** The best lies are buried in truth; the con is in the fine print.
5. **If it looks too good to be true, it is.** Not "might be." Is.

The canonical mechanism, in case you think you'd spot it: send 1,000 letters predicting a volatile stock, half up and half down. Write again only to the 500 who got the right call, splitting them again. By letter six, sixteen people have watched you be right six consecutive times and are begging to invest.

## Application

1. **Pick your stage first, then your allocation.** Accumulating: 100% broad stock index (or 80/20 if you'll rebalance). Preserving: add bonds to the level that lets you sleep, around 75/20/5.
2. **Consolidate to the fewest funds that do the job.** Every additional holding buys tracking work and, usually, cost.
3. **Look up the expense ratio of everything you own today** and multiply the difference against an index fund by your expected holding period. That number is the decision.
4. **Rebalance annually on a fixed, memorable date,** plus after any 20%+ market move — and do the trades inside tax-advantaged accounts.
5. **If you won't do step 4, buy a target-date fund and stop.** The maintained simple plan beats the abandoned optimal one.
6. **If you have an advisor, ask which of the three models pays them,** and price it as a percentage of your future retirement income, not of assets.
7. **Have the con conversation with your partner** — Collins' argument for index funds includes leaving behind a portfolio a surviving spouse can run on autopilot, with nothing that requires trusting a stranger.
