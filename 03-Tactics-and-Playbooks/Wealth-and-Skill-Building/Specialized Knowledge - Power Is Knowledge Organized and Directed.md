# Specialized Knowledge - Power Is Knowledge Organized and Directed

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Tactics and Playbooks Index|Tactics and Playbooks]]

## One-line summary
"Knowledge is power" is false — knowledge is *potential* power, and it converts only when it is specialized, organized into a plan, and pointed at a definite end; it also does not have to be inside your own head.

## Context
Hill's answer to the person who believes their lack of formal education is disqualifying. His evidence: university faculties hold nearly all the general knowledge civilization possesses and are not wealthy, because they specialize in teaching knowledge rather than organizing and using it. Written in 1937, when "the order of the day is SPECIALIZATION" was a fresh observation about employers preferring specialists with track records over straight-A generalists.

## Key insights

- **General vs. specialized knowledge.** General knowledge, however vast, does little for the accumulation of money. Only knowledge that is specialized to the service you intend to sell, and directed through a plan of action, becomes power.
- **You don't need to hold the knowledge yourself.** Carnegie knew nothing about the technical side of steel and did not care to; he sourced it from his Master Mind group. Ford, cross-examined in court to prove him ignorant, pointed to the row of push-buttons on his desk that would summon a man who could answer any question — and asked why he should clutter his mind with facts he could requisition. Hill's conclusion: anyone who knows where to get knowledge when needed, and how to organize it into a plan, is educated.
- **The real definition of "educate."** From *educo* — to draw out, to develop from within. An educated person is one who can acquire what they want without violating the rights of others, not one holding a credential. Degrees represent "miscellaneous knowledge."
- **Learning does not stop at graduation.** Successful people in every calling never stop acquiring knowledge related to their major purpose; the unsuccessful assume the acquiring period ended with school. Schooling "does little more than put one in the way of learning how to acquire practical knowledge."
- **Free things are undervalued.** Hill's observation that public schools and libraries fail to impress because they cost nothing, and that the self-discipline of a paid, deliberate programme of study is itself part of the value. His own anecdote: he abandoned a correspondence course in advertising and only finished it because the school kept billing him — and the training later paid.
- **Employers read self-directed study as a leadership signal.** Someone who gives up spare time to study at home has demonstrated ambition, which is the one weakness Hill says has no remedy.

## Where to buy knowledge

Hill's list of dependable sources, in his order:

1. Your own experience and education.
2. Experience and education available through the cooperation of others — the [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Hiring-and-Team/The Master Mind Alliance - Borrowed Brains Held in Harmony|Master Mind alliance]].
3. Colleges and universities.
4. Public libraries — where all the knowledge civilization has organized already sits.
5. Special training courses, especially part-time and distance study, chosen for flexibility and for the consultation access they carry.

## Specialized knowledge is cheap; ideas are not

Hill's sharpest point in this chapter: specialized knowledge is among the most plentiful and cheapest forms of service you can buy — "if you doubt this, consult the payroll of any university." What is scarce is the imagination to combine specialized knowledge with an idea into an organized plan. His illustration: an unemployed grocery salesman took an accounting course, contracted with 100 small merchants to keep their books for a nominal monthly fee, then built a fleet of bookkeeping offices fitted into delivery trucks. Specialized knowledge plus imagination; neither alone.

## Application

1. Name the specialized knowledge your definite purpose actually requires — start from the goal, not from what looks interesting.
2. Decide for each piece whether to **acquire** it or **ally** for it. Anything that is a one-time input, or that someone else has spent a decade on, is usually cheaper to buy than to learn.
3. For what you will acquire, pick the source by consultation access and feedback, not prestige.
4. Pay for something. A deliberate, costed programme with deadlines finishes; free open-ended study does not.
5. Keep an explicit "how will this be organized into action" line against each thing you learn. Knowledge that never enters a plan has produced nothing.
