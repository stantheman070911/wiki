# F-You Money - The Three-Part Formula and the 25x Target

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Tactics and Playbooks Index|Tactics and Playbooks]]

## One-line summary
Financial independence is not a retirement date but a stock of money large enough to say no — reached by one three-part formula (spend less than you earn, invest the surplus, avoid debt), measured by one number (25× your annual spending), and paced almost entirely by your savings rate rather than your income.

## Context
Collins' pursuit of financial independence *"has never been about retirement."* He likes working. It has been about options — about being able to say no, and about not being the middle-aged guy laid off after twenty years and almost instantly broke.

The term comes from James Clavell's *Noble House*, where a character seeks enough money to be free of the demands of others. Collins' key departure from the novel is that **F-You Money is not a single threshold.** His first tranche was $5,000 at age 25, saved from a $10,000 salary. It was not enough to retire on. It was enough to resign over a denied leave request — at which point his boss reopened the negotiation and he got a six-week leave plus a permanent month of annual vacation. *"F-You Money not only paid for the trip, it bought me room to negotiate."*

This is the entry's central reframe: **the first useful amount of F-You Money is small, and its return is bargaining power.**

## The formula

> Spend less than you earn — invest the surplus — avoid debt

That is the whole thing. Collins credits three things with getting his family to independence despite a long list of investing mistakes:

1. An unwavering **50% savings rate**.
2. **Avoiding debt** — never even a car payment.
3. Eventually embracing **indexing**.

Note what is not on the list: picking well, timing well, or earning spectacularly.

## Independence is as much about needs as about assets

*"Being independently wealthy is every bit as much about limiting needs as it is about how much money you have."* The parable Collins opens the book with is the monk and the minister: the minister tells the monk that if he learned to cater to the king he wouldn't have to live on rice and beans; the monk replies that if the minister learned to live on rice and beans he wouldn't have to cater to the king.

The evidence for the "needs" half is that the relationship to income is weak in both directions. A friend of Collins' received an $800,000 annual bonus and genuinely could not make ends meet — he was burning $175,000 a quarter, so financial independence was a distant dream. Meanwhile at the other end, someone content living on $7,000 a year is independent at $175,000 of assets.

## The 25x target and the math of a modest income

Financial independence arrives when **your assets equal 25× your annual spending** — the inverse of the 4% withdrawal rate. Collins' worked case, deliberately built on a low income:

- You earn **$25,000** and organise your life to live on **$12,500**.
- Two things happen at once: needs halved, and $12,500/year of investable cash created.
- Your target is $12,500 ÷ 4% = **$312,500**.
- At the market's 1975–2015 average, you reach it in roughly **11.5 years**.
- If you then stop saving entirely, double your spending to the full $25,000, and simply leave the pot alone, it grows to ~$962,000 in ten more years — which supports $38,478 a year at 4%. You can quit *and* give yourself a raise.

The lesson is not the specific numbers — Collins is emphatic that the 11.9% historical average must not be treated as a forecast — but the structural point: **halving your spending does double duty**, cutting the target and funding the contributions simultaneously.

## Savings rate is the throttle

Collins' recommended rate is **50%**, and he notes that the more committed reach 70–80%. The mechanism he cares about is twofold: *"you learn to live on less even as you have more to invest."*

The case study in the book is a 26-year-old earning $70,000, debt-free, saving 24%. Collins pushes him to 50% and points out the leverage available at that moment: *"He is employed, young and childless. Never will he be in a stronger position to take it to the next level."* The minimum commitment, if 50% is out of reach: **pledge that every future salary increase goes to investments, not to lifestyle.**

## The two stages, not the two ages

Collins refuses to divide investing life by age. He divides it by stage:

- **Wealth Accumulation Stage** — you have earned income, you are adding money.
- **Wealth Preservation Stage** — earned income has slowed or stopped, and investments are left to grow or called on for income.

These are not sequential-once. You might take a sabbatical, take a lower-paid job you love, launch a business, or return to work after five years out; Collins moved between them repeatedly across his career. Your allocation follows the stage, not the birthday — which is the argument against the standard "subtract your age from 100" rule.

## The bucket priority for contributions

Where to put the money, in order of attractiveness (US account types; the *ordering logic* travels even when the account names don't):

1. **Employer plan up to the full match** — the match is free money, so it outranks everything.
2. **Deductible IRA** — same tax treatment as the employer plan, but you control the investment choice rather than being limited to the plan menu. This matters most when the plan's fund options are poor.
3. **Back to the employer plan, up to the annual maximum.**
4. **Ordinary taxable account** — taxed on dividends and distributions, but accessible any time without penalty, which is what makes it the bridge for anyone retiring before traditional retirement age.

Two operational notes: automate the transfers, because *"it is easier and makes it more likely he'll stay the course"*; and if you must liquidate legacy actively-managed funds to consolidate, the capital-gains bill is usually a small, one-off cost worth paying — though a large enough position deserves a real analysis of tax liability against ongoing fee drag.

## Application

1. **Name your first tranche, and make it small.** Don't set "retirement" as the target — set the amount that would let you resign, take a leave, or refuse a bad assignment. Collins' was two years' savings.
2. **Compute your 25× number** from actual annual spending, not income. Recompute it whenever your spending baseline moves; every $1,000/year of lifestyle you add costs $25,000 of freedom.
3. **Set a savings rate as a fixed first claim on income,** targeting 50%. If that's impossible now, commit that 100% of the next raise goes to investments.
4. **Attack the spending side and the asset side as one move** — they are the same lever pulled twice.
5. **Fund in bucket order:** match, then the account you control, then the plan maximum, then taxable. Automate all of it.
6. **Identify your current stage, not your age,** and re-assess allocation whenever the stage shifts.
