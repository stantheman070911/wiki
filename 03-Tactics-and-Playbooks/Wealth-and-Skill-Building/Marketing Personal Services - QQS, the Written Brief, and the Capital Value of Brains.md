# Marketing Personal Services - QQS, the Written Brief, and the Capital Value of Brains

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Tactics and Playbooks Index|Tactics and Playbooks]]

## One-line summary
If your income comes from personal services you are a merchant selling a product, and the same rules apply: know the merchandise, price it by the value of service rendered rather than by what you need, and present it as a prepared case instead of an application.

## Context
The back half of Hill's Organized Planning chapter, written for people who had lost everything in the Depression and had nothing but their services to sell. Its premise — that "jobs are now partnerships" between employer, employee, and the public they both serve, and that the "go-getter" has been replaced by the "go-giver" — reads as an early statement of what later became personal branding. The mechanics survive better than the 1937 packaging (photographs pasted into leather-bound briefs, offers to work unpaid on probation).

## The QQS formula

Quality + Quantity + Spirit = complete salesmanship of service. Hill's claim is that price and duration of employment are set by all three, and that the third is the one people neglect.

| Element | Definition | Failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Quality** | Every detail of the work performed as efficiently as possible, with greater efficiency always in view | Competent but static output |
| **Quantity** | The *habit* of rendering all the service you are capable of, increasing as skill grows | Doing exactly the job description |
| **Spirit** | The habit of agreeable, harmonious conduct that induces cooperation from others | Being right and unbearable |

Carnegie, per Hill, would not retain anyone — however great the quantity or quality of their work — who did not work in a spirit of harmony. Hill goes further: pleasing conduct can partly compensate for deficits in the other two, but nothing substitutes for it.

## Sell yourself as a prepared case, not an application

Hill's central tactical claim: it is not the lawyer who knows the most law who wins, but the one who best prepares the case. His procedure for getting a specific position:

1. Decide exactly what job you want — **if it doesn't exist, propose creating it**.
2. Choose the specific company or person you want to work for.
3. Study them: policies, personnel, advancement paths, competitors.
4. Analyse yourself and figure out **what you can offer** — services, ideas, developments you can actually deliver.
5. **Forget about "a job" and whether there's an opening.** Concentrate on what you can give.
6. Put the plan on paper, in full detail, neatly.
7. Present it to the person with authority to act.

The written brief should state education and why you specialized as you did, relevant experience, references, the *specific* position sought (never "just a position" — that advertises a lack of specialized qualification), your reasons for being qualified for that particular position, and demonstrated knowledge of the prospective employer's business gathered by research beforehand.

Hill's own case study: a woman built a fifty-page plan book for her son — researched at the library and by interviewing the prospective employer's competitors, containing half a dozen concrete suggestions for the employer (which were adopted). He was hired at the first interview, as a junior executive, at a salary he set. Hill's point is about time, not flattery: the preparation cost weeks and saved roughly ten years of starting at the bottom, where "the outlook is not so very bright or encouraging" and the habit of routine kills ambition before opportunity ever sees you.

## Worth versus want

- **Your requirements have nothing to do with your worth.** Many people mistake their wants for their just dues. Value is set entirely by your ability to render useful service, or your capacity to induce others to render it.
- **Before negotiating a raise or moving, be sure you are worth more than you now receive.** Hill's cautionary anecdote is the applicant who, offered "all you are worth after a week's trial," refused because he was already getting more than that.
- **The capital value of brains.** Hill's rough valuation: multiply annual income by 16⅔, on the reasoning that money rents at about 6% a year, so your annual income represents 6% of your capital value. The arithmetic is a period artefact; the underlying framing is not — earning capacity is an asset that cannot be depreciated by a downturn, stolen, or spent, unlike financial capital.

## Application

- Rate yourself on QQS this quarter. Most people are strong on quality, weak on quantity as a habit, and blind on spirit. Ask someone who won't flatter you which of the three is capping your price.
- Before your next role or client pitch, spend the week doing the research and write the brief: their business, the specific problem you'll own, and three concrete suggestions you'd implement. Lead with what you'll give, not with whether there's an opening.
- Apply for a named position with named responsibilities. If it doesn't exist, define it and propose it.
- Answer honestly, once a year: *if I had been the purchaser of my own services this year, would I be satisfied with the purchase?* — and *in what ways did I render more and better service than I was paid to render?*
