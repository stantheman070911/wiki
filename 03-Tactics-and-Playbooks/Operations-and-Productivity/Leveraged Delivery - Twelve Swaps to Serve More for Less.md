# Leveraged Delivery - Twelve Swaps to Serve More for Less

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Tactics and Playbooks Index|Tactics and Playbooks]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Operations-and-Productivity/Operations and Productivity Map|Operations and Productivity]]

## One-line summary
Fix delivery by changing the *terms* of delivery, not by hiring more people to do it the same way — find the expensive components customers don't actually value, swap each for a cheaper form, and keep only what they'd notice losing.

## Context
Once demand and conversion are leveraged, delivery becomes the constraint: quality feels at risk, the founder is still in the room, and headcount is the only lever anyone reaches for. But most delivery cost sits in *how* the work is packaged — synchronous, custom, one-to-one, unlimited — rather than in the part customers are paying for. Three levers change that: the product terms, the technology, and the people.

## Lever 1 — Rewrite the terms of delivery

Twelve swaps, each of which alone can produce a several-fold gain in throughput, and which compound when stacked. Price usually holds; scalability doesn't.

| From | To |
| --- | --- |
| One-to-one | One-to-many |
| Done-for-you | Done-with-you, or do-it-yourself |
| Phone support | Email, chat, or community |
| In person | Remote or hybrid |
| Reactive (on demand) | Scheduled |
| Live | Recorded |
| Synchronous | Asynchronous |
| Custom | Templated |
| Unlimited scope | Defined limits with priced change-orders |
| Expert-led | Expert-led pods (two experts, five helpers each) |
| Scattered tasks | Batched work |
| Many small touches | Fewer, denser touches |

**The selection rule:** keep only what the customer genuinely values, and find the lowest-cost substitute for everything else — which is usually far more of the delivery than you assume. Trading three low-touch interactions for one high-touch one often *raises* perceived value while cutting cost.

**The counterintuitive case.** A founder onboarding medical practices ran seven remote calls per client, plus the email coordination between them. Replacing all seven with one day in person — the client flies out three technical staff — was faster, cheaper, and unambiguously perceived as higher value. Roughly 5x throughput. Similarly, an agency doing three in-person recording days per quarter moved the two prep days to recorded/virtual and kept one day live: 3x leverage from one swap.

## Lever 2 — Automate workflows, not job titles

Stop thinking in org charts (circles, dots, reporting lines) and start thinking in workflows.

1. Take one role and list every behavior it performs — an editor might have ten.
2. Mark each behavior as rules-based/repetitive or judgment-intensive.
3. Automate the rules-based ones; keep the judgment ones human.
4. The role doesn't disappear — it concentrates on the two behaviors where the expertise is actually necessary, and output per person rises five- to tenfold.

The test of success is that the process runs without you: one operator writing a checklist of an internal-transfer process, loading it as a prompt, and handing it to a recruiter produced two placements that week with no founder involvement. A single person now runs a channel publishing five mid-form and fifteen short-form pieces a day.

## Lever 3 — Run talent acquisition as a marketing funnel

If people are the delivery constraint, the supply side needs the same rigor as the demand side. The two processes are structurally identical:

| Customer acquisition | Talent acquisition |
| --- | --- |
| Lead generation | Application generation |
| Lead nurturing | Applicant nurturing |
| Sales call | Interview |
| Customer onboarding | Employee onboarding |
| Customer success | Training and management |
| Retention | Employee retention |

The job ad *is* an ad; the offer conversation *is* a sale. Businesses that struggle to fill roles are almost always running occasional job postings instead of a repeatable funnel — and would never accept that standard on the customer side.

## Application
1. List every component of your current delivery and mark which ones a customer would complain about losing. Everything unmarked is a candidate for a swap.
2. Pick two or three swaps from the table rather than one; they compound.
3. Before adding a swap, check whether the cheaper form is also *worse* for the customer — sometimes the denser, more expensive-looking option (one in-person day) is cheaper for you and better for them.
4. Define scope limits and priced change-orders explicitly, especially in done-for-you and construction-style services where scope and time creep silently.
5. Decompose one role this month into its behaviors, automate the rules-based half, and measure output per person before and after.
6. If headcount is the binding constraint, build the talent funnel — ad, nurture, structured interview, onboarding, retention — before you post another job listing.
