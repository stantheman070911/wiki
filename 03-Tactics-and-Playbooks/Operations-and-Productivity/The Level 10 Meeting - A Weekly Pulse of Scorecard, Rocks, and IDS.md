# The Level 10 Meeting - A Weekly Pulse of Scorecard, Rocks, and IDS

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Tactics and Playbooks Index|Tactics and Playbooks]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Operations-and-Productivity/Operations and Productivity Map|Operations and Productivity]]

## One-line summary
A fixed 90-minute weekly meeting that spends 25 minutes reporting and 60 minutes solving — because the whole design exists to stop a leadership team from discussing problems in the reporting phase.

## Context
This is where EOS's Data, Issues, and Traction components are actually operated. Everything else in *Traction* produces inputs to this meeting; this meeting is what makes the rest stick. Its two supporting artefacts, the Scorecard and Rocks, are worth stating first because the agenda is meaningless without them.

## The two inputs

### Rocks — a 90-Day World

**Three to seven priorities per person, per quarter** (preferably closer to three). The company has Rocks, the leadership team has Rocks, then everyone else does — set in that order, which is how alignment is produced rather than announced.

The image is a glass cylinder with rocks, gravel, sand, and water. Pour water, sand, and gravel first and the rocks won't fit. Put the rocks in first and everything fits. *When everything is important, nothing is important.*

The reason for 90 days is psychological. A cotton-picking crew facing acres of field would throw a stick as far as they could and *"pick to the stick"* — then throw it again. A quarter is a stick. Wickman took the term from Verne Harnish's *Mastering the Rockefeller Habits*, who took the analogy from Covey.

### Scorecard — five to fifteen weekly numbers

*A handful of numbers that can tell you at a glance how your business is doing*, reviewed weekly, with **every person in the company owning at least one number.**

The critical distinction: a **P&L is a trailing indicator** — its data arrives after the fact and you cannot change the past. A weekly activity-based Scorecard lets you change the future. Wickman's illustration of flying blind is the pilot announcing that the gauges are out, he has no idea of speed, direction, or fuel — *"the good news is that we're making great time!"*

His non-business precedent is Giuliani's CompStat: the NYPD had tracked arrests and 911 response times, both trailing indicators reported quarterly or annually, by which point the crime pattern had already changed. Daily and weekly activity numbers let precinct commanders see patterns and redeploy.

## The Level 10 agenda

Same day, same time, same agenda, **90 minutes**, every week.

| Item | Time | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Segue | 5 min | Each person shares good news, personal and business |
| Scorecard | 5 min | Review the 5–15 numbers; anything off-track **drops to IDS** |
| Rock review | 5 min | Each Rock reported "on track" or "off track" only; off-track **drops to IDS** |
| Customer/employee headlines | 5 min | Short news, good or bad; anything needing action **drops to IDS** |
| To-Do list | 5 min | Last week's seven-day commitments, "done" or "not done" |
| **IDS** | **60 min** | Identify, Discuss, Solve — the actual work |
| Conclude | 5 min | Recap to-dos, cascading messages, rate the meeting 1–10 |

**The entire design is the word "drops."** The reporting items exist only to surface issues onto the list; discussion in those items is forbidden. *"The biggest pitfall with most teams is that they launch right into discussing and trying to solve an issue. You must fight that urge."* Twenty-five minutes of reporting buys sixty minutes of undivided problem-solving.

Two roles are required: one person runs the agenda and keeps the team on it; one person maintains the agenda, Scorecard, Rock Sheet, To-Do and Issues lists so they are in front of everyone each week.

Operating rules worth copying verbatim:

- **Start on time, always.** *"The only reasons for missing the weekly Level 10 Meeting are vacation or death."* Don't reschedule, don't cancel — the show goes on without whoever is missing.
- **One sheet of paper.** Agenda, to-dos, and Issues List on it. **No minutes** — if you want to know what was covered, read that week's agenda.
- **On-track means the owner believes they will finish it by quarter end.** Even an on-track Rock someone has a concern about drops to IDS.
- **To-dos are seven-day action items; Rocks are 90-day priorities.** Nothing should sit on the To-Do list more than two weeks, and **90% should drop off every week.**

## IDS — the Issues Solving Track

**Identify.** Most teams never do this and it's why they solve nothing. The stated problem is usually a symptom. The issue owner also states *what kind* of issue it is and what is needed — a decision, an agreement, or feedback on an idea — because the team resolves things far faster when it knows the objective. *"I just want to make sure that everyone is clear and agrees on the new pricing policy"* is a different meeting from "we need to decide."

**Discuss.** Where teams burn their lives. Rules:

- **Say everything you have to say, once.** *"More than once is politicking."*
- Don't be afraid to propose a solution early; voicing one forces others to react and produces a fuller discussion than open-ended talk.
- **Fight for the greater good, not your department.** If the right solution hurts your team, pushing it elsewhere is turf protection, not debate.
- **"Tangent alert!"** Wickman's clients averaged as many as ten tangents while discussing a single issue — *sales are down → the salespeople → Jack → Jack and Sue's conflict → the past-due letter → the letterhead.* Say the words out loud when it happens; if the tangent is a real issue, it goes on the Issues List in priority order.
- When discussion becomes redundant, stop. That is the signal to move on.

**Solve.** A conclusion or action that makes the issue **go away forever** — usually one or more to-dos on the To-Do list, owned by one person, due in seven days.

The economics that justify the sixty minutes: *by taking the time to address a problem, you will save two to ten times that amount of time in the future.*

## The Meeting Pulse

The weekly L10 sits inside a rhythm:

- **Weekly** — Level 10, 90 minutes.
- **Quarterly** — a full day: segue, review the previous quarter, review the V/TO, set next quarter's Rocks, tackle key issues, next steps. Rocks are reviewed **"done" or "not done" only**, which is why they must be specific enough to be binary.
- **Annually** — two days: segue, review the previous year, team health building, SWOT and Issues List, and the V/TO through the one-year plan. **You should be achieving 80% or better of your goals to truly be great.**

The segue at each level does one job: transitioning the team from working *in* the business to working *on* it.

## Application

- Put the L10 in the calendar as a permanent, same-time weekly block and hold it even when half the team is out. Rescheduling once destroys it.
- Build the Scorecard from **activity** numbers, not outcome numbers — the things that predict next month's revenue rather than report last month's. If a number can't be moved this week by someone in the room, it doesn't belong.
- Give every person one number. A Scorecard the leadership team owns alone doesn't produce accountability below it.
- Enforce the drop rule for four weeks before judging the format. The instinct to solve during Scorecard review is exactly the habit the meeting is designed to break.
- Rate the meeting 1–10 at the end and act on scores below 8. The rating is the feedback loop that keeps the format from decaying.
- Rewrite Rocks until every one is binary. "Improve onboarding" cannot be scored; "new client onboarding SOP written and used on the next five clients" can.
