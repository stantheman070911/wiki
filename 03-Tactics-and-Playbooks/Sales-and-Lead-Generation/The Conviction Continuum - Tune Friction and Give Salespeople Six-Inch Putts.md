# The Conviction Continuum - Tune Friction and Give Salespeople Six-Inch Putts

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Tactics and Playbooks Index|Tactics and Playbooks]] → [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Sales-and-Lead-Generation/Sales and Lead Generation Map|Sales and Lead Generation]]

## One-line summary
Every prospect needs some quantity of information before they can buy; deliver that information one-to-many *before* the call so the salesperson's job shrinks to a six-inch putt — and treat friction as a dial to be tuned in both directions, not a defect to be removed.

## Context
Conversion stops scaling when the salesperson has to be a consultant, teacher, therapist, cheerleader, and product expert at once. That role is expensive to hire, slow to train, hard to manage, and produces wide performance variance — which shows up as long calls, multi-conversation sales, price comparison, and a founder who closes far better than anyone on the team. The fix is not a better closer. It is moving the information burden out of the conversation.

## Diagnosis — signs conversion is underleveraged
- Calls run long, and it takes several of them to close.
- Buyers compare mainly on price, even when trust exists.
- The founder's close rate is well above the team's.
- Ramp time for a new rep is long, and performance varies widely across the team.
- Leads arrive at wildly different readiness levels from different sources.

## The model

**The conviction continuum.** Picture a golf hole. A prospect at the tee box needs the full drive — heavy education, proof, risk reversal, clarification — before they can buy. A prospect on the green needs a tap-in. The continuum measures **how much information this buyer needs to decide**, and the whole design goal is to move the ball down the fairway *before* a salesperson is involved.

**Six-inch putts.** It is far easier to hire and train someone to sink repeated six-inch putts than to teach drives, irons, wedges, chips, and long putts. Design the process so reps take the last, easy shot. That gives lower variance between reps, near-zero ramp time, cheaper hires, and a system that survives its best salesperson leaving.

**Information demand is set by perceived risk, not price.** A $100k timeshare may need three days in a room; a $100k flooded-basement restoration is decided in an hour. What raises information demand is irreversibility, delay, expense relative to the buyer's means, and absence of prior trust — not the number on the invoice. Fully remote sales need more information than in-person ones.

**Friction is a dial with a sweet spot.** Too little friction and you fill the calendar with people who can't buy; too much and too few get through. Both ends produce the same number of sales; the middle produces more. Every added step trades volume for readiness.

| Add friction when | Remove friction when |
| --- | --- |
| Leads are too cold | Prospects already understand the product |
| Call quality is poor | There are too few purchase opportunities |
| Prospects are unqualified | Checkout or scheduling is needlessly complicated |
| Reps spend the call educating | Demand is strong but access is hard |
| Buyers aren't ready to decide | Brand already supplies the trust |

**The mechanisms are interchangeable.** VSLs, case studies, applications, deposits, presentations, webinars, testimonials, structured pre-call education — all are just information delivered one-to-many. Which one you use matters far less than whether the prospect arrives convinced.

## Application
1. Plot your current process on the continuum: list every asset a prospect consumes before the call, and honestly assess how much of the conviction work still happens *on* the call.
2. If prospects arrive cold, insert the minimum number of chunks that carry the most information — a VSL, an application, a deposit — and expect cost per lead to rise while cost per *customer* falls.
3. If prospects arrive convinced but there are too few of them, strip steps out. Count the clicks to purchase and cut them.
4. Rewrite the rep's job description around the tap-in: they confirm fit and take the order. Move education, proof, and objection handling into the pre-call assets.
5. Measure the trade at the CAC level, never at cost-per-lead or close rate alone — friction that halves your leads and thirds your CAC is a win.
6. Re-tune whenever traffic source or price changes; the sweet spot moves with lead temperature.

## Notable examples
- **Same leads, ten times the price.** A $500 weight-loss challenge sells lead → call → close. A $5,000 package sells lead → video → application → call → close. Identical leads; two added friction steps; a zero added to the price. The second conversation is also *shorter and easier*, because the prospect is warmer.
- **A $200M solar company** stacks video before the closer arrives and video again mid-appointment — more proof, data, and stories — so the ask lands on a prepared buyer. Less rep time, higher close rate, higher price.
- **CAC $5,000 → $1,500** by *adding* a VSL funnel to cold traffic. Same creative, same targeting, same prospects: "they weren't ready to give it to you because they didn't trust you." Call quality, warmth, and willingness to spend all rose.
- **Tesla** modelled its purchase flow on ordering a pizza, cutting ~100 clicks to ~5. A buyer with enough prior information will buy a $40,000 car online — evidence that the removal end of the dial is just as real as the addition end.
- **A $15M book launch in a weekend** (117,000 buyers, B2B): a 90-minute presentation expanded into a three-day event sold what a bare email offer never could.
