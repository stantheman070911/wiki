# Winning Agreement Without Winning the Argument - Carnegie's Twelve Moves

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Persuasion-and-Influence/Persuasion and Influence Map|Persuasion and Influence]]

## One-line summary
There is only one way to get the best of an argument — avoid it — because a person convinced against their will holds the same opinion still; the twelve moves are how you get the outcome without producing the loser.

## Context
The most expensive persuasion mistake is treating disagreement as a contest to be won on the merits. Even a clean victory leaves the other party humiliated and their pride mobilized against you, so you win the exchange and lose the decision. This entry is the operational middle of Carnegie's book: what to do once you are already opposed.

## The twelve moves

1. **You cannot win an argument.** Lose it and you lose; win it and you have wounded the other person's pride. Treat the impulse to prove someone wrong as a signal to stop talking.
2. **Never say "you're wrong."** Show respect for the other person's opinion. Announcing an error is announcing that you are the superior intellect, which recruits their ego against the fact.
3. **If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically.** Say worse about yourself than the other person was preparing to say. It removes their entire prepared position and there is nothing left to attack.
4. **Begin in a friendly way.** A drop of honey catches more flies than a gallon of gall. Opening in anger guarantees the other side matches you.
5. **Get the other person saying "yes, yes" immediately** — the Socratic method. Open on the points you agree about; a person who has said no has committed their pride to the negative, and reversing it is far harder than never provoking it.
6. **Let the other person do a great deal of the talking.** They know their own business and problems better than you, and their objections often dissolve as they voice them.
7. **Let the other person feel the idea is theirs.** People trust conclusions they reached themselves far more than conclusions handed over.
8. **Try honestly to see things from the other person's point of view.** There is a reason they hold the position; find it before you argue with it.
9. **Be sympathetic with their ideas and desires.** Say, and mean, that you would feel exactly as they do in their position.
10. **Appeal to the nobler motives.** People generally have two reasons for an action — the real one and one that sounds well. Address the one that sounds well and you give them a way to move without loss of face.
11. **Dramatize your ideas.** Statements of fact do not compete with demonstrations; showmanship is not optional in a crowded field.
12. **Throw down a challenge.** When nothing else works, appeal to the desire to excel — the wish to prove one's worth is a stronger motive in capable people than money.

## The moves that carry most of the weight

**The silent close.** A supplier's representative arrived at the final pitch for a $1.6M upholstery contract having lost his voice to laryngitis. He wrote on a pad that he could not speak; the client's president exhibited the samples, argued their merits, and effectively took the salesman's side of the discussion. He won the contract, and concluded he would have lost it had he been able to talk.

**Asking for help instead of pitching.** Eugene Wesson called on one New York stylist weekly for three years — 150 failures — always presenting finished sketches to be judged. He then brought *unfinished* sketches and asked how they should be completed to be usable. The buyer took three days, gave his ideas, and bought every sketch drawn to them, plus scores more. An X-ray manufacturer used the identical move: a letter admitting the new machines were not perfect and asking the department head to look them over and say how they could be improved. He cancelled a dinner to do it, sold himself, and ordered the equipment.

**Yes-momentum.** Getting a stream of small agreements first works because a "no" is a commitment the whole organism then defends. The psychological cost of reversal is what you are avoiding, not the logic.

## Application

- **Before disagreeing, ask what it would cost to be right.** If winning the point costs the decision, concede the point and keep the decision.
- **Convert your proposal into a request for advice.** Bring the 80-percent version and ask how to finish it. Authorship is the strongest form of buy-in and it is cheap to give away.
- **Front-load agreement.** Open any contested conversation with two or three statements the other side must assent to before touching the disputed item.
- **Pre-write your own concession.** Identify the weakest part of your position and state it yourself, before they do.
- **Give the face-saving reason.** When you need someone to move, supply a motive they can repeat publicly without appearing to have caved.

## Limits and cautions

Appealing to nobler motives assumes reasonable good faith; against a party who is straightforwardly extracting, it reads as naivety and should be paired with hard constraints. And "let them feel it is their idea" is not the same as concealing authorship for credit — Colonel House gave Wilson public credit for ideas he had planted, wanting results rather than attribution. If you need the credit, use a different method.
