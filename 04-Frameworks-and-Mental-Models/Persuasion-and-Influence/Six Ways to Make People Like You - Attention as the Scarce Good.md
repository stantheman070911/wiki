# Six Ways to Make People Like You - Attention as the Scarce Good

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Persuasion-and-Influence/Persuasion and Influence Map|Persuasion and Influence]]

## One-line summary
Being liked is not a function of being interesting but of being interested — six cheap, repeatable behaviours that redirect attention away from yourself and toward the other person's name, interests, and self-esteem.

## Context
Liking is a documented compliance lever, but most advice on it is either cosmetic or unusable. Carnegie's contribution is that the six behaviours are concrete enough to rehearse and require no charisma. They matter most in the situations where you have no leverage — cold outreach, a first meeting, a stalled account, a new team — because attention is the one resource you can spend freely before you have earned anything else.

## The six behaviours

| # | Behaviour | The mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Become genuinely interested in other people | You make more friends in two months by becoming interested in others than in two years of trying to make them interested in you |
| 2 | Smile | The cheapest possible signal that you are glad to be there; it costs nothing and cannot be stockpiled |
| 3 | Remember and use a person's name | To its owner, a name is the sweetest and most important sound in any language — and forgetting it reads as not mattering |
| 4 | Be a good listener; encourage others to talk about themselves | Rapt attention is an implied compliment few people can resist |
| 5 | Talk in terms of the other person's interests | The route to someone's attention runs through the thing they already treasure |
| 6 | Make the other person feel important — and do it sincerely | The Golden Rule, applied continuously rather than occasionally |

## Why listening does the heaviest lifting

The most counter-intuitive of the six is that **you are judged a good conversationalist by how well you listen, not by what you say**. Carnegie spent an evening at a dinner party saying almost nothing to a botanist whose field he knew nothing about; the botanist told their host he was a most interesting conversationalist. He had asked questions and paid attention.

The commercial version is sharper. Henry Duvernoy called on a New York hotel manager every week for four years trying to sell bread, and failed. When he instead found out what the man cared about — a hotel-executives' association he was president of — and let him talk about it for half an hour, the hotel's steward phoned days later asking for samples and prices. Bread was never mentioned.

Edward Chalif, wanting a corporation president to fund a Boy Scout trip, opened by asking to see the framed million-dollar cheque he had heard the man kept. He got the funding for five boys instead of one, a letter of credit, and a standing relationship.

**The precondition is homework.** Theodore Roosevelt sat up the night before a visit reading about whatever his guest cared about. The behaviour looks like natural range; it is preparation.

## Application

1. **Before any meeting that matters, spend fifteen minutes finding one thing the person is proud of** — a company they founded, a paper they wrote, a hobby they post about — and open there rather than with your agenda.
2. **Ask, then stay quiet.** Resist the urge to trade an equivalent story of your own. The signal you want to send is attention, and swapping stories converts attention into competition.
3. **Capture names deliberately.** Repeat the name once in the first minute, write it down with a detail attached afterwards. Treat forgetting as a process failure, not a personality trait.
4. **Let others excel you.** La Rochefoucauld's line — if you want enemies, excel your friends — is the practical reason to hold your own accomplishments until asked. A colleague who announced her placements daily had no friends in the agency; when she started asking about theirs instead, that reversed.
5. **Use the small courtesies.** "I'm sorry to trouble you," "would you mind," "thank you" cost nothing and reliably convert a transaction into a relationship.

## Limits and cautions

The sixth principle carries the whole set: it only works when the interest is real. Feigned interest is detectable and converts liking into distrust, which is worse than where you started. If you cannot find something honest to be interested in, the correct move is to say less, not to manufacture enthusiasm.
