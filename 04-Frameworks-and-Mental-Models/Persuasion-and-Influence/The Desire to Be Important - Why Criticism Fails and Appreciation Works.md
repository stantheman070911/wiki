# The Desire to Be Important - Why Criticism Fails and Appreciation Works

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Persuasion-and-Influence/Persuasion and Influence Map|Persuasion and Influence]]

## One-line summary
People are not creatures of logic but creatures of pride, so criticism produces defense rather than change — and the only durable lever is to satisfy the near-universal hunger to feel important, then attach what you want to what they already want.

## Context
Carnegie's three foundational techniques sit underneath every other tactic in his book and most of the persuasion literature that followed. They matter because the default move — telling someone they are wrong and expecting correction — has a near-zero success rate, and its failure is invisible to the person making it. Written in 1936 from case notes on adult-education students, the material is unfashionably plain and empirically stubborn.

## The three foundations

### 1. Don't criticize, condemn, or complain

Criticism puts a person on the defensive and makes them strain to justify themselves. It wounds pride, injures the sense of importance, and arouses resentment — and it rarely changes the behaviour, because the criticized party almost never concludes they were at fault. Carnegie's evidence is that even hardened criminals described themselves to him as misunderstood public benefactors; if they do not blame themselves, the ordinary employee will not either.

The discipline this implies is **understanding before condemning**: ask why the person did what they did. The reconstruction is usually more useful than the verdict, and it produces sympathy and tolerance as a by-product.

### 2. Give honest and sincere appreciation

The deepest, least-satisfied human craving is to be appreciated — William James's word was *craving*, not wish or desire. Nearly every other appetite gets fed; this one usually does not.

Carnegie's evidence is commercial. Charles Schwab was paid over a million dollars a year by Andrew Carnegie not for technical knowledge — many of his subordinates knew more about steel — but for the ability to arouse enthusiasm, which he attributed to being, in his own words, "hearty in my approbation and lavish in my praise." Rockefeller, when a partner lost a million dollars on a bad South American buy, congratulated him on having preserved 60 percent of the investment, and closed the incident.

**The line between appreciation and flattery is sincerity, not technique.** Appreciation comes from an accurate reading of the other person; flattery is telling them what they already think about themselves. Flattery is counterfeit currency and eventually gets its passer into trouble. The way out of flattery is not restraint but attention: when you are not solving a specific problem, you spend most of your time thinking about yourself — redirect that attention to the other person's actual good points and you will not need to invent any.

### 3. Arouse in the other person an eager want

There is only one way to get anyone to do anything: make them want to do it. Force and threat work until your back is turned.

Since every action anyone has ever taken was taken because they wanted something, persuasion reduces to a single reframing question, asked **before you speak**: *how can I make this person want to do this?* Carnegie's fishing analogy is the compressed form — he liked strawberries and cream, but he baited the hook with worms, because the fish's preference is the only one that matters.

## Application

1. **Impose a 24-hour rule on criticism.** When you are about to point out a fault, first write down why the person might reasonably have acted that way. Most criticisms do not survive the exercise; the ones that do become better-aimed.
2. **Run the advantages/disadvantages sheet.** Facing a rent increase of 300 percent, Carnegie went to the hotel manager and, on a divided sheet of paper, listed the advantages *to the manager* of freeing the ballroom and then the disadvantages *to the manager* of losing a paying tenant and the advertising value of the crowds. He never stated what he wanted. The increase came down to 50 percent.
3. **Rewrite any request that opens with your own needs.** A letter beginning "our company desires…" is dead on arrival. Barbara Anderson, relocating to Phoenix, wrote to twelve banks about what her ten years of experience could contribute to their growth, never about what she wanted; eleven invited her to interview.
4. **Make praise specific.** "You're good" reads as flattery; naming exactly what was superior about a particular piece of work reads as observation. Specificity is what carries sincerity.
5. **Practise on strangers with nothing at stake.** Carnegie's test case was telling a bored postal clerk he admired his head of hair. The point of the drill is that it inverts the habitual question from *what can I get* to *what can I honestly admire*.

## Limits and cautions

Carnegie is explicit that this is not manipulation and not a bag of tricks: each party should gain, and the principles work only when they come from sincere interest. Applied insincerely they are detectable and self-defeating — which is also the practical reason to treat them as a habit rather than a technique deployed at need.
