# Confirm Their Self-Opinion - Lowering Defenses Before You Persuade

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Persuasion-and-Influence/Persuasion and Influence Map|Persuasion and Influence]]

## One-line summary
Everyone carries a self-opinion — *I act of my own free will, I am intelligent in my own way, I am basically good* — and persuasion is almost entirely a matter of whether your approach challenges that picture, leaves it neutral, or actively confirms it.

## Context

Greene's Law of Defensiveness. From early childhood we build walls: first a sense of physical space, then personal dignity — *people should not coerce or manipulate us into doing things we don't want to.* Adulthood hardens them, for good reason. We are continually judged, and people continually want something from us: our time, money, ideas, labour.

But the walls do come down, and the circumstances are instructive. A night of drinking with friends; a rally where you're swept along with hundreds of others; falling in love and having it reciprocated. What these have in common isn't the alcohol or the crowd — it is that **you feel inwardly secure, not judged but accepted.** You see a reflection of yourself in others, you can relax, and with no need to turn inward you direct your mind outward — toward a cause, an idea, someone else's happiness.

> **Creating this feeling of validation is the golden key that will unlock people's defenses.**

The failure mode is worth naming precisely, because it is what most people do. Pleading, cajoling, guilt-tripping and improvising in the moment make people *more* defensive. Even when those tactics work, the support is thin with an undercurrent of resentment — you took from them, and they close off to further influence. Worse, a long run of butting against resistance breeds a mounting frustration that leaks into your manner: you seem needy, you try too hard to please, you look **defeated before starting.** That becomes self-fulfilling, and most people never locate the source.

## The self-opinion

Everyone holds a perception of their own character and worth. **Whether it's accurate is irrelevant** — what matters is that they hold it. Three components are near-universal:

| Component | What it sounds like inside | Consequence for you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Autonomy** | *"I am acting of my own free will."* Even when we were manipulated or bowed to peer pressure, we tell ourselves we chose. Forced obedience produces either a story about having chosen, or secret rebellion. | Any hint of coercion kills the attempt. |
| **Intelligence** | *"I am intelligent in my own way."* The plumber values his knowledge of a house's inner workings and thinks his politics come from solid common sense. Nobody is comfortable being gullible; if they can't claim conventional smarts they'll claim cleverness. | Winning the argument loses the person. |
| **Goodness** | *"I am basically good and decent."* We support the right causes, treat people well, are team players. Tough discipline gets relabelled "tough love." | Never cast doubt on the saintly self-image, even inadvertently. |

Layered on top are personalized versions that regulate specific insecurities — *I'm a free spirit, I'm self-reliant and need nobody, I'm a rebel who disdains authority* — each carrying an implied superiority in that one dimension, and each usually rooted in childhood (the rebel with a disappointing father; the self-reliant type with a distant mother).

The critical point: **the self-opinion is primary, and beliefs are downstream of it.** Someone who sees themselves as tough and self-reliant gravitates toward hard-edged philosophies and will reinterpret even a shared religion to match. You may think you hold your ideas on their merits; mostly you hold the ones your self-opinion can live with.

## Three possible outcomes

When you try to convince someone, exactly one of three things happens:

1. **You challenge their self-opinion.** You imply they're stupid, gullible, or less than good — even subtly, even just by knowing better. *Walls go up that will never come down.*
2. **You leave it neutral.** The reasonable, calm, emotionally flat approach. They stay dubious and resistant, but you haven't tightened them, and your arguments have room to work.
3. **You confirm it.** You supply the one thing nobody can supply for themselves — *"we can imagine that we are independent, intelligent, decent, and self-reliant, but only other people can truly confirm this for us."* Insecurity relaxes, attention turns outward, the mind opens to suggestion — and if they help you, **it feels like their own free choice.**

**The low-self-opinion exception.** Some people believe they're unworthy, unlikeable, or too troubled, and feel relieved when bad things happen because it confirms the picture. Insisting they can easily better their lives clashes with that belief and gets discounted. The rule still holds — *work from within their self-opinion*, empathizing with the injustices they've faced, and only then make gentle corrections.

## The five strategies

**1. Become a deep listener.** The standard advice — talk less, listen more — is useless while you still prefer your own internal monologue. The motivation has to be genuine: *"You know your own thoughts only too well. You are rarely surprised… each person you encounter represents an undiscovered country."* Mechanically: don't interrogate; watch nonverbal cues for the topics that make their eyes light up and steer there (childhood, family, the ins and outs of their work, a cause they care about); occasional questions that play off what they said; steady eye contact and nodding. **The strongest signal of listening is mirroring back what they said in your own words, filtered through your experience.** They may do 80% of the talking and still experience it as a lively exchange. They will leave feeling better about themselves — you let them be the star — and reveal their insecurities and unmet desires in the process.

**2. Infect them with the proper mood.** Moods are contagious, which makes them a tool. The strongest posture is **complete indulgence**: you don't judge, you accept them as they are. Greene's model is Marie de Vionnet in Henry James's *The Ambassadors* — she listens deeply and, without responding, gives the impression of total understanding; her *"beautiful conscious mildness"* has Strether under her spell long before she asks for anything. The variant is warmth through laughter and shared pleasure, which was Lyndon Johnson's mode: free-flowing alcohol (his own drinks watered down so he stayed in control), bawdy jokes, an arm around the shoulder. **A light touch on the hand or arm reliably produces positive feeling without the recipient locating the source** — but avoid holding eye contact through it, which reads sexually. And your expectations transmit nonverbally: teachers who expect more from pupils improve their results without saying anything, so imagine the person at their most generous before you meet them.

**3. Confirm each of the three universals.**

- **Autonomy — make them want what you were going to ask for.** The template is Tom Sawyer and the fence: absorbed body language reframed a chore as something interesting, then he framed it as a rare opportunity and a test of skill, and once other boys saw it as a group activity nobody wanted to be left out. He ended up lying in the shade collecting apples. *"How can you get others to perceive the favor you want to ask for as something they already desire?"* The competitive variant: Billy Wilder wanted Marlene Dietrich for a role she'd refused on principle, so he asked her to evaluate screen tests by two actresses he knew were terrible for it; appalled, she volunteered. **On gifts: smaller is better.** Large ones make it obvious you're buying loyalty and offend the sense of independence; small rewards spread over time let people tell themselves they earned it, and bind far more tightly.
- **Intelligence — stop winning arguments.** Contradiction implies you thought it through better, which makes people cling harder. Neutrality is adequate; agreement is better. Disraeli's sharper version: *"If you wish to win a man's heart, allow him to confute you"* — disagree at first, even with some heat, then come round to their view, confirming both their intelligence and their powers of influence. Asking for advice does the same work. Beaumarchais, whose *Marriage of Figaro* the king wanted censored, convened a tribunal of academics, courtiers and ministers and submitted unreservedly to every cut they proposed — everyone wanted to add something of their own, and he kept his play.
- **Goodness — attach the ask to a cause.** They aren't buying clothes, they're keeping jobs local. Keep it subtle, let others carry the message, make it look prosocial and popular so people want to join rather than being pleaded with. Watch your labels: *team member*, not *employee*. Putting yourself one-down — a harmless faux pas, then asking forgiveness — hands them moral superiority, a position people enjoy occupying. And the counter-intuitive one: **when you need a favour, don't remind them what you did for them.** Gratitude is rare because it reminds us of dependence. Remind them of the good things *they* did for you — it confirms *"yes, I am generous,"* and they'll want to keep living up to it.

**4. Allay their insecurities.** Draw them out in conversation until you can identify what they're uncertain about — looks, creative power, status, uniqueness, popularity. First, **never trigger it**; people have fine antennae for any word or gesture that touches it. Then flatter precisely there. The rule of thumb: *flattery works on what someone is uncertain about, not on what they know is false.* Praise a hopeless basketball player's game and it rings false; praise an uncertain one's and it works. Chesterfield's example: skilled flatterers said little to Richelieu about statecraft — where he was secure — and praised him as a poet, where he was vain and uncertain. Refinements: **praise effort, not talent** (talent implies luck; people want to feel they earned it); with superiors, agree and validate rather than flatter outright; never follow praise with a request — let time pass, and start slightly cool so you have room to warm; route compliments through third parties where possible; mix in a small criticism to give praise a realistic hue (*"I loved your screenplay, although act two might need a little work"*). When someone asks your opinion of their work, understand what's actually being asked: **they want support and confirmation, delivered as realistically as possible.** What gives insincerity away is never the words but the nonverbals — stiff body, fake smile, wandering eyes.

**5. Use their resistance — mental judo.** The most resistant people usually have *low* self-opinion expressed as rebellion; they must assert their will and will find dozens of reasons your advice won't work for them. Don't counter the force; channel it.
- **Use their emotions.** A suspended teenager, told that the principal expected him to fail at home and that doing well would embarrass him, threw himself into homework out of spite — exactly the therapist's goal.
- **Use their language.** Milton Erickson, faced with a husband set on a "friendly divorce," agreed enthusiastically and prescribed the details: one last friendly night at a hotel, one last friendly glass of champagne, one last friendly kiss. **Using someone's own words back at them has a hypnotic effect** — how can they refuse what they themselves said?
- **Use their rigidity.** The Zen master Hakuin, asked to get a miserly pawnbroker to practise, offered to *buy* each prayer and chant as a business transaction. By the seventh day the man had lost count because he'd become absorbed in the chanting, and soon refused payment.

## On the moral objection

*"Why can't we all just be honest and ask for what we want?"* Greene's answer is two-part and worth having ready. First, **be on guard with people who say this**: we cannot tolerate powerlessness, and someone who needs to believe in their own angelic qualities and can't square that with wanting influence tends to become passive-aggressive — pouting and guilt-tripping to get the same result. Second, **influence is unavoidable**: everything you say or do is read for intent, including silence. *"Since the game is unavoidable, better to be skillful at it than in denial or merely improvising in the moment."*

The genuinely useful defence is that competence at this is prosocial: it lets you move people who hold dangerous ideas, and becoming good at it *requires* immersing yourself in another person's perspective. Note also the honest constraint Greene keeps repeating — **all of it works best when the feelings aren't wholly faked.** Empathy is the engine, not the veneer.

## Application

- **Before any ask, write down the target's self-opinion in their own words.** Which of the three universals matters most to them, and what personalized version sits on top? That sentence is your constraint.
- **Audit your usual approach for challenges.** Do you correct, contradict, or out-argue? Each one is a wall going up. Choose neutrality at worst.
- **Convert the ask into something they already want** before you make it — a rare opportunity, a test of skill, something others are joining. If you're pleading, you've already lost.
- **Ask for advice instead of agreement.** It confirms intelligence, produces investment, and often improves your plan.
- **When you need a favour, recall their past generosity, not yours.**
- **Locate the uncertain quality, not the obvious one**, and praise there — for effort, moderately, without an adjacent request, and ideally through someone else.
- **With a hardened refusal, stop pushing.** Adopt their framing and their words, then bend the direction. Judo, not force.
