# Leverage Is the Scaling Variable - Demand, Conversion, Delivery

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Focus-Execution-and-Systems/Focus Execution and Systems Map|Focus, Execution, and Systems]]

## One-line summary
Past roughly $1M you cannot add hours, only leverage — so scaling becomes the repeated work of raising output per unit of input in exactly three places (demand, conversion, delivery), each of which becomes the bottleneck the moment you fix the one before it.

## Context
Hormozi's account of his own income steps — four figures a month at 22 to nine figures a month at 34 — attributes every jump to a change in *leverage*, never to a proportional increase in effort. The framework exists because the $1–3M plateau is not an effort problem: the founder is already at maximum hours, and the margin is too thin to fund a mistake. What has to change is the ratio between what goes in and what comes out.

## The model

**Leverage = output ÷ input.** Low leverage is a lot in for a little out; high leverage is a little in for a lot out. *Scaling is what happens when leverage rises* — it is a consequence, not an aim. "Scaling is my goal" is a category error; "where am I underleveraged?" is the operative question.

**The leverage ladder.** Each income tier corresponds to a different leverage source, and the sources are multiplicative when stacked:

| Stage | Leverage source |
| --- | --- |
| Employee | Own labor only |
| Self-employed | Own labor, better priced |
| Service business owner | Other people's labor |
| Media business owner | Content — one input, many viewers |
| Capital allocator | Money making money |
| Large-scale operator | People + media + technology + capital combined |

**The three-part throughput chain.** Time goes in on the left, money comes out on the right, and only three stages sit between them:

1. **Leveraged demand** — more of the right people find out about your stuff, consistently, without more of your time.
2. **Leveraged conversion** — more of them buy, without longer calls or scarcer sales talent.
3. **Leveraged delivery** — more of them get served, at the same or lower cost, without you.

**The cycle, not the fix.** Raise demand and conversion becomes the constraint; fix conversion and delivery becomes the constraint; fix delivery and you return to demand at a higher volume. Every solution manufactures the next problem — which is why there is no terminal operating model and no single tactic worth hunting for.

## Why the $1–3M band is a risk problem, not an effort problem
- At $2M on a 20% margin you hold ~$400k of profit. Every real move — one or two senior hires, narrowing to a single avatar, raising price, replacing the offer — bets most of it.
- So the bet that gets you *out* of the band is larger than the one that got you *in*. Getting in rewarded working as hard as possible; getting out requires changing how you work and how you price risk. Counterintuitive, and the reason so many businesses cluster here.
- Speed from the outside is not more activity. Founders can't move faster than founders; fast-scaling companies simply make fewer strategic mistakes. Experience buys speed by removing trial and error — which is also why a second business blows past the early levels and then stalls at exactly the same one the first did.

## Application
1. Name your binding stage before choosing a tactic: not enough people find out (demand), enough find out but too few buy (conversion), or enough buy and you can't serve them (delivery).
2. For that stage, ask the leverage question rather than the effort question — "how do I get more out for the same in?" — and reject any answer that consumes more founder hours.
3. Stack leverage sources rather than swapping them; people, media, technology, and capital multiply.
4. Below ~$1M, don't reach for this framework at all: one product, one avatar, one channel, done consistently, is the whole job. Organizational design becomes the lever only after that.
5. Expect to re-run the diagnosis after every fix, and budget the risk explicitly — the move out of the swamp costs more margin of error than you are comfortable spending.
