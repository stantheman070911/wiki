# The V/TO - Eight Questions That Turn a Vision Into a Plan

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Focus-Execution-and-Systems/Focus Execution and Systems Map|Focus, Execution, and Systems]]

## One-line summary
A company's whole vision fits on two pages as answers to eight questions, ordered from timeless to this-quarter — and the value is not the document but the requirement that the leadership team agrees on every answer.

## Context
Most strategic plans fail one of two ways: they are a hundred pages nobody reads, or they are a slogan nobody can act on. The **Vision/Traction Organizer** is EOS's answer — deliberately two pages, with the top page (Vision) fixed and the bottom page (Traction) rewritten every 90 days. It is the tool for the Vision component in [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Focus-Execution-and-Systems/The Six Key Components - EOS's Model of a Whole Business|the Six Key Components]].

The completion criterion is not "we wrote it down." It is that **everyone on the leadership team can answer all eight questions and absolutely agree.** Wickman recommends a one- or two-day off-site to do it.

## The eight questions

1. What are your **core values**?
2. What is your **core focus**?
3. What is your **10-year target**?
4. What is your **marketing strategy**?
5. What is your **three-year picture**?
6. What is your **one-year plan**?
7. What are your **quarterly Rocks**?
8. What are your **issues**?

The ordering is the design. Questions 1–4 are essentially timeless; 5–6 are the medium horizon; 7 is the next 90 days; 8 is the honest list of what stands in the way. **The Vision page (1–4) and the Traction page (5–8) are separated so the second can churn without disturbing the first.**

## Core values — three to seven, discovered not invented

Core values are *a small set of vital and timeless guiding principles.* Three to seven; less is more. They are not aspirational marketing — **they already exist in your organisation and your task is to rediscover them.**

The discovery process, run with the leadership team off-site for at least two hours:

1. Each person lists **three people who, if you could clone them, would lead you to market domination** — preferably from inside the company.
2. Post all the names.
3. Work out what characteristics those people share.
4. Narrow to three to seven.

The test of whether you actually have them: **you hire, fire, review, reward, and recognise on them.** Absent that, they are wall décor. Wickman's supporting evidence is *Built to Last* — Collins and Porras found that companies enduring across decades defined core values early and built the culture around them.

The most common objection is Rob Dube's at Image One: *we have a ton of urgent issues, let's do the core-values thing later.* The reply is that defined values are what let you resolve people-issues at all, which is why they come first.

## Core focus — the diamond mine you're standing on

Two questions, answered to just a few words each:

- **Why does your organisation exist?** Its purpose, cause, or passion. The test: when it's clear, *you shouldn't be able to tell what business you're in* — it should travel to any industry.
- **What is your niche?** The thing you're genuinely best at.

Wickman's warning is about **"shiny stuff."** His parable is Conwell's *Acres of Diamonds*: Ali sells his farm to hunt diamonds across Palestine and Europe, dies penniless, and the farm's new owner finds the brook running through it full of them. *Most people are sitting on their own diamond mines.*

The worked example: Broder & Sachse, a real-estate management firm, put a million dollars and eighteen months into an engine powder-coating venture that looked like a million-dollar idea on paper, and closed it after three months and $300,000 of losses. They were lucky enough to recover most of the capital; the year and a half of lost focus was unrecoverable. Internally, a shiny distraction is now called *a CCT*, after the failed company's initials.

The inverse: Image One, once its core focus was clear, **eliminated its entire computer-networking division** to concentrate on simplifying clients' printing environments — and grew about **30% a year for four years** into a sale at high multiples. *"Decide what business you are in, and be in that business."*

## 10-year target, marketing strategy, and the descent to 90 days

**10-year target** — one big, clear, long-term number or state that everyone in the company can name.

**Marketing strategy** — four elements, kept on the V/TO because sales and marketing focus is a vision decision, not a campaign decision:

1. **Target market / "The List"** — your ideal customers by demographic, geographic, and psychographic characteristics, specific enough to be a list you could actually build.
2. **Three Uniques** — the individual items need not be unique; *it's the combination of all three that makes you different.* No one else should do all three the way you do. To find them: list everything you believe makes you different, ask your ideal customers what they think is unique about you (*it's a ten-minute phone call*), then eliminate down to three.
3. **Proven Process** — your delivery method, drawn as a diagram the prospect can see.
4. **Guarantee** — the risk you take off the buyer's table.

If you believe in your Three Uniques, **never apologise for them.** Southwest's Herb Kelleher, on a serial complainer's letter about the lack of assigned seating, first class, and meals, wrote back in sixty seconds: *"Dear Mrs. Crabapple, we will miss you. Love, Herb."*

**Three-year picture, one-year plan, quarterly Rocks** are the same vision at shortening horizons — the discipline of *long-term prediction* being *not really about foretelling what will happen; it's making a decision about what you will do tomorrow based on what you know today.* The leader's job is to *climb the tree* often enough that the road being cut through the jungle isn't a zigzag.

**Issues** is the honest list of everything in the way. Putting it on the vision document is deliberate: a vision with no stated obstacles is a wish.

## Shared by all

A vision one person holds is not a vision. Wickman's implementation notes:

- Answer the eight questions **as a leadership team**, not as a founder presenting.
- Expect several sessions. Core values and core focus in particular resist being rushed — *like core values, your core focus already exists; it's just a matter of chiselling away the non-core items.*
- Then **roll it down**: company Rocks, then leadership-team Rocks, then everyone else's — which is how alignment is actually produced.
- Re-read the whole V/TO at the quarterly meeting. It is a living two pages, not an artefact.

## Application

- Book the off-site before you read further. The V/TO is not fillable at a desk, and the leadership team's disagreement is the deliverable.
- Test your existing values with the clone exercise. If the names your team lists don't obviously exhibit the values on your website, the website is wrong.
- Apply the CCT test to every opportunity on your desk right now: does it sit inside the core focus? If not, the correct answer is no, however good the spreadsheet looks.
- Pick your Three Uniques by elimination and then commit hard enough to write a Kelleher letter. Uniques that you apologise for are positioning you don't actually hold.
- Once the V/TO exists, ask five people at random what the company's core focus and 10-year target are. The spread in their answers is your real Vision score.
