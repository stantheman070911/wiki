# Synthetic vs. Creative Imagination - Sitting for Ideas

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Focus-Execution-and-Systems/Focus Execution and Systems Map|Focus Execution and Systems]]

## One-line summary
Hill splits imagination into recombination of what you already know and the arrival of what you don't — and gives a repeatable procedure for the second: load the known factors deliberately, then clear the mind and wait, rather than reasoning harder.

## Context
Hill's Imagination chapter, plus the practice he describes in the Sex Transmutation and Brain chapters. His metaphysical explanation — that the mind picks up thought vibrations from the ether and from "Infinite Intelligence" — is not carried over here. What is worth keeping is the two-faculty distinction and the working procedure attached to it, which is a recognisable description of incubation and is testable regardless of what one believes is doing the work.

## The two faculties

| | **Synthetic imagination** | **Creative imagination** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it does | Rearranges existing concepts, ideas, and plans into new combinations | Produces ideas that were not in the input — "hunches," inspirations |
| Raw material | Experience, education, observation | Nothing you can point to; it arrives |
| When it's used | Most of the time; the main workhorse for converting a purpose into a plan | When synthetic recombination has run out |
| How it improves | With use, like a muscle | With use, and with the degree to which you rely on it and make demands of it |

Hill's ordering matters: **use synthetic first and exhaustively.** The scientific inventor "begins by organizing and combining the known ideas or principles accumulated through experience," and only when that stock proves insufficient turns to the other faculty. Edison ran more than ten thousand combinations synthetically before the answer to the incandescent light arrived.

## The procedure: sitting for ideas

Hill's two-step description of what a working inventor does:

1. **Stimulate the mind above its ordinary plane**, using one of the mind stimuli (see below) or any other that works for you.
2. **Concentrate on the known factors** — the finished part of the problem — and build a clear picture of the unknown part. Hold that picture until it has been taken over by the subconscious. **Then relax, clear the mind of all thought, and wait** for the answer to arrive.

His example is Dr. Elmer Gates, who kept a soundproof, lightproof room in his laboratory containing only a table and a pad. He would sit, shut off the lights, concentrate on the known factors, and stay there until ideas began to flash — on one occasion writing for nearly three hours. Gates completed over two hundred patents this way, many of them started but abandoned by others, and earned a living being paid by the hour by large corporations to "sit for ideas."

Two related habits Hill records: a great orator who closed his eyes before the climaxes of his speeches, and a financier who closed his eyes for two or three minutes before every decision.

## The ten mind stimuli

Hill's list of what raises the mind above its ordinary working plane. Eight he calls natural and constructive, two destructive:

Desire for sex expression · love · a burning desire for fame, power, or money · music · friendship · a Master Mind alliance · shared suffering · auto-suggestion · fear · **narcotics and alcohol**.

He is unambiguous that the last two are the destructive pair — noting the writers who worked under the influence and adding that many such men destroyed themselves. Fear also appears on the list, which is worth noting: stimulation is not the same as benefit. Hill's substantive point is the altitude metaphor — a mind lifted above the day-to-day plane of food, clothing, and shelter "occupies the same position as one who has ascended in an airplane," seeing past the horizon that limits vision on the ground.

The lengthy sex-transmutation theory attached to this list (that redirected sexual energy is the source of genius, that men rarely succeed before forty because they dissipate it) is Hill's own and rests on no evidence; the usable residue is that strong emotion, not cold reason, is what sets the creative faculty in motion.

## Ideas are the scarce input

The chapter's practical claim: specialized knowledge is cheap and plentiful; the imagination to combine it into an idea is not. His examples are commercial rather than artistic — a drug clerk who paid a country doctor $500 for a kettle and a formula, and added the one ingredient the doctor lacked (Coca-Cola); a publisher who lifted a book's sales by a million copies by changing nothing but the title. Hill's line: **there is no standard price on ideas — the creator of ideas makes their own price.**

## Application

1. Exhaust the synthetic faculty before expecting the other one. Enumerate combinations of what you already know, on paper, until the list is genuinely dry.
2. Then load the problem deliberately: write down what is known and what is missing, hold it clearly, and stop working on it. The stopping is the step people skip.
3. Book the empty slot. Gates' soundproof room is a walk, a shower, or a phoneless half hour — the requirement is no input, not any particular setting.
4. Capture immediately and act fast. Hill insists that when a plan flashes into mind you put it into action at once; delay is what kills it (see [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Mindset-and-Identity/Persistence as a Trained Habit - Eight Causes, Sixteen Symptoms, Four Steps|the stillborn-idea point]]).
5. Choose your stimulus deliberately and honestly. Music, a strong motive, and a good group are on Hill's list for a reason; so are the two he warns about.
