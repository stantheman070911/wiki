# How to Think About Money - Opportunity Cost Is Compounding's Evil Twin

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Mindset-and-Identity/Mindset and Identity Map|Mindset and Identity]]

## One-line summary
There are four escalating ways to look at a banknote — what it buys, what it earns, what it earns after inflation, and what its earnings can themselves earn — and which one you habitually use determines whether you end up poor, middle class, comfortable, or rich, largely independent of income.

## Context
Collins' exercise: prop up a $100 note and consider what it means to you.

1. **What could I buy with it?** A dinner for two. A pair of sneakers. A tank of fuel.
2. **I could invest it.** At historical market returns of 8–12%, I could spend the earnings each year and still always have my $100 working.
3. **I'll invest it but only spend ~4%,** reinvesting the rest so the principal and its income keep pace with inflation.
4. **I'll invest it, reinvest what it earns, reinvest what *that* earns,** and think about spending only after compounding has done its work.

*"One view will keep you poor, one will get you into the middle class, one will take that a step further and the last will make you rich."*

This is a mindset entry rather than a tactics one because the constraint being described is not arithmetic. It is which question you reflexively ask.

## Level I: stop thinking about what money buys

The failure mode is understanding money *only* in terms of purchasing. Mike Tyson earned some $300,000,000 and went bankrupt on a lifestyle reputed to cost $400,000 a month. Collins' point is not that Tyson was foolish — it is that the world is full of athletes, performers, lawyers, doctors and executives on whom money landed and immediately flowed off into other people's pockets. *"They never learned how to think about money."*

The corrective is one sentence: **stop thinking about what your money can buy; start thinking about what your money can earn — and then what the money it earns can earn.**

The immediate consequence is that spending destroys two things, not one: the money, and everything that money would have produced. And so on, recursively, forever.

## Level II: opportunity cost, and how to actually compute it

Everyone understands that buying a $20,000 car in cash means you no longer have $20,000. Fewer notice that financing or leasing it is a decision that reads: *"I don't want to pay twenty thousand dollars for this car. I want to pay much, much more."*

But even paying cash, the car costs far more than its price. To quantify it, pick a proxy for what the money would otherwise earn — Collins uses the low end of the market's historical range, **8%**:

- $20,000 × 8% = **$1,600 per year** forgone.
- Over the ten years you own the car: **$16,000**. The $20,000 car now costs $36,000.
- And that still understates it, because it ignores what each of those $1,600 chunks would have earned, and what *those* earnings would have earned.

> Think of opportunity cost as [compounding's] evil twin.

The elegant definition of financial independence that falls out of this: **you are financially independent when the power of compounding on your assets exceeds the opportunity cost of what you spend.** Below that line, every purchase moves the finish line away from you. Above it, you only need to reinvest enough to outpace inflation and keep spending under what the pot can replenish.

## Level III: own businesses, not price quotes

The third shift is how you hold what you own. Collins uses Buffett's much-abused "Rule #1: never lose money," which people misread as a claim to dodge drawdowns. Buffett *"lost"* about $25 billion in the 2008–9 crash, cutting his fortune from $62bn to $37bn. He didn't time it, and on record he calls trying a fool's errand: *"The Dow started the last century at 66 and ended at 11,400. How could you lose money during a period like that? A lot of people did because they tried to dance in and out."*

What he did instead was know, on an emotional level, that he still owned exactly the same fraction of the same companies.

Collins' translation for an ordinary investor: if $10,000 buys you 186.32 shares of a total-market index fund, then a week later — at a higher price or a lower one — **you still own 186.32 shares**, which is a piece of roughly 3,700 businesses. The typical investor treats holdings as little bits of data that move up and down, which makes any bad day genuinely frightening. Understanding what you actually own is what makes the same day survivable.

## The needs-and-wants layer

The reason level I is so sticky is that it is under constant, well-funded attack. *"There are pervasive and powerful marketing forces at work seeking to obscure the idea that such a choice exists."* The lines between need and want are deliberately and continually blurred, and the science behind the persuasion is genuinely impressive.

Collins' illustration is a friend who bought a top-end video camera and declared, in a burst of enthusiasm, *"you just can't raise a child properly without one of these!"* Billions of children have been raised without ever being filmed. Everyone knows someone who will tell you what they can't live without; if you want to be wealthy — by controlling needs as much as by growing assets — those beliefs are the thing to re-examine.

## Application

1. **Before any significant purchase, run the opportunity-cost line:** price × 8% × years you'll own it, added to the price. Do it once on something you already bought and the habit sticks.
2. **Price financed purchases as what they are** — a stated preference to pay more than the sticker.
3. **When markets fall, count units, not currency.** You still own the same shares of the same businesses. Write the share count down somewhere you'll see it.
4. **Interrogate any "can't live without" claim,** including your own, against how people lived before the thing existed.
5. **Locate yourself against the independence line:** is compounding on your assets currently larger or smaller than the opportunity cost of your annual spending? That ratio, not a number in a bank account, is the status report.
6. **Escalate one level.** If you habitually think at level I, the next move isn't level IV — it is asking, on the next $100 that arrives, what it could earn.
