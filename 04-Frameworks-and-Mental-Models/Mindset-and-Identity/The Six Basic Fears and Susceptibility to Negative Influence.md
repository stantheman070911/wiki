# The Six Basic Fears and Susceptibility to Negative Influence

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Mindset-and-Identity/Mindset and Identity Map|Mindset and Identity]]

## One-line summary
Every fear that matters reduces to six, each of which announces itself through specific *behaviours* rather than felt dread — so the diagnostic move is to look for the symptom you're exhibiting, not the fear you're aware of.

## Context
Hill's closing chapter, written for readers who would answer "I fear nothing" and be wrong. His argument is that fear is usually invisible to the person carrying it — "so subtle and deeply seated that one may go through life burdened with it, never recognizing its presence" — and that only a behavioural inventory will surface it. The chapter is the most dated part of the book (the 1937 physiology and the claims about disease are wrong), but the symptom lists remain the sharpest self-audit tool in it.

## The six fears, and how each shows up

Hill orders them by frequency. The first three, he says, sit under most of what people call worry.

| Fear | Behavioural symptoms to look for |
| --- | --- |
| **Poverty** | Indifference and tolerance of one's circumstances; indecision and letting others think for you; doubt expressed as alibis, or as envy and criticism of the successful; worry expressed as fault-finding and overspending; **over-caution** — knowing every road to disaster and none to success, waiting for the right time until waiting is the habit; procrastination, planning what to do *if* you fail instead of burning the bridges |
| **Criticism** | Self-consciousness; lack of poise; sidestepping issues and agreeing without examining; inferiority complex covered by big words, imitation, and boasting; extravagance to keep up appearances; lack of initiative — evasive answers, no confidence in your own ideas; lack of ambition, criticising people behind their backs and flattering them to their faces |
| **Ill health** | Hypochondria and negative self-suggestion; chasing fads and remedies; talking about illness; using imaginary illness to bid for sympathy or excuse laziness |
| **Loss of love** | Jealousy and suspicion without grounds; habitual fault-finding with those closest to you; spending or gambling beyond your means to buy affection |
| **Old age** | Slowing down and developing an inferiority complex around forty; apologising for your age; killing off initiative and imagination on the belief you're too old — when, Hill argues, forty to sixty are the most useful years |
| **Death** | Thinking about dying instead of making the most of living, generally from lack of purpose or a suitable occupation. Hill's remedy: a burning desire for achievement backed by useful service; "a busy person seldom has time to think about dying" |

**Fear of criticism gets special weight.** Hill calls it the enemy at the bottom of the destruction of most ideas that never reach the planning stage: it robs initiative, destroys imagination, and limits individuality. He notes it usually operates unrecognised, and that a person's closest relatives are often the worst offenders — parents who build inferiority complexes in children through unnecessary criticism are committing, in his words, a crime of the worst nature.

## Worry is indecision, not fear

Hill's most portable claim: **worry is a form of sustained fear caused by indecision**, and therefore a state of mind that can be controlled. "We do not worry over conditions, once we have reached a decision to follow a definite line of action." Each of the six fears converts to worry through indecision, and each dissolves through a decision:

- Death → decide to accept it as inescapable.
- Poverty → decide to get along with whatever you can accumulate, without worry.
- Criticism → decide not to worry about what others think, do, or say.
- Old age → decide to accept it as a blessing carrying wisdom and self-control.
- Ill health → decide to forget symptoms.
- Loss of love → decide you can get along without it if necessary.

## The seventh evil: susceptibility to negative influence

Hill separates this from the six fears and calls it more deeply seated and more often fatal than all of them: the habit of leaving your mind open to other people's negative influence. It is dangerous precisely because most people don't recognise they're subject to it, and because it enters through well-meant words from relatives as readily as from enemies. His protections:

- Recognise that you are, by nature, lazy, indifferent, and susceptible to any suggestion that harmonises with your weaknesses.
- Keep your mind closed against anyone who depresses or discourages you, and deliberately seek the company of people who influence you to think and act for yourself.
- Do not expect trouble — "it has a tendency not to disappoint."
- Keep the mind occupied with a definite purpose backed by a definite plan. Hill's most practical line: **the most practical of all methods for controlling the mind is keeping it busy.**

## The alibi habit

Hill closes with fifty-seven "IF" alibis — *if I had money, if I had a good education, if I were younger, if I could get a break* — and one observation that makes the list worth keeping: people defend their alibis because they created them, and it is human nature to defend your own brain-child. The last alibi on his list is the only honest one: *if I had the courage to see myself as I really am, I would find out what is wrong with me and correct it.*

## Application

- Read the symptom column, not the fear column. Pick the two behaviours you recognise in yourself this month and trace them back to which fear they belong to.
- Convert every standing worry into a decision. If you can't name the decision the worry is standing in for, the worry is doing your thinking for you.
- Audit your inputs for a week: who among your acquaintances encourages you most, cautions you most, discourages you most. Hill's questionnaire asks whether someone you consider a friend is in reality your worst enemy because of their influence on your mind — an uncomfortable question worth answering once.
- Write down your favourite "if only" sentence. It is your alibi, you made it, and you will defend it unless you name it.
