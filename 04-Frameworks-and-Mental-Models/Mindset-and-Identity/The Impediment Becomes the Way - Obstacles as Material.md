# The Impediment Becomes the Way - Obstacles as Material

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Mindset-and-Identity/Mindset and Identity Map|Mindset and Identity]]

## One-line summary
An obstacle can block a particular action but never the underlying intention, because the mind converts whatever stands in its way into the object it works on — what was the impediment becomes the readiest way forward.

## Context
The reason obstacles stop most people is not the obstacle but the assumption that there is exactly one route and it has been closed. Marcus's formulation is the sharpest statement of the alternative, and it is not motivational: it rests on a specific mechanism — the distinction between an *action* (blockable, external) and an *intention* (unblockable, internal), plus the mind's ability to redirect toward whatever is currently possible.

## The mechanism

The passage, in Casaubon's rendering:

> By some of these it may be, that some operation or other of mine, may be hindered; however, of my mind and resolution itself, there can be no let or impediment… For by these the mind doth turn and convert any impediment whatsoever, to be her aim and purpose. **So that what before was the impediment, is now the principal object of her working; and that which before was in her way, is now her readiest way.**

Two clauses do the work:

1. **The reservation.** Marcus notes that the mind inclines toward its objects "with due exception, and reservation" — the Stoic *reserve clause*, roughly *I will do X, fate permitting*. Because the commitment was never unconditional about the route, a blocked route is not a broken commitment.
2. **The conversion.** The mind performs a "ready conversion of objects; from that which may not be, to that which may be." It moves attention from what is now impossible to what is now available, and treats *that* as the work.

Elsewhere he makes the practical form explicit: where you meet a hindrance, you may use it for **the exercise of another virtue**. The blocked path calls out a different capability than the open one would have.

## What it is not

- **Not that obstacles are good.** Marcus does not claim the plague, the war, or the betrayal were fortunate. He claims they are material — the thing your character now works on.
- **Not a guarantee of the original outcome.** The intention survives; the specific plan may not. Confusing the two produces stubbornness, not resilience.
- **Not a solo doctrine.** He is direct about limits: if you cannot reach the top of the battlement alone but can with help, do not therefore give it over — get help and go with no less alacrity.

## Application

1. **Restate the blocked plan as an intention.** "Launch in Q3 via channel A" is an action; "reach this buyer profitably" is the intention. Write both down before responding to the blockage, because the intention usually has three routes and you were attached to one.
2. **Ask which capability the obstacle is calling for.** A stalled negotiation calls for patience; a missing skill calls for learning; an unreasonable partner calls for justice or for exit. Name it explicitly; this converts frustration into a defined task.
3. **Attach the reserve clause when you commit, not after you fail.** Stated in advance, "we will do this, conditions permitting" costs nothing and removes the identity crisis when conditions do not permit.
4. **Treat the obstacle as the new primary object for one working session.** Not as something to route around, but as the thing you study — most obstacles contain the information that would have improved the original plan.
5. **Recover, do not restart.** Marcus's own note is that you will fall short of this repeatedly; the instruction is to return to it, and to return "as they that have sore eyes to their sponge" — as relief, not as penance.
