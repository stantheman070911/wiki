# Persistence as a Trained Habit - Eight Causes, Sixteen Symptoms, Four Steps

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Mindset-and-Identity/Mindset and Identity Map|Mindset and Identity]]

## One-line summary
Persistence is not a character trait you either have or lack — it is a state of mind with eight identifiable causes and sixteen observable symptoms of absence, which means it can be diagnosed and built rather than merely wished for.

## Context
Hill claims that of the men he analysed at length — Ford and Edison in particular, year by year over many years — he found no single quality other than persistence that remotely explained their results. His diagnostic move is what makes the chapter useful: instead of exhorting people to persist, he treats "I lack persistence" as a symptom with a locatable cause, and gives lists for both.

## The eight causes of persistence

Where persistence exists, Hill says these are underneath it. Work backward from the list when you find yourself quitting.

1. **Definiteness of purpose** — knowing what you want; the first and most important. A strong motive forces you over difficulties.
2. **Desire** — persistence is comparatively easy in pursuit of something intensely wanted. Weak desires bring weak results, "just as a small amount of fire makes a small amount of heat."
3. **Self-reliance** — belief in your ability to carry out the plan.
4. **Definiteness of plans** — organized plans encourage persistence *even when they are weak or impractical*. Having a plan matters separately from having a good one.
5. **Accurate knowledge** — knowing your plan is sound, from experience or observation. Guessing instead of knowing destroys persistence.
6. **Cooperation** — sympathy and harmonious cooperation with others.
7. **Will-power** — the habit of concentrating thought on building plans for a definite purpose.
8. **Habit** — persistence is the direct result of habit. Fear in particular is cured by forced repetition of acts of courage.

## The sixteen symptoms of its absence

Hill's list, compressed. These are behaviours, not feelings — that is what makes them checkable.

Failure to define exactly what you want · procrastination backed by an array of alibis · lack of interest in acquiring specialized knowledge · indecision and passing the buck · relying on alibis instead of building plans · self-satisfaction (for which he says there is little remedy) · indifference, expressed as readiness to compromise rather than meet opposition · blaming others and treating circumstances as unavoidable · weak desire from neglecting the choice of motive · eagerness to quit at the first sign of defeat · no organized plans written down where they can be analysed · neglecting to move on ideas or seize opportunity · wishing instead of willing · compromising with poverty rather than aiming at riches · searching for shortcuts, trying to get without giving · **fear of criticism** — which Hill puts at the head of the list because it usually operates unrecognised in the subconscious.

On that last one, Hill's own confession is the clearest example: when Carnegie proposed he spend twenty years organizing a philosophy of achievement, his first reaction was a rapid-fire cascade of alibis, every one traceable to fear of what people would say. His conclusion: **most ideas are stillborn and need the breath of life injected through definite plans of immediate action — the time to nurse an idea is the moment of its birth.**

## The four steps to build it

1. A definite purpose backed by burning desire for its fulfilment.
2. A definite plan, expressed in continuous action.
3. A mind closed tightly against negative and discouraging influences — including from relatives, friends, and acquaintances.
4. A friendly alliance with one or more people who will encourage you to follow through with both plan and purpose.

## Three feet from gold

Hill's governing anecdote. R. U. Darby's uncle struck gold in Colorado, raised money from relatives for machinery, mined a few cars of rich ore, then hit a fault and lost the vein. They drilled, failed, quit, and sold the machinery for scrap. The scrap buyer hired a mining engineer, who calculated the vein resumed three feet from where they had stopped. It did. Darby's later career selling life insurance — over a million dollars a year, a top-fifty performer nationally — he attributed to that lesson: "the better portion of all sales I have made were made after people had said NO."

The generalisable claims:
- **Defeat is usually temporary and quitting is the only thing that makes it permanent** — Hill says over five hundred successful men told him their greatest success came one step beyond the point where defeat had overtaken them.
- **Every failure carries the seed of an equivalent advantage** — but only for those who analyse the failure to find the lesson, as Darby did. Hill is explicit that the people who profit from defeat are the ones who study it.
- **The persistence test is a filter, not an obstacle.** Fannie Hurst collected thirty-six rejection slips from one publisher across four years in New York before breaking through.

## Application

- When you feel yourself quitting, run the eight causes as a checklist and find which is missing. Usually it is (1) or (4) — an unclear purpose or no written plan — masquerading as a willpower problem.
- Audit yourself against the sixteen symptoms once, honestly, with someone who won't flatter you. Alibi-building is the tell.
- Treat any "I can't because…" sentence as a hypothesis about fear of criticism, and test it: whose criticism, specifically, and what would it actually cost you?
- Act on new ideas within days, not weeks. The window in which an idea survives contact with your own doubt is short.
- After each defeat, write down what the failure taught you before you rebuild the plan. Undigested failure produces "quitability," digested failure produces the opposite.
