# Things Do Not Touch the Soul - Judgment Is Where Disturbance Lives

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Mindset-and-Identity/Mindset and Identity Map|Mindset and Identity]]

## One-line summary
Events stand outside you, silent and inert; the entire disturbance is produced by the opinion you attach to them — and since the opinion is the one thing genuinely yours, it is also the only place intervention is possible.

## Context
Marcus Aurelius was writing privately, to himself, as emperor — under plague, war, and betrayal — which is why *Meditations* reads as maintenance notes rather than doctrine. The core operation it rehearses is the separation of an event from the verdict passed on it. This matters practically because almost every attempt to manage stress targets the event, which is the half you do not control, and leaves the judgment untouched, which is the half you do.

## The distinction

The Stoic division is between what is *up to us* — opinion, impulse, desire, aversion, the whole "ruling part" — and what is not: health, wealth, reputation, other people, outcomes. Marcus applies it as a repeated correction rather than a philosophy:

> The things themselves… come not unto thee themselves; but thou in a manner goest unto them. Let then thine own judgment and opinion concerning those things be at rest; and as for the things themselves, they stand still and quiet, without any noise or stir at all.

And more compactly: **"as for the things themselves, they touch not the soul, neither can they have any access unto it: neither can they of themselves any ways either affect it, or move it."** The soul alone moves itself, and it is moved according to the opinions it grants itself.

The consequence is that externals are classed as **indifferents**: life and death, honour and dishonour, labour and pleasure, riches and poverty happen to good and bad alike and are therefore of themselves neither shameful nor praiseworthy. They are not *unimportant* — Marcus spent his life on imperial business — but they are not where good and bad reside.

## Where the disturbance actually enters

Marcus catalogues five ways the mind wrongs itself, which doubles as a diagnostic list:

1. Being **grieved or displeased with what happens** — which he calls a direct apostasy from the nature of things.
2. Turning **away from another person**, or being led by desires tending to their harm — the state of the angry.
3. Being **overcome by pleasure or pain**.
4. **Dissembling** — acting or speaking falsely and covertly.
5. Acting **to no certain end**, rashly and without considering how it relates to the common purpose.

Note that four of the five are not about circumstance at all. They are about the response.

## The corrective moves

- **Take away the opinion.** "Remember that all is but opinion and conceit." Removing the verdict — *I have been wronged* — removes the injury. Not because the event did not happen, but because the injury was resident in the verdict.
- **Name what you are dealing with.** For any event, ask: does this come from the order of things, from another person's ignorance, or from chance? If from a person, they are acting through ignorance of what is genuinely good for them, so respond by the law of fellowship — kindly and justly.
- **Ask what the situation calls for.** "What is this that now my fancy is set upon? … which of all the virtues is the proper virtue for this present use?" — meekness, fortitude, truth, sincerity, contentment. Converting a provocation into a virtue-selection problem is the practical heart of the method.
- **Use hindrance as material.** Where an impediment blocks one virtue, it makes room for exercising another; and remember you inclined toward the outcome with reservation in the first place.

## Application

1. **Separate the event from the verdict in writing.** Two columns: what happened (facts only, no adjectives) and what I am saying about it. Nearly all the distress lives in the second column, and seeing it there makes it optional.
2. **Adopt the reserve clause.** Commit to outcomes with an explicit *fate permitting* attached. The commitment stays total; the identification does not. This is why disappointment is not the price of ambition.
3. **When someone wrongs you, run the ignorance test first.** Ask what they believed made their action reasonable. This is the same move Carnegie arrives at from the persuasion side, for entirely different reasons.
4. **Audit the five self-woundings weekly.** Which of the five did I do this week? The list is short enough to memorize and specific enough to catch you.
5. **Do not confuse this with suppression.** The instruction is to withdraw the *judgment*, not to perform calm. Marcus is explicit that when you fall short and are cast off, return to it — the return, not the unbroken record, is the practice.

## Limits and cautions

The dichotomy of control is a tool for allocating effort, not for excusing passivity or tolerating genuine injustice — Marcus's own conclusion from it was more duty, not less. And it is misused when applied to other people: telling someone their suffering is merely their opinion is a category error. It is a first-person instrument.
