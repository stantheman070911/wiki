# Decide Promptly, Change Slowly - Indecision as the Seed of Fear

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Decision-Making-and-Risk/Decision Making and Risk Map|Decision Making and Risk]]

## One-line summary
People who accumulate wealth reach decisions promptly and change them slowly if at all; people who don't reach decisions slowly and reverse them fast — and the drift from indecision to doubt to fear is what makes the second pattern expensive.

## Context
Hill's analysis of 25,000 people who had failed put lack of decision near the head of his thirty causes of failure, and his analysis of several hundred people who had accumulated over a million dollars found the prompt-decision habit in every one of them without exception. The chapter's subtitle is "the mastery of procrastination," and its claim is asymmetric: the cost of deciding wrongly-but-fast is bounded, while the cost of the indecision habit compounds into worry and fear.

## Key insights

- **The pattern, both ways.** Successful: decide quickly, change slowly. Unsuccessful: decide slowly if at all, change quickly and often. Hill notes Ford's version of this looked like obstinacy from the outside — he kept building the Model T while advisors and customers urged a change — and concedes Ford probably held on too long, but argues firmness of decision made the fortune before the delay cost anything.
- **Indecision is the seedling of fear.** Indecision crystallises into doubt; the two blend into fear. The blending is slow, which is why the three grow unobserved. Worry, in turn, is sustained fear caused by indecision — so the antidote to worry is not reassurance but a decision.
- **Opinions are the cheapest commodity on earth.** Everyone has a stock ready to hand out. If you are influenced by opinions when you decide, "you will have no desire of your own." Hill's rule: take no one into your confidence except your [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Hiring-and-Team/The Master Mind Alliance - Borrowed Brains Held in Harmony|Master Mind group]], chosen for complete sympathy with your purpose. Where you need facts from others, gather them quietly without disclosing the purpose.
- **Closed mouth, open eyes and ears.** Talking more than you listen costs you knowledge, discloses your plans to people who would rather beat you to them, and displays your exact stock of knowledge to anyone who has more. Hill's epigram: *"Tell the world what you intend to do, but first show it."*
- **Close friends and relatives are the usual source of the damage** — not maliciously. Ridicule "meant to be humorous" and well-meant opinions are how most inferiority complexes get installed.
- **Definiteness of decision requires courage, proportional to what's at stake.** Hill's extended case is the Declaration of Independence: Samuel Adams responding to a bribe-or-hang ultimatum from Governor Gage by telling him to stop insulting an exasperated people; Adams locking the door of the meeting room and refusing to let anyone leave until a decision on a Continental Congress was reached; fifty-six men signing what was, if the Colonies lost, their own death warrant. His point for the reader: you are not staking your life on your decisions, only your economic freedom — which makes the excuse for indecision thinner, not thicker.

## Application

- Set a decision deadline whenever you notice yourself gathering more input. The marginal opinion is nearly always cheaper than the delay.
- Distinguish the two moves explicitly: deciding is fast, *reversing* is slow and requires new evidence, not new discomfort.
- Keep an inner circle of two or three people whose judgment you have chosen deliberately, and stop discussing plans outside it. Collect facts from others without announcing why you want them.
- When something is nagging at you, ask what decision the worry is standing in for. Make it, even if the decision is "accept this and stop optimising."
- Notice when the reason you haven't decided is that you're waiting for someone's permission or approval. That is the fear-of-criticism symptom, not a research gap.
