# Prospect Theory - Reference Points, Loss Aversion, and the Fourfold Pattern

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Decision-Making-and-Risk/Decision Making and Risk Map|Decision Making and Risk]]

## One-line summary
People don't value outcomes, they value *changes from a reference point* — losses roughly twice as heavily as gains — and they systematically overweight small probabilities, which together explain lotteries, insurance, stubborn sellers, and why bad deals get accepted at the end of a bad quarter.

## Context
Classical theory says a decision-maker evaluates final states of wealth. Prospect theory says nobody does that. Two additions — a **reference point** and **loss aversion** — plus a distortion of how probabilities are weighted, predict a set of behaviours that expected-utility theory cannot. Kahneman is candid about why it won: not because it is true, but because *the concepts it added were worth the trouble; they yielded new predictions that turned out to be true.*

## The three ingredients

1. **Reference dependence.** Outcomes are coded as gains or losses relative to a neutral point — usually the status quo, but it can be an expectation, an entitlement, or a target. Change the reference point and you change the decision without changing a single dollar.
2. **Diminishing sensitivity.** The difference between $100 and $200 feels larger than between $1,100 and $1,200. This holds for losses too.
3. **Loss aversion.** Losses loom larger than corresponding gains. The standard ratio: **people weigh losses about twice as much as gains.** *"He weighs losses about twice as much as gains, which is normal."*

Separately, **decision weights ≠ probabilities.** Small probabilities are overweighted (the **possibility effect**: going from 0% to 5% feels bigger than 5% to 10%) and near-certainties are underweighted relative to certainty itself (the **certainty effect**: 95% → 100% feels enormous).

## The fourfold pattern

Combine the two and you get four cells, three familiar and one that was genuinely new.

| | **Gains** | **Losses** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High probability** (certainty effect) | Fear of disappointment → **risk averse.** Accept less than expected value to lock in a sure gain. *A plaintiff with a strong case settles cheap.* | Hope to avoid the loss → **risk seeking.** Reject a favourable settlement and gamble. *A defendant facing near-certain damages fights on.* |
| **Low probability** (possibility effect) | Hope of a large gain → **risk seeking.** *Lottery tickets.* | Fear of a large loss → **risk averse.** *Insurance.* |

The bottom-left cell explains why lottery ticket buyers are indifferent to how minuscule the odds are: without a ticket you cannot win, with one you have a chance, and *"what people acquire with a ticket is more than a chance to win; it is the right to dream pleasantly of winning."*

The bottom-right explains why insurance is profitable: people pay far above expected value, because they are buying the elimination of a worry, not just protection. Parents asked to pay for a safer insecticide would pay **$2.38** to cut a risk by two-thirds, but **$8.09 — more than three times as much** — to eliminate it entirely. The certainty premium is real money.

The top-right cell is the dangerous one commercially. **Facing a probable loss, people become risk-seeking.** This is the manager who doubles down on a failing project, the trader who enlarges a losing position, the founder who bets the company rather than accept a down round.

## The endowment effect

Loss aversion applied to ownership: giving something up is coded as a loss, acquiring the same thing as a gain, so owners demand more than buyers will pay. This is not a preference — it is an asymmetry created by who currently holds the item. It disappears for goods held explicitly for exchange (a merchant's inventory) and appears strongly for goods held for use.

Practically: sellers of businesses, houses, and equity stakes are not being irrational for sport when their price seems high. Their reference point is possession.

## Narrow framing and risk policies

The most fixable error in the book. **Humans are narrow framers**: we evaluate each decision as it arrives, in isolation. A rational agent frames broadly, considering decisions jointly.

Kahneman's arithmetic: take a gamble with a 50% chance of winning $200 and 50% of losing $100. Someone weighting losses 2× values a single play at **zero** and declines. Bundle two of them and the package is worth **$50**. Bundle five and the expected value is $250 with only an 18.75% chance of losing anything.

> The aggregation of favorable gambles rapidly reduces the probability of losing, and the impact of loss aversion on preferences diminishes accordingly.

Nothing about the person's loss aversion changed. Only the frame did. The remedy is a **risk policy**: decide once how you will treat a *class* of small favourable bets, then apply it without re-deciding each time. The mantra is *you win a few, you lose a few* — and its real job is controlling your emotional response when you do lose.

The qualification matters: this works for bets that are genuinely favourable and genuinely small relative to your resources. It is not a licence for [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Decision-Making-and-Risk/Risk of Ruin - Time Probability, Repeated Exposure, and Survival First|ruinous exposure]] — a bet that can end the game is never part of a bundle, because there is no next play.

## Application

- **Set the reference point deliberately in every price, offer, and negotiation.** The identical deal reads as a gain or a loss depending on what you name first. A discount off list is a gain; a surcharge is a loss; the money is the same.
- **Sell the elimination of a worry, not the reduction of one.** The $2.38-vs-$8.09 finding is a pricing instruction: complete guarantees, "never worry about X again," and full-coverage tiers can command more than proportionally more.
- **Expect sellers to be anchored to possession.** When a valuation gap looks irrational, check whether you are arguing with the endowment effect. Changing the reference point (what the asset would be worth *to them* in cash, next year) works better than arguing about the number.
- **Watch for the top-right cell in your own organisation.** When a project or quarter is heading for a certain loss, the people responsible will take risks they would refuse in any other state. That is exactly when to insert a decision review with someone whose reference point differs.
- **Write risk policies for recurring small decisions** — experiment budgets, ad tests, small bets, insurance deductibles — and apply the policy rather than deliberating each instance. Broad framing is the only reliable way to stop declining bundles of good bets one at a time.
- **Never bundle a bet that can end the game.** Aggregation logic requires that you get to keep playing.
