# The Outside View - Reference Classes Against the Planning Fallacy

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Decision-Making-and-Risk/Decision Making and Risk Map|Decision Making and Risk]]

## One-line summary
Forecasting from the details of your own plan produces a best-case scenario dressed as an estimate; forecasting from the record of similar projects produces something usable — and the switch costs one phone call that almost nobody makes.

## Context
Kahneman's own team, writing a textbook, estimated two years to completion. Their curriculum expert then recalled that of every comparable team he knew, **about 40% never finished, and none took less than seven years.** The team had all worked with those teams. Nobody had thought to ask. They pressed on. The book took **eight years** and was never used.

Three lessons came out of that afternoon, and they are the spine of this entry: the **inside/outside view** distinction, the **planning fallacy**, and **irrational perseverance** — the group's refusal, on hearing the grim number, to abandon the project. *"Facing a choice, we gave up rationality rather than give up the enterprise."*

## Inside view vs. outside view

**The inside view** builds a forecast from the specifics in front of you: the tasks you've listed, the pace you've observed, plus a margin for error. It is what everyone does spontaneously. It fails for a structural reason — it is [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Decision-Making-and-Risk/System 1 and System 2 - The Two-Character Model of Judgment|WYSIATI]] applied to the future. You extrapolate from the chapters you already wrote (which were the easy ones, done at peak enthusiasm) and you cannot enumerate the divorces, illnesses, and coordination failures that will actually consume the schedule. *There are many ways for any plan to fail, and although most of them are individually too improbable to anticipate, the likelihood that something will go wrong in a big project is high.*

**The outside view** ignores your plan almost entirely. It asks: what class of projects does this belong to, and what happened to them? That base rate becomes the anchor, adjusted — modestly — for anything genuinely distinctive about your case.

**The planning fallacy** is the predictable gap: plans and forecasts that are unrealistically close to best-case scenarios, and that could be improved by consulting the statistics of similar cases.

## Why the outside view loses even when it's available

The failure is not ignorance. Kahneman's expert *had all the knowledge required* to produce the correct estimate and reached his own initial estimate without ever using it. He knew the base rate and did not think to apply it — the same move as the students who knew the seizure statistics and still predicted the individual would help.

Then, when the outside view was finally stated aloud, **the team collectively ignored it.** Pallid statistics do not survive contact with a concrete personal impression.

The refusal often wears moral clothing. Asked the probability of winning "cases like this one," Kahneman's lawyer cousin replied that **every case is unique** — with a look suggesting the question was superficial. The same proud emphasis on uniqueness shows up in medicine, in founders, and in anyone whose identity is invested in the project being special.

## Overconfidence is a feature, not a defect you can train away

Kahneman is unusually pessimistic about debiasing individuals: *overconfidence is a direct consequence of features of System 1 that can be tamed — but not vanquished.* The obstacle is that **subjective confidence tracks the coherence of your story, not the quality or amount of information behind it.** More detail makes a plan feel more likely and be less likely.

> Organizations may be better able to tame optimism in individuals than individuals are.

That is the actionable claim: this is an institutional problem with institutional fixes, not a willpower problem.

## The procedure

1. **Identify the reference class.** Not "software projects" — *projects of this size, with this kind of team, in this kind of organisation.* The class should be narrow enough to be relevant and wide enough to have members.
2. **Get the class statistics.** Completion times, failure rates, cost overruns. Kahneman's expert produced his informally, from memory, in one conversation. It was not up to scientific standards and it was still far better than the inside view.
3. **Make the baseline prediction** — what you'd predict knowing only the category.
4. **Adjust from the baseline, not from your plan.** Adjust only for factors that genuinely distinguish your case, and adjust less than you want to.
5. **Run a [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Decision-Making-and-Risk/The Pre-Mortem - Imagine Failure Before It Happens|pre-mortem]] before committing.** Gary Klein's procedure: once the team has nearly decided but before formal commitment, say *"Imagine it is a year from now. We implemented this plan. The outcome was a disaster. Write a brief history of that disaster."* Its two effects are precisely targeted — it breaks the groupthink that suppresses doubt once a leader has tipped her hand, and it points imagination at failure modes WYSIATI would never surface.
6. **Decide in advance what number would kill the project** — and say so before you hear it. Otherwise you will do what Kahneman's team did: hear the number, feel the discomfort, and proceed anyway.

## Application

- Before any project estimate goes into a plan, make **one phone call** to someone who has run the comparable thing, and ask two questions: how long did it actually take, and what fraction of attempts you know of never finished.
- Keep your own reference class. Log every project's original estimate against its actual duration and cost. After a dozen entries you have a personal multiplier, and it is more trustworthy than any individual estimate you will ever produce.
- Treat "every case is unique" — from a lawyer, a consultant, a founder, or yourself — as a refusal to be measured, not as an argument.
- Make the pre-mortem a standing gate before capital commitments and major launches. It costs ten minutes and it is the only debiasing tool in the book with a track record inside organisations.
- Watch for irrational perseverance in yourself: the moment you learn the odds are bad and feel your reasoning reorganise itself to keep going. Name it when it happens; that is most of the defence.
