# The Market Always Goes Up - Self-Cleansing Indexes and the Foam You Should Ignore

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Decision-Making-and-Risk/Decision Making and Risk Map|Decision Making and Risk]]

## One-line summary
A broad index rises over time for two structural reasons — failures can only lose 100% while winners have no ceiling, and dying companies are continuously replaced by live ones — so the binding constraint on your returns is not market behaviour but your own capacity to sit still while the price of your holdings is cut in half.

## Context
Most investing advice responds to volatility by **treating the symptom**: diversify across every asset class imaginable and hope a few pull through. Collins' response is that this guarantees sub-par performance in exchange for the slim hope of a smoother ride, and that the actual problem is elsewhere.

> Toughen up cupcake and cure your bad behavior.

The evidence that behaviour is the binding constraint: **the vast majority of mutual fund investors get worse returns than the funds they own report.** The fund's number is what you'd have made by holding. The investor's number is what jumping in and out actually produced. The gap is the cost of psychology.

## Why a broad index rises: two structural reasons

**1. The market is self-cleansing.** Picture every company in a total-market index on a performance bell curve. The worst possible outcome for any one holding is −100%: the company fails, drops below the index's size threshold, and disappears. The best has **no upper limit** — 200%, 1,000%, 10,000%. That asymmetry produces a permanent upward bias. Meanwhile the dead are continuously replaced: of Charles Dow's original twelve stocks from 1896, exactly one remained in the modern Dow. The index is not a fixed basket of companies; it is a rolling membership that ejects the dying and admits the rising, with no decision required from you.

Collins' warning attached to this: **the mechanism only works with broad-based index funds.** Once active management starts choosing which members to hold, the self-cleansing property is replaced by someone's judgement — and charged for.

**2. You own businesses, not paper.** Stocks are pieces of operating companies full of people working to expand and serve a customer base, in an environment that rewards those who deliver and discards those who don't. It is that dynamic — not any property of the ticker — that makes equities the best-performing asset class in history.

## The beer and the foam

The single most useful frame in the book. A poured beer in an opaque mug is partly beer, partly foam, and you cannot see the proportion.

- **The beer** is the actual operating businesses you own a share of.
- **The foam** is the traded price, rising and falling minute to minute. This is the market of CNBC, of the daily report, of the Wall-Street-as-casino comparison, of every expert confidently contradicting another expert.

On any given day, you cannot tell how much of a price is beer and how much is foam — which is exactly why a company can plummet one day and soar the next. **Only the beer compounds.** The foam is what drives average investors onto the ledge, and it is what you must learn to ignore. *"We're in it for the beer."*

Why the foam is so loud: nobody watches a rational person discussing long-term investing. Predicting the Dow at 25,000 or 5,000 gets ratings. Drama is the product.

## The four ways people lose money in a rising market

1. **Believing you can time it.** To time the market once you must be right twice — call the top, then call the bottom — and then repeat it. The world is full of investors who got the first call right and then watched the recovery from the sidelines. Collins' proof that it can't be done reliably: *the person who could would be far richer than Warren Buffett and twice as lionised.* Nothing would be more profitable, which is precisely why so many claim it.
2. **Believing you can pick stocks.** Collins joined an investment research firm in 1989 specifically to gain the information edge — and found analysts who knew their industries' executives, middle managers, customers, suppliers and receptionists, spoke to them weekly, and still could not predict stock performance. The reason is upstream: corporate forecasts are assembled from salespeople guessing at customer behaviour, filtered through managers guessing at their salespeople, then revised upward when leadership finds them unpresentable. *"Predicting the future is a dicey proposition for even the most gifted psychics, and they are not burdened with this process."*
3. **Believing you can pick winning managers.** There are more equity mutual funds than there are publicly traded US stocks. Roughly 7% fold each year, quietly, with assets folded into better-performing funds — which is how companies keep claiming all their funds are stars. Survivorship is a marketing instrument.
4. **Watching the foam.**

Collins' own version of the humility test comes from a martial arts instructor on high kicks: *"Before you decide to use kicking techniques on the street ask yourself: 'Am I Bruce Lee?' If the answer is 'no' keep your feet on the ground."* Investing version: am I Warren Buffett?

## Crashes are the process, not an interruption

Collins is explicit that this is knowledge you need *in your gut*, not intellectually — his own 1987 failure proves the difference. He knew crashes were normal, held for three or four months after Black Monday, then lost his nerve and sold at approximately the bottom. He bought back a year later above the pre-crash high, having locked in the loss and paid a premium for his seat.

What to hold instead:

- Over a 60–70 year investing life, expect **2–3 events on the scale of 2008**, roughly every 25 years, plus far more frequent smaller collapses.
- **Your wealth will be cut in half more than once.** Not might be.
- Every time, the media will scream sell; every time, only those with the nerve to stay will prosper. In good times the same media will explain that this time is different. Both are wrong.
- A crash early in your investing life is **a gift** — every pullback during accumulation buys more shares per dollar.
- Even the 1929 Big Ugly Event, the one true 90% drawdown in 115+ years of data, punished only investors unlucky enough to have bought their entire portfolio precisely at the peak. Buy in 1926–27 and you were positive within ten years. And because the Depression was deflationary, the surviving money bought far more than the same nominal sum had before.

Which produces the real entry test: *"the question is not 'Should I invest in stocks now?' Rather it is 'Should you invest in stocks at all?'"* Until you are certain you can watch your wealth halve and still stay the course, the answer is no.

## The corollary: don't dollar cost average a lump sum

Spreading a windfall into the market over 12 months feels like risk reduction; it is a bet. Between 1970 and 2013 the market rose in 33 of 43 years — **77% of the time.** So DCA is a ~23% bet that you'll be spared some pain, against a ~77% chance you'll be spared some gain, paying more per share with each tranche. Worse, it is market timing with a friendlier name, it silently changes your asset allocation (you are holding a large cash position by default), and at the end of the schedule you face exactly the same risk of a crash the day after you finish.

Collins' concession is honest and worth keeping: if the alternative is not investing at all, DCA. *"But it will mean that you've adjusted your investing to your psychology rather than the other way around."*

Note the distinction he draws: **regular investing out of income is not DCA** — it is simply putting money to work as it arrives, which is the correct default. The critique applies only to deliberately withholding a lump sum you already have.

## Application

1. **Decide the stay-the-course question before you invest, not during a crash.** Write down what you will do when the portfolio halves. If the honest answer is "sell," your allocation is wrong today.
2. **Rehearse the numbers in advance:** 2–3 major crashes per investing life, a halving more than once, and recovery every single time in 120+ years of data. Knowing this intellectually didn't save Collins in 1987; the point is repetition until it's visceral.
3. **Separate beer from foam whenever you check a price.** Ask what changed about the underlying businesses. Usually nothing did.
4. **Treat financial media as entertainment with a business model.** Someone is always predicting every possible outcome, so someone is always right, and their luck will be read as insight.
5. **Invest lump sums immediately** according to your target allocation. If you can't sleep, DCA — but name it as a psychological accommodation, not an optimisation.
6. **Reframe drops during accumulation as discounts,** and keep buying. The mechanism that makes a crash a gift only works if you're still adding.
