# Getting Wealthy vs. Staying Wealthy - Survival, Room for Error, and Tails

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Decision-Making-and-Risk/Decision Making and Risk Map|Decision Making and Risk]]

## One-line summary
Getting money and keeping money are opposite skills — the first needs optimism and risk, the second needs paranoia and frugality — and because compounding and tail events both require you to still be playing, survival is not a defensive posture but the highest-return strategy available.

## Context
Two men crossed paths in October 1929. Jesse Livermore, short the market, made the inflation-adjusted equivalent of **$3 billion in a single day** and became one of the richest men alive. Abraham Germansky, heavily long, was last seen tearing ticker tape into pieces on Wall Street and was never found. Four years later Livermore — having grown confident and levered up — had lost everything, and eventually took his own life.

Housel's point is not about markets. It is that **the skills that produce a fortune actively destroy it if you don't switch.**

## Getting vs. keeping are different skills

| Getting money | Keeping money |
| --- | --- |
| Taking risks | Avoiding risk |
| Optimism | Humility, and fear that it can be taken away |
| Putting yourself out there | Frugality |
| Confidence in your edge | Accepting that some of it was luck, so past success won't repeat on demand |

The evidence that most people never make the switch: **40% of companies successful enough to go public lost effectively all their value over time**, and the Forbes 400 turns over roughly **20% per decade** for reasons unrelated to death or family transfers.

The canonical illustration is Rick Guerin — the third member of the Buffett–Munger partnership, whom almost nobody remembers. Buffett's account: *"Charlie and I always knew that we would become incredibly wealthy. We were not in a hurry to get wealthy; we knew it would happen. Rick was just as smart as us, but he was in a hurry."* Levered on margin loans, Guerin got margin-called in the 1973–74 downturn and sold his Berkshire stock to Buffett at under $40 a share. Same skill at getting wealthy. One extra skill at staying.

## Why survival is the whole game: compounding

Buffett's net worth is ~$84.5 billion. **$84.2 billion of it was accumulated after his 50th birthday; $81.5 billion after he qualified for Social Security.** Housel's thought experiment: if Buffett had started at 30 with a normal $25,000 instead of a precocious $9.3 million, kept his extraordinary 22% annual returns, and retired at 60 — he'd be worth **$11.9 million, 99.9% less.**

The counterpoint proves it from the other side. Jim Simons has compounded at **66% annually since 1988** — three times Buffett's rate, the best record anyone has — and is roughly 75% less rich, because he found his stride at 50 and has had less than half as long to compound.

> His skill is investing, but his secret is time.

So: *good investing isn't about earning the highest returns, because the highest returns tend to be one-off hits that can't be repeated. It's about earning pretty good returns that you can stick with and that can be repeated for the longest period of time.*

## Why survival is the whole game: tails

The second reason you must stay in the game is that almost all the return arrives in a handful of moments you cannot identify in advance.

- **Venture capital:** of 21,000+ financings 2004–2014, **65% lost money**; **0.5%** returned 50x or more and produced the majority of the industry's returns.
- **Public equities:** of the Russell 3000 since 1980, **40% of components lost at least 70% and never recovered**; effectively all of the index's return came from the **7%** that outperformed by two standard deviations. The index rose 73-fold anyway.
- **Disney:** by 1938 Walt had produced 400+ cartoons, most beloved, most losing money. *Snow White*'s 83 minutes paid off every company debt and bought the Burbank studio.
- **Berggruen:** the great art dealers *"bought vast quantities"* and held long enough for the portfolio return to converge on the return of its best elements. They operated like index funds.

Buffett has owned 400–500 stocks and made most of his money on ten. Munger: *"If you remove just a few of Berkshire's top investments, its long-term track record is pretty average."*

The corollary is liberating: **you can be wrong half the time and still make a fortune.** Bezos, after the Fire Phone: *"If you think that's a big failure, we're working on much bigger failures right now."* Reed Hastings, cancelling productions: *"Our hit ratio is way too high right now… we should have a higher cancel rate overall."* Chris Rock tests 40–50 club shows before a tour; the special you see is the tail.

But tails only pay you if you're present when they arrive. Housel's Sue/Jim/Tom simulation — $1 a month into US stocks from 1900 to 2019 — has Sue (never sells) at **$435,551**, Jim (sells during recessions) at $257,386, Tom (sells late, re-enters late) at $234,476. Recessions covered only 22% of those months. *A good definition of an investing genius is the man or woman who can do the average thing when all those around them are going crazy.*

## The three practices of a survival mentality

**1. Aim to be unbreakable, not to be right.** Cash held during a bull market feels like a 9% annual insult. But *preventing one desperate, ill-timed sale can do more for your lifetime returns than picking dozens of big-time winners.* The measured return on that cash is not 1%.

**2. Plan on the plan not going according to plan.** *A plan is only useful if it can survive reality.* Room for error — Graham's margin of safety — is not conservatism. Conservatism avoids a level of risk; **margin of safety raises the odds of success at a given level of risk by increasing your chances of survival.** *"The purpose of the margin of safety is to render the forecast unnecessary."* Its magic: **the higher your margin of safety, the smaller your edge needs to be.**

The card counter is the model. A 2% edge means the house still wins 49% of hands, so you hold a hundred basic units. There is never a moment when you're so right you can bet everything.

Housel's own version: he assumes his lifetime returns will be **⅓ lower than the historic average**, and saves accordingly.

**3. Be barbelled — optimistic about the future, paranoid about what prevents you reaching it.** Over 170 years US living standards rose 20-fold, during which 1.3 million Americans died in nine wars, ~99.9% of companies created went out of business, the market fell more than 10% at least 102 times and lost a third of its value at least 12 times, and 33 recessions ran a cumulative 48 years — *the number of forecasters who predicted any of those recessions rounds to zero.* Growth amid loss is the normal path, not a contradiction. **You need short-term paranoia to stay alive long enough to exploit long-term optimism.**

## Two failure modes to name

**Optimism bias in risk-taking** — *"Russian roulette should statistically work" syndrome.* Taleb: *"You can be risk loving and yet completely averse to ruin."* If something is 95% likely to work, the 5% will arrive at some point in your life; if that 5% is ruin, the 95% isn't worth it no matter how appealing. **Leverage is the devil here** — it converts routine risks into ruinous ones, and being wiped out erases the option to act when opportunity is best.

**Single points of failure.** Everything that can break will eventually break, so anything many things depend on is a countdown. German tanks at Stalingrad: 104 in reserve, **fewer than 20 operable** — field mice had eaten the wiring insulation. Housel's own portfolio companies were nearly killed by a burst water pipe, a health-department complaint about a customer's dog, a spoofed CEO email mid-fundraise. You cannot prepare for what you cannot envision; you can avoid depending on one thing. The biggest single point of failure with money is sole reliance on a paycheque with no savings buffer.

## Application

- **Name which game you are playing right now.** If you are still accumulating, optimism and risk are correct. If the thing you have is worth more than the thing you're reaching for, switch: cut leverage, hold cash, stop scaling exposure with results.
- **Set a margin of safety numerically, not rhetorically.** Assume returns a third below history; assume the project takes the outside-view duration; hold a year of payroll (Bill Gates's rule at early Microsoft) or Buffett's *"more than ample cash."*
- **Audit for single points of failure once a year** — one client over ~25% of revenue, one channel, one key person, one supplier, one platform account. You are not forecasting the failure; you are removing the dependency.
- **Judge results at portfolio level, never individually.** Expect most bets to be duds. If your hit rate is high, you are not taking enough shots — Hastings's complaint, applied to your own experiments.
- **Refuse ruinous exposure regardless of odds.** Favourable expected value is not sufficient when the downside ends the game. Leverage is the usual mechanism; check for it before you check anything else.
- **Pre-commit to behaviour during the 1% of days that decide everything.** Write down now what you will do in a 30% drawdown or a lost anchor client, because the decision you make then will outweigh years of ordinary decisions.
