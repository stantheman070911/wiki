# Die With Zero - Life Energy, the Net Worth Peak, and Decumulation

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Decision-Making-and-Risk/Decision Making and Risk Map|Decision Making and Risk]]

## One-line summary
Money is stored life energy, so every dollar you die holding is hours of your life you worked for and never spent — which makes the real optimization problem not "how much can I accumulate?" but "when do I stop accumulating and start deliberately spending down?"

## Context

Perkins is an energy trader who treats the question of how to allocate a life as an engineering optimization: **maximize fulfillment, minimize waste.** The waste he is attacking is specific and measurable — money earned but never converted into experience.

The framing came from a boss early in his career. Perkins, a screen clerk on $18,000 a year, proudly reported saving about $1,000. The reply: *"Are you a f***ing idiot? To save that money? You came here to make millions. Your earning power is going to happen!"* The formal name for the point is **consumption smoothing** — you transfer money from years of abundance into lean years. Perkins had been running it backwards: taking money from his starving 25-year-old self to give to his wealthy 60-year-old self.

## Money is stored life energy

The unit of account is not dollars but hours. Any money you earned represents life energy you spent earning it, whatever the wage. The corollary is a calculation most people never run:

> **Elizabeth**, a fictitious 45-year-old in Austin earning $60,000, nets $48,911 — $19.56/hour at 50 hours a week. She saves $16,000/year from 45 to 65, retires with $770,000, spends $32,000/year, and dies at 85 leaving **$130,000**. Divide $130,000 by $19.56 and you get **6,646 hours — more than two and a half years of 50-hour weeks worked for free.**

The same arithmetic runs at any income; higher earners simply die with more. And the "but I love my job" objection doesn't survive it: *optimization doesn't care where the money came from.* If work is genuinely a fulfilling experience, keep working — but then you must spend the proceeds, or you have thrown away an extra life you were handed.

## The evidence that people save too much, too late

- Median US net worth **keeps rising into the mid-seventies** — $124,200 for 45–54, $224,100 for 65–74, and a peak of **$264,800 for 75-plus.** People in their seventies are still net savers.
- An EBRI study of the first 20 years of retirement: retirees holding $500,000+ at retirement had spent down a median of **11.8%** two decades later. **One-third of retirees increased their assets after retiring.** Pensioners — the group most insured against ruin — decumulated least (4% vs. 34% for non-pensioners).
- Spending declines with age regardless: average household spending falls from **$65,000 (55–64) → $55,000 (65–74) → $42,000 (75+)**, and that decline holds even counting rising healthcare costs.
- Retirement planners already name the pattern: **go-go years → slow-go years → no-go years.** The public has not absorbed it, so people plan for flat spending from retirement to death and therefore oversave.

Perkins's own illustration is his grandmother, to whom he gave $10,000 in his twenties. The only thing that ever came of it was a $50 sweater she bought him. She had no "go" left. Her furniture stayed under plastic covers her whole life — *the senselessness of indefinitely delayed gratification, in miniature.*

Two forces produce the error. **Myopia** afflicts the spender; **inertia** afflicts the saver. As Shefrin and Thaler put it, *"It is hard to teach an old household new rules."*

## Precautionary saving is usually the wrong instrument

The most common defence of oversaving is catastrophic medical cost. Perkins's answer is that **you cannot self-insure your way out of it**: cancer treatment can run half a million a year; his father's end-of-life hospital nights ran $50,000 each. Whether you saved $10,000 or $250,000 changes the outcome by days. *Either the government will pay for it or you will die.*

The structural point generalizes: **for every risk you fear, there is an insurance product, and the fact that one exists proves the risk can be priced and transferred.** Buying long-term-care insurance early is cheaper than hoarding a self-insurance fund against a crisis that may never come. And healthcare money spent on the front end — prevention, fitness, screening — buys far more than the same money spent at the end.

## Longevity risk and why you are a bad insurance agent

Dying with exactly zero is impossible because you don't know your death date. But *not knowing exactly* is not the same as *knowing nothing*: actuarial calculators (the Society of Actuaries' Longevity Illustrator, Living to 100) give you a probability distribution to plan against. Refusing to look means saving as if you'll live to 150.

Two symmetric risks:

| Risk | What it is | The instrument |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mortality risk** | Dying too early, leaving dependents exposed | Life insurance |
| **Longevity risk** | Dying too late, outliving your savings | Income annuities |

An insurer can price your death because it pools millions of lives and lets the errors cancel. **You cannot pool.** Self-insuring against longevity therefore forces you to hold a worst-case cushion — which is precisely the oversaving that wastes your life energy. This is the "annuity puzzle": economists consider annuities the rational answer and few people buy them.

The practical instruction is not "buy an annuity." It is: **tell your adviser what problem you are solving.** A fee-only adviser optimizing for maximum wealth is solving the wrong problem. *"We are solving for your total life enjoyment."*

## The survival threshold, then the peak

**Step 1 — the floor (a number).** The bare minimum you need never to run out:

> **survival threshold = 0.7 × (cost to live one year) × (years left to live)**

The 0.7 accounts for interest your remaining principal earns while you draw it down (at ~3% above inflation the exact factor is ~0.71; at 5% it falls to ~0.58). Home equity, a reverse mortgage, or an annuity can all count toward it.

**Step 2 — the peak (a date, not a number).** Once past the floor, stop thinking in dollar targets. *No number ever feels like enough* — $2M justifies working for $2.5M, which justifies $3M — and the target moves forever while your health does not. Enjoying an experience needs **money, free time, and health simultaneously**; every extra year of accumulation buys money by spending the other two.

So your **net worth peak** is the date you stop growing wealth and start spending it down. Perkins's simulations, run across many health/earnings/return scenarios, put the optimal peak for most people at **age 45–60** — earlier for the unhealthy or fast-earning, later for the biologically young. It is tied to *biological* age, not chronological: of two 50-year-olds, the one with the body of a 40-year-old should peak later than the one with the body of a 65-year-old.

Waiting until 62 or 65 — the Social Security and Medicare dates that anchor most people — almost guarantees you work longer than necessary for money you will never spend. Worth noting: **retirement is often not voluntary.** In one study of ~14,000 newly retired workers, 39% of 2014 retirees were forced out and another 16% partially forced.

## Decumulation is a behavioural problem

Once past the peak you must **spend more than you earn, and spend at a declining rate** — more in your fifties than your sixties, more in your sixties than your seventies — because your capacity to convert money into experience is falling the whole time. Most of the experiences worth having cluster **roughly between ages 20 and 60**; the couple strolling the beach in the retirement commercial is doing something you actually wanted to do before you retired.

The obstacle is not arithmetic. *For people used to accumulating wealth, decumulation doesn't come naturally.* If you keep working because you love the job, fine — but ramp spending to match, or the enjoyment of the work doesn't rescue the waste.

## Application

- **Convert your leftover to hours.** Take your projected estate, divide by your after-tax hourly rate, and read the answer as unpaid working time. That number, not a philosophy, is what changes behaviour.
- **Run a life-expectancy calculator and write the date down.** Not to be morbid — to make the balance between present and future computable at all. Perkins runs a countdown app for the same reason: *the reminder of death gives a much-needed urgency.*
- **Compute your survival threshold** with `0.7 × annual survival cost × years left`. Everything above it is money you must aggressively deploy on experiences.
- **Set your peak as a date, not a number,** anchored to biological age — get objective health testing rather than guessing. For most people it lands between 45 and 60.
- **Stop self-insuring risks the market prices.** Look at long-term-care insurance and income annuities before you look at a bigger cash pile; check whether your adviser is compensated on assets under management before trusting their view of annuities.
- **Move health spending to the front end.** A small permanent health deficit compounds — ten pounds of excess weight is forty pounds of extra force on your knees — and health is the multiplier on every future experience.
- **Say the goal out loud to whoever manages your money:** maximize total life enjoyment, not terminal net worth. A great stock picker is the wrong specialist for this problem.
