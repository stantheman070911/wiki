# System 1 and System 2 - The Two-Character Model of Judgment

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Decision-Making-and-Risk/Decision Making and Risk Map|Decision Making and Risk]]

## One-line summary
Your mind runs a fast, automatic, always-on story generator (System 1) and a slow, effortful, lazy auditor (System 2) — and because the auditor rarely checks the storyteller's work, confident judgment is usually a coherence signal, not an accuracy signal.

## Context
Almost every error in this vault's decision-making section has the same shape: a plausible answer arrived instantly, felt right, and was never audited. Kahneman's two-character model is the machinery underneath. It is not a claim about brain anatomy — he is explicit that System 1 and System 2 are useful fictions — but it makes the failure modes nameable and therefore catchable.

## The two characters

**System 1** runs automatically and cannot be switched off. It perceives, remembers, associates, feels, and — critically — *constructs a coherent story* from whatever is currently activated. It is where expertise lives (a chess master's few candidate moves are all strong), and it is also where heuristics live.

**System 2** is deliberate, sequential, and effortful. It allocates attention, does arithmetic, holds several things in mind at once, and monitors System 1. Its operations are physically costly: pupils dilate, heart rate rises, blood glucose drops. Its governing habit is the **law of least effort** — it endorses System 1's suggestions unless something forces it to intervene.

Three consequences follow, each with a name.

### 1. Substitution — answering the easier question

When a hard question has no ready expert answer, System 1 quietly swaps in an easier one and answers that. An executive facing *"Should I invest in Ford stock?"* answered *"Do I like Ford cars?"* — and did not notice the swap. This is the engine of every heuristic: the substituted question feels like the original because System 1 never flags the exchange.

### 2. The lazy controller — System 2 doesn't check

*A bat and ball cost $1.10. The bat costs one dollar more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?* The answer 10¢ arrives instantly and is wrong (the correct answer is 5¢). More than **50% of students at Harvard, MIT, and Princeton** gave the intuitive answer; at less selective universities the failure-to-check rate exceeded 80%. The cost of checking was a few seconds. They didn't pay it.

The same laziness lets belief drive logic rather than the reverse. Given *"All roses are flowers. Some flowers fade quickly. Therefore some roses fade quickly,"* most students endorse an invalid syllogism — because the conclusion is plausible. **When System 1 is in charge, the conclusion comes first and the arguments follow.**

**Ego depletion** compounds this. Sustained self-control drains the capacity for more of it, and depletion looks like reverting to defaults. The starkest evidence: eight Israeli parole judges approved roughly **65% of requests right after a food break, falling to near zero just before the next one**. Tired and hungry judges fall back on the easy default — denial.

### 3. WYSIATI — what you see is all there is

System 1 represents only *activated* ideas. Information not retrieved might as well not exist. So it builds the best story available from what it has and reports no discomfort about what is missing.

Ask *"Will Mindik be a good leader? She is intelligent and strong…"* — an answer arrives immediately. Note what you did **not** do: ask what you'd need to know before forming a view. What if the next two adjectives were *corrupt* and *cruel*?

The measure of success for System 1 is the **coherence** of the story, not the amount or quality of the data. This produces the book's most useful and unsettling corollary, demonstrated with mock jurors who heard only one lawyer's argument: **people given one-sided evidence, who knew it was one-sided and could easily have generated the other side, were more confident than people who heard both.** Knowing little makes it easier to fit everything you know into a coherent pattern.

## Why confidence is not evidence

> Subjective confidence in a judgment is not a reasoned evaluation of the probability that this judgment is correct.

Confidence is a *feeling*, produced by the coherence of the information and the cognitive ease of processing it. Kahneman's own version is the **illusion of validity**: as an army psychologist he ran leadership assessments whose predictive accuracy was repeatedly shown to be near-random — and it changed nothing about how confident he and his colleagues felt about the next candidate the next morning. Knowing the general rule had no effect on confidence in individual cases.

The practical inversion: **take admissions of uncertainty seriously; treat declarations of high confidence as evidence that someone has built a coherent story, not that the story is true.**

## Application

- Before acting on a fast judgment about a person, hire, market, or investment, ask the WYSIATI question out loud: *"What would I need to know to answer this well, and how much of it do I actually have?"* If the honest answer is "not much," downgrade confidence rather than the decision.
- When a decision feels easy, suspect substitution. Write down the question you were actually asked and the question you answered. If they differ, you found the bug.
- Treat one-sided input as a confidence *inflator*, not a data point. Deliberately commission the counter-case before a big commitment — see [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Decision-Making-and-Risk/The Pre-Mortem - Imagine Failure Before It Happens|The Pre-Mortem]].
- Schedule consequential judgments — hiring calls, pricing decisions, negotiation sessions — early in the day or after a break. The parole-judge finding is not about judges; it is about System 2 under load.
- Learn to recognise the phrase *"it's obvious"* in your own mouth. It is the sound of System 1 delivering and System 2 not checking.
