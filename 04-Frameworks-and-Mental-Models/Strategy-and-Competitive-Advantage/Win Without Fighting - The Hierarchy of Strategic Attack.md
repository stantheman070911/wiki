# Win Without Fighting - The Hierarchy of Strategic Attack

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]]

## One-line summary
Taking the objective whole beats destroying it, so attack in strict order of preference — the rival's plan first, then their alliances, then their forces, and only as a last resort their fortifications.

## Context
The intuitive picture of competition is a head-on contest of strength, and it is the most expensive option available. Sun Tzu's third chapter ranks four levels of attack by cost and yield, and the ranking is what makes it useful: it converts "compete harder" into an ordered menu where the cheapest options are also the most effective ones, and where the option everyone reaches for first is ranked last.

## The hierarchy

| Rank | Attack | Business translation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 (best) | **Balk the rival's plans** | Anticipate and pre-empt the strategy itself — launch first, close the partnership they need, change the terms of the category |
| 2 | **Prevent the junction of their forces** | Isolate them from allies, channels, and suppliers before those relationships harden |
| 3 | **Attack their army in the field** | Contest them directly, where they are already at full strength |
| 4 (worst) | **Besiege walled cities** | Assault an entrenched, well-defended position — a category incumbent's core, a locked-in enterprise account |

Sun Tzu quantifies the bottom rung. Preparing the siege engines takes three months, piling the earthworks three more; then the commander, unable to control his irritation, throws his troops at the walls "like swarming ants," loses a third of them, and the town still holds. This is the pattern of a frontal attack on an incumbent's strongest position, and the six months of preparation is precisely why it feels like commitment rather than error.

**Supreme excellence is breaking resistance without fighting** — subduing the troops without battle, taking the cities without siege, overthrowing the kingdom without long operations in the field. With forces intact you then dispute the mastery, so the triumph is complete without a man lost.

## The force ratios

Sun Tzu gives an explicit engagement table, and its lower rungs matter more than its upper ones:

- **10:1** — surround them
- **5:1** — attack directly
- **2:1** — divide your force, one part engaging conventionally and one making a diversion
- **Evenly matched** — you may offer battle, but only a capable commander should
- **Slightly inferior** — avoid engagement and watch
- **Quite unequal** — flee

The last three are the operative ones. An obstinate fight by a small force ends, in the long run, with its capture. "Avoid" and "flee" are strategy, not cowardice.

## The five essentials for victory

1. Knowing when to fight and when not to fight.
2. Knowing how to handle both superior and inferior forces.
3. Having an organization animated by the same spirit through all ranks.
4. Being prepared yourself and waiting to take the rival unprepared.
5. Having capability *and* not being interfered with by the sovereign.

## The three ways a ruler ruins their own army

The fifth essential gets its own treatment, because the most common source of defeat is internal:

1. **Hobbling** — ordering an advance or retreat while ignorant of whether it can be obeyed. Like tying a thoroughbred's legs together.
2. **Governing an army as one administers a state** — applying civil norms to a domain that runs on opportunism and flexibility. The military and civil spheres are distinct; you cannot handle an army in kid gloves.
3. **Employing officers without discrimination** — ignoring adaptation to circumstance, using people without regard to where they fit. This shakes the confidence of everyone below them.

When the organization becomes restless and distrustful, rivals move in. This is "simply bringing anarchy into the army, and flinging victory away."

## Know yourself and know the other side

The chapter's closing line is the most-quoted sentence in strategy and is usually flattened into a slogan. Its structure is a three-state payoff table:

| Knowledge | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Know the rival and know yourself | You need not fear the result of a hundred battles |
| Know yourself but not the rival | For every victory gained you will also suffer a defeat |
| Know neither | You will lose every engagement |

The middle row is the interesting one: self-knowledge alone gets you to roughly a coin flip. It is not enough, and the confidence it produces is exactly what makes it dangerous.

## Application

1. **Before authorizing a competitive push, name which of the four levels you are on.** If the answer is "attack their fortified position," require an explicit argument for why levels 1–3 are unavailable.
2. **Look for the plan-level move first.** What would make their strategy irrelevant rather than merely contested — a different pricing basis, a category redefinition, a channel they cannot follow you into?
3. **Buy the alliance before you fight the army.** Exclusive distribution, key partnerships, and the hire of a pivotal person are level-2 moves and cost a fraction of a level-3 fight.
4. **Write down your force ratio honestly.** If you are materially outmatched with no diversion available, disengagement is the correct call and should be made early, while it is still cheap.
5. **Audit yourself for the three ruinous interventions.** Founder-level overrides of teams closer to the ground are all three at once, and they are invisible from the top.
