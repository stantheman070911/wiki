# Rapidity Over Protraction - The Economics of a Campaign

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]]

## One-line summary
A campaign burns capital, morale, and optionality every day it runs, so the objective is victory rather than long operations — clumsy speed has sometimes won, but no state has ever benefited from a prolonged war.

## Context
Sun Tzu's second chapter is titled "Waging War" but is entirely about ways and means: what a campaign costs per day, who pays, and what the delay does to your position with third parties. It is the oldest surviving argument that burn rate is a strategic variable rather than an accounting one, and it applies directly to funded market-share fights, protracted lawsuits, feature wars, and any initiative kept alive past its decision point.

## The argument

**1. The daily cost is fixed and large.** A hundred thousand men in the field cost a thousand ounces of silver a day before a single engagement — home expenditure, front-line expenditure, entertainment of guests, glue and paint, chariots and armour. The cost accrues whether or not anything is happening.

**2. Protraction blunts the instrument.** When weapons are dulled, ardour damped, strength exhausted, and treasure spent, the organization is worse at fighting than when it started. Delay does not preserve the option; it degrades it.

**3. Third parties price your exhaustion.** "Other chieftains will spring up to take advantage of your extremity" — and once that happens, no amount of wisdom can avert the consequences. This is the decisive point for business: a long fight with one rival is an invitation to everyone else, and the invitation is issued automatically.

**4. Therefore speed beats cleverness.** Stupid haste has been seen in war; clever protraction has not. The reasoning is not that haste is good but that tardiness is never anything but foolish, because it means impoverishment regardless of the tactical merits.

**5. Only someone who understands the costs can run a campaign profitably.** Understanding the evils of war *is* the qualification for waging it well — knowing the burn is what forces the urgency.

## Living off the enemy

Sun Tzu's supply doctrine is the ancient version of capital efficiency:

- **Do not raise a second levy or load the supply wagons more than twice.** Do not restructure and re-fund mid-campaign; carry what you need and go.
- **Bring materiel from home, but forage on the enemy.** One cartload of the enemy's provisions is worth twenty of your own, because twenty are consumed transporting one to the front. Resources taken at the point of contact avoid the entire logistics tax.
- **Reward whoever takes the first spoils, and fold captured units into your own.** Substitute your flags on the captured chariots and treat prisoners well. This is "using the conquered foe to augment one's own strength" — the acquisition thesis, stated in the fifth century BCE.
- **Maintaining an army at a distance impoverishes the people**; proximity to an army raises prices and drains local substance. Distance from your base is a compounding tax.

## Application

1. **Put a per-week burn figure on every contested initiative** — fully loaded, including the leadership attention it consumes — and post it where the decision-makers see it. Most protracted fights survive because nobody prices the running cost.
2. **Set the decision date before you start, not after you are losing.** "Let your great object be victory, not lengthy campaigns" is only actionable if the campaign has a terminal date attached at launch.
3. **Prefer resources captured at the point of contact.** Revenue from the contested market, talent hired from the rival, distribution earned by the fight — these carry no transport tax. Funding a price war purely from headquarters is the twenty-carts-to-deliver-one case.
4. **Name the third parties who benefit from a long fight** before you enter one. If a protracted duel with your nearest competitor hands the category to a third player, the fight is already lost on the calculation.
5. **Resist the second levy.** A campaign that needs a second round of funding on the original thesis is usually telling you the thesis was wrong, not that it was underfunded.

## Limits and cautions

Sun Tzu is arguing against *aimless* protraction, not endorsing recklessness — the same book lists recklessness first among the five fatal faults of a commander. Fabian strategy, deliberately measuring your endurance against a rival who suffers more from a long campaign, is a real option; the test is whether the protraction is a chosen instrument with a mechanism, or the residue of an undecided decision.
