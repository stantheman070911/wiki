# Cheng and Ch'i - Direct and Indirect, Momentum and Timing

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]]

## One-line summary
Every engagement combines an expected move that fixes attention (*cheng*) with an unexpected one that decides the outcome (*ch'i*) — the two are interchangeable rather than fixed, and their combinations are inexhaustible, which is why a small force with position and timing beats a larger one without.

## Context
Most competitive playbooks contain a list of moves. Sun Tzu's fifth chapter argues that the list is the wrong unit: there are only two kinds of move, and everything else is combination. Five notes make every melody, five colours every hue, five tastes every flavour. In battle there are only the direct and the indirect, and these two "give rise to an endless series of maneuvers," leading into each other in turn like moving in a circle.

## Cheng and ch'i

**Cheng (正)** is the direct, orthodox, expected operation — the one the rival has fixed their attention on. **Ch'i (奇)** is the indirect, unorthodox, surprising one — from an unexpected quarter.

The crucial subtlety is that **the labels are relative to the rival's perception, not to the move's shape**. As the T'ang emperor T'ai Tsung's commentary puts it: a *ch'i* maneuver becomes *cheng* if you make the rival regard it as *cheng*; then your real attack is *ch'i*. If the rival perceives a movement meant as *ch'i*, it immediately becomes *cheng*. The whole secret is confusing the rival so they cannot fathom your real intent.

Han Hsin marched ostentatiously on Lin-chin — that was *cheng* — while throwing a force across the Yellow River in wooden tubs, which was *ch'i*.

Practically: **the direct method joins battle, the indirect method secures victory.** Both are required. A pure *ch'i* strategy with nothing holding attention is just an unsupported flank.

## Momentum and the moment

Two images carry the operational content:

**Energy (勢) is positional, not personal.** The onset of good troops is like the momentum of a round stone rolled down a mountain a thousand feet high. A log or stone stays still on level ground and moves on a slope; square, it stops, round, it rolls. The clever combatant "looks to the effect of combined energy, and does not require too much from individuals" — they consider the power of the force in bulk first, then use each person according to capability, and do not demand perfection from the untalented. **Design the slope; do not recruit heroes to push the stone uphill.**

**Decision (節) is the timing of release.** Energy is the bending of a crossbow; decision is the release of the trigger. The falcon's strike is effective not because of its speed but because of the self-restraint that holds it until the exact moment. Nelson at Trafalgar took a storm of shot for several minutes without replying, then fired at close range. Terrible in onset, prompt in decision.

## Simulated disorder

Deception in this chapter is presented as a capability test rather than a trick:

- Simulated **disorder** presupposes perfect discipline.
- Simulated **fear** presupposes courage.
- Simulated **weakness** presupposes strength.

You cannot feign confusion to lure someone in unless your subdivisions and signals are genuinely tight enough that the confusion is only apparent. This is why deception is a late-stage capability: it is a use of organizational quality, not a substitute for it.

Sun Pin's retreat from Wei is the demonstration — 100,000 campfires the first night, 50,000 the next, 20,000 the third, until P'ang Chuan pursued a supposedly disintegrating force into an ambush.

## Application

1. **Name the *cheng* and the *ch'i* in your current plan.** If you cannot identify what is holding the rival's attention while the deciding move develops, you have one move, not a strategy.
2. **Check whether your surprise is still a surprise.** The moment a competitor recognizes the flanking move, it becomes the expected one — and your obvious move may now be the real opening. The labels flip; re-read the board rather than the plan.
3. **Fix the slope before staffing.** If a role requires an exceptional individual to succeed, redesign the role. Combined energy over individual heroics is the difference between a system and a run of luck.
4. **Build the release trigger explicitly.** Define, in advance, the observable condition on which you commit fully — then hold fire until it appears. Most commitments are released early, out of impatience rather than signal.
5. **Do not simulate weakness you actually have.** The precondition clause is not decorative: feigned disorder on top of real disorder is simply disorder.
