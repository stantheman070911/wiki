# Weak Points and Strong - Concentrate Where They Are Not

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]]

## One-line summary
Concealing your intentions forces the rival to defend everywhere, which makes them weak everywhere — so you can be one united force against their fractions and arrive locally superior no matter the overall totals.

## Context
This is the chapter Sun Tzu's commentators call the art of war in a nutshell, and it answers the question the force-ratio table leaves open: what do you do when you are outnumbered overall? The answer is that overall numbers are the wrong measure. What decides an engagement is the ratio *at the point of contact*, and that ratio is manufactured by concealment.

## The mechanism

**1. Arrive first.** Whoever is first in the field and awaits the rival will be fresh; whoever comes second and hastens to battle arrives exhausted. Position before engagement is most of the outcome.

**2. Impose your will; do not accept theirs.** The clever combatant "imposes his will on the enemy, but does not allow the enemy's will to be imposed on him." One mark of a great operator is that they fight on their own terms or do not fight. Hold out advantages and the rival comes to you of their own accord; inflict damage and they cannot approach.

**3. Attack only what is undefended.** You can be certain of succeeding in your attacks if you attack only places that are undefended, and certain of your defense if you hold only positions that cannot be attacked. Hence: **that commander is skilled in attack whose opponent does not know what to defend, and skilled in defense whose opponent does not know what to attack.**

**4. Concealment converts into numerical advantage.** By discovering their dispositions while remaining invisible, you can keep your force concentrated while theirs must divide. You form a single body; they split into fractions. A whole against separate parts of a whole means **you are many to their few**, whatever the totals say.

**5. Dispersal is self-inflicted.** If they strengthen the van they weaken the rear; strengthen the left and they weaken the right. "If he sends reinforcements everywhere, he will everywhere be weak." Numerical weakness comes from having to prepare against possible attacks; numerical strength comes from compelling the other side to make those preparations.

**6. Know the place and time.** Knowing where and when the engagement will happen, you can concentrate from the greatest distances. Not knowing, your left wing cannot help your right and your van cannot relieve your rear.

## Forcing and refusing engagement

Two symmetrical moves follow:

- **To force a fight** with a rival sheltered behind ramparts, attack something else they are obliged to relieve. Sun Tzu was no believer in frontal attacks; cut the line they must protect and they come out.
- **To refuse a fight** even with no defenses built, "throw something odd and unaccountable in his way." Chu-ko Liang, about to be attacked at Yang-p'ing, struck his colours, stopped the drums, and flung the gates open with a few men sweeping the ground. Ssu-ma I suspected an ambush and withdrew.

## Water

The chapter's closing image is the reason this cannot be reduced to a checklist:

> Military tactics are like unto water; for water in its natural course runs away from high places and hastens downwards. So in war, the way is to avoid what is strong and to strike at what is weak.

Water shapes its course to the ground; the strategist works out victory in relation to the opponent actually faced. **Just as water retains no constant shape, in warfare there are no constant conditions** — and therefore: *do not repeat the tactics that gained you one victory*, but let your methods be regulated by the infinite variety of circumstances. One who modifies tactics in relation to the opponent and wins may be called a heaven-born captain.

## Application

1. **Ask what the rival must defend, and go elsewhere.** Their strongest position is usually their most public one; the undefended ground is a segment, a channel, a buyer role, or a price point that does not appear in their reporting.
2. **Manufacture local superiority.** You do not need to be larger; you need to be larger *in one segment on one date*. Pick the narrowest ground on which you can be the biggest thing present.
3. **Make your intentions expensive to read.** Public roadmaps, pre-announced launches, and loud hiring all convert your *ch'i* into *cheng* and let a larger rival concentrate. Announce after you have arrived.
4. **Watch for the reinforce-everywhere signal.** A competitor spreading resources across every segment to cover you has just made themselves weak everywhere; that is the moment to concentrate.
5. **Retire the tactic that worked.** The playbook that won the last segment is now the one your rival has studied. Treat a repeated winning tactic as a liability with a half-life.
