# Five Factors and Seven Comparisons - Calculate Before You Commit

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]]

## One-line summary
Before committing to a contest, score both sides on five factors and seven head-to-head comparisons — the outcome is largely determined by that arithmetic, and "many calculations lead to victory, few calculations to defeat."

## Context
Sun Tzu opens with the claim that war is a matter of vital importance where survival is at stake and therefore must be studied rather than improvised. His first move is not tactical: it is to insist the outcome is *forecastable* from a structured comparison made before engagement. This is the ancestor of every honest strategic diagnosis, and its practical value is that it makes the decision *not* to compete a first-class output.

## The five factors

| Factor | Sun Tzu's meaning | Business reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Moral Law** (道) | Whether the people are in complete accord with the ruler and will follow him regardless of danger | Mission alignment: will your team accept cost and risk because they believe in the aim? |
| **Heaven** (天) | Night and day, cold and heat, seasons and times | Timing and macro conditions you do not control — cycles, regulation, capital climate |
| **Earth** (地) | Distances, danger and security, open ground and narrow passes, chances of life and death | Terrain: market structure, channel access, distribution geography, unit economics of reach |
| **The Commander** (將) | Wisdom, sincerity, benevolence, courage, strictness | Leadership quality — judgment, credibility, care, nerve, and the willingness to enforce |
| **Method and Discipline** (法) | Subdivision of the army, gradations of rank, maintenance of supply roads, control of expenditure | Org design, decision rights, logistics, and financial control |

Every general knows these five; the one who genuinely knows them wins, the one who does not, fails.

## The seven comparisons

Score both sides, head to head. Sun Tzu claims these seven alone let him forecast the result:

1. Which ruler is in harmony with their people?
2. Which commander has the greater ability?
3. Which side has the advantages of Heaven and Earth — timing and terrain?
4. On which side is discipline more rigorously enforced?
5. Which force is stronger — morally as well as physically?
6. Which side's officers and men are better trained?
7. On which side is there greater constancy in reward and punishment — is it certain that merit is rewarded and failure penalized?

Note what is *not* on the list: raw resources appear once, in item 5, and even there qualified by morale. Five of the seven are about people, training, and the credibility of the incentive system.

## The five dangerous faults of a commander

The COMMANDER factor is scored not just on virtues but on absence of five besetting faults, each of which is a virtue taken to a ruinous extreme:

1. **Recklessness** — bravery without forethought; leads to destruction, and can be lured into an ambush.
2. **Cowardice** — never taking a risk, letting advantages slip; leads to capture.
3. **A hasty temper** — provokable by insult.
4. **Delicacy of honour** — over-sensitive to shame, so goadable into fighting on someone else's terms.
5. **Over-solicitude for one's people** — a misplaced pity that relieves a besieged part at the cost of the whole.

When an army is overthrown, the cause is usually found among these five.

## Application

1. **Score the seven before committing budget to a contested market.** Do it as a two-column table with a named owner for each row, and force a numeric call rather than a paragraph.
2. **Treat "do not engage" as a legitimate result.** The chapter's entire purpose is that the calculation precedes the commitment. A comparison you only run after committing is a rationalization.
3. **Audit your own five faults, by name, quarterly.** Each is the shadow of a strength you already have — the reckless founder is the decisive one, the over-solicitous manager is the caring one.
4. **Modify plans as circumstances shift.** Sun Tzu closes the chapter by refusing to make the calculation binding: having taken the counsel's profit, avail yourself of helpful circumstances beyond the ordinary rules. The comparison sets the prior; contact with the rival updates it.
5. **Keep the calculation private.** The chapter ends by noting these devices "must not be divulged beforehand." A scored comparison that leaks is a gift to the other side.
