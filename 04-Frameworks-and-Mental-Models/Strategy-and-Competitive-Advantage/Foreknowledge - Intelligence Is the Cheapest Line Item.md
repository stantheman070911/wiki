# Foreknowledge - Intelligence Is the Cheapest Line Item

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]]

## One-line summary
Foreknowledge of the rival cannot be deduced, calculated, or divined — it can only come from people — so grudging the small sum that buys it while spending vastly more per day on the campaign itself is the most expensive economy available.

## Context
Sun Tzu's final chapter is an argument about budget allocation, and its structure is what makes it useful. He opens not with tradecraft but with the daily cost of the campaign, then observes that armies may face each other for years over a victory decided in a single day. To remain ignorant of the rival's condition because you begrudge a hundred ounces of silver in salaries — while the campaign burns a thousand a day — he calls the height of inhumanity, and disqualifying in a leader.

The modern equivalents are direct: a company spending millions on a market entry and nothing on customer discovery, or running a year-long build with no channel into what the buyer actually wants.

## Foreknowledge cannot be derived

The chapter's sharpest claim is epistemological. What lets a leader strike and achieve things beyond ordinary reach is **foreknowledge**, and it:

- **cannot be elicited from spirits** — no oracle, no vision;
- **cannot be obtained inductively from experience** — reasoning from analogous cases will not give it;
- **cannot be obtained by deductive calculation** — length, breadth and magnitude yield to mathematics; human intentions do not.

Knowledge of the other side's dispositions can only be obtained **from other people**. As one commentator puts it: the spirit-world yields to divination, natural science to inductive reasoning, the laws of the universe to calculation — but an opponent's dispositions are ascertainable through people and people alone.

This is why desk research, market models, and inference from public behaviour are structurally insufficient. They tell you what happened; they do not tell you what the other side intends.

## The five classes

| Class | Who they are | Modern analogue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Local** | Inhabitants of the district, won over by kind treatment | Customers, users, and people who live in the market you are entering |
| **Inward** | Officials of the rival organization | Insiders — the aggrieved, the passed-over, the ones who want their side to fail so their judgment is vindicated |
| **Converted** | The rival's own agents, detached and turned | The competitor's channel partners, analysts, and consultants who now work with you |
| **Doomed** | Agents given false information to carry back, knowingly or not | Deliberate misdirection routed through a channel you know they monitor |
| **Surviving** | Those who go, observe, and return with news | Your own people in the field — win/loss interviews, conferences, trials of the rival product |

When all five are at work, none can discover the system: this is "divine manipulation of the threads," and the sovereign's most precious faculty.

## Two cautions the chapter itself supplies

**Inward spies cut both ways.** The commentary is explicit that an insider offering to help may be a plant — the classic case being P'o-t'ai, whipped until he bled and sent to a rival governor as a defector, who then signalled an ambush that destroyed the force he had invited in. **A source who arrives volunteering exactly what you want to hear is the case requiring most scrutiny, not least.**

**Converted spies are the hinge.** Because they can both feed you truth and carry your falsehood back, they are the class Sun Tzu says to treat most generously. The Ch'in campaign against Chao turned entirely on planted reports that persuaded the Chao king to replace a cautious general with an overconfident theorist; the resulting army was annihilated.

## Application

1. **Set an explicit intelligence line item as a fraction of the campaign budget.** If a market entry costs a million a year and customer discovery costs nothing, the allocation is wrong on the chapter's own arithmetic.
2. **Prefer people over reports.** Thirty conversations with buyers, churned customers, and ex-employees of the competitor will out-predict any amount of secondary research. This is the "from other men" clause, and it is not a metaphor.
3. **Interview your losses.** Win/loss calls are the cheapest surviving-spy programme available, and most organizations do not run them because losing is unpleasant to revisit.
4. **Assume the flow is bidirectional.** Whatever channel you use to learn about a rival is a channel they can use on you. Decide deliberately what your public behaviour teaches them.
5. **Discount volunteered intelligence in proportion to how welcome it is.** Score sources on incentive, not on plausibility.
