# Authority Is Earned, Not Assumed - Ambivalence, Vision, and the Entitlement Curse

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Life-Leadership-and-Wellbeing/Life Leadership and Wellbeing Map|Life, Leadership, and Wellbeing]]

## One-line summary
People feel two things about every leader at once — they need one and resent needing one — so support is structurally fickle, and the only durable protection is authority earned through vision and empathy rather than the position itself.

## Context

Greene's Law of Fickleness. It rests on a prior claim about emotion: **our feelings are almost never pure.** We hold love and hostility, admiration and envy, at the same time. The pattern is set in childhood — even with attentive parents we resented our dependence on their care, and even with cold ones we glossed over the coldness because admitting they didn't care would have been unbearable. *"When one emotion dominated us, the other lay underneath, a continual ambivalent undertone."*

Part of the reason is that strong pure emotions are frightening — they read as a momentary loss of control — so we unconsciously balance them with the contrary feeling. And we don't notice, because contemplating our own emotional complexity is baffling and we prefer simple stories about ourselves and everyone else.

**Leaders sit exactly on this fault line**, because we unconsciously associate them with parental figures:

| The need | The resentment |
| --- | --- |
| Groups fragment into narrow agendas and competing interests. Without someone above those interests seeing the larger picture, hard decisions never get made and nobody is guiding the ship. We crave that, and feel *"disoriented, even hysterical"* without it. | We fear those in power will use the position to accumulate more and enrich themselves. We are willful, and dislike the inferiority and dependence of serving. And we secretly envy the recognition and privileges leaders hold. |

## Why support collapses without warning

The ambivalence tips negative at the first sign of abuse, insensitivity, or incompetence — and it is invisible until it does. **Leaders see smiles in the corridor and applause at meetings and mistake deference for reality.** People almost always defer to those above them, because their fate is in those hands and they cannot afford to show what they feel. So the mistrust accumulates silently, and surfaces all at once with an intensity that shocks the person it lands on.

Greene's historical point is that this isn't a modern, democratic phenomenon. Among indigenous cultures and early civilizations, revered chiefs and kings were routinely put to death for showing age or weakness, losing a battle, presiding over a drought, or favouring their own clan — *and the executions were occasions of celebration*, a release of pent-up hostility toward leadership as such. We no longer execute chiefs; we do it symbolically in elections and media, and we still blame leaders for economic downturns they don't control the way our ancestors blamed them for rainfall.

## Authority: the Roman sense of the word

Some leaders build a bulwark against this — Moses, Asoka, Pericles, Scipio Africanus, Elizabeth I; more recently Lincoln, King, Buffett, Merkel, Jobs. Greene calls that **authority**, in the original sense of the Latin *auctoritas*, whose root means **"to increase or augment."**

Romans granted it to those who embodied the wisdom of the republic's founders — and it produced an *augmented presence*: increased prestige and credibility, so that every word carried extra weight. Such leaders **didn't need speeches or force**; citizens willingly followed. Crucially, it bought them latitude: *"they were not judged merely by their latest success."* For a notoriously fractious and power-suspicious people, authority was the mechanism that kept combativeness in check and got things done.

**The essential ingredient is adherence to a higher purpose** — ideals that transcend the pettiness of daily political life.

## The twin pillars: vision and empathy

**Vision.** The fundamental task of a leader is to provide a far-reaching view, see the global picture, work for the greater good, and maintain unity. That is what people actually crave. Anything that looks petty, self-serving, or indecisive stirs the ambivalence. Set practical goals from the vision and guide the group toward them — and become genuinely masterful at the visionary process through practice, because that produces real confidence, *"as opposed to the fake confidence of those who are merely grandiose."*

**Empathy.** Leadership is a dynamic relationship, and your slightest gesture has unconscious effects. Attune to shifting moods; **never assume you have support.** Empathy must be visceral enough that you can feel respect draining. Show respect and trust downward and it flows back; the goal is to engage people's willpower so they *identify* with the mission rather than comply with it.

**But empathy must not become softness.** Yielding to the group's will signals weakness. On the primary task — vision and direction — be stern and immovable. Listen to ideas and adopt the good ones, but hold that you have the fuller command of the picture, and refuse political pressure to look fairer at the cost of diluting the vision. *"Toughness and empathy are not incompatible, as Queen Elizabeth I demonstrated."*

## The friction spiral

When both pillars are missing, a predictable sequence runs:

1. Members feel the distance, and sense they're viewed as replaceable pawns.
2. They register the absence of direction and the constant tactical reaction to events.
3. Respect erodes quietly — they listen less closely, spend more of the day on their own interests, form factions, work at three-quarter speed.
4. If the leader responds with force, they turn passive-aggressive. **If the leader responds by pleading for support, respect falls further — "as if the group were now leading the leader."**

> This friction, caused by the leader's own inattentiveness, is why so many leaders get so little done and are so mediocre.

Do it the other way and the ambivalence doesn't vanish — that would violate human nature — but it becomes **manageable**. People still waver, and they forgive faster; they have come to dread the disunity that would follow your absence. Instead of dragging the group uphill you channel its creative energy.

## The entitlement curse

The failure mode Elizabeth I deliberately avoided. Her predecessors — Henry VIII, Mary Queen of Scots — assumed loyalty came with the position. The costs were structural, not merely moral:

- **Homage without connection.** Subjects paid respect while feeling the distance and how little they were considered.
- **Blunted effectiveness.** Ministers were cowed, so their energy went into appeasing the monarch rather than using their own intelligence.
- **Neglect of the boring work.** Governing details were beneath them; glory came from wars of conquest that drained the country.

Elizabeth's counter-move was to invert the assumption: **feel no entitlement, keep the weakness of your actual position in mind, and turn active — earn the loyalty rather than passively expecting it.**

## Seven strategies for establishing authority

The premise: authority means people *willingly* follow — they choose your advice and want your wisdom. Force may occasionally be necessary, but it is the opposite of this.

1. **Find your authority style — Authenticity.** It has to emerge from your actual character and strengths, because a borrowed style reads as opportunistic. Greene's archetypes: the **Deliverer** (acute hatred of injustice, powerful with words — Moses, King); the **Founder** (keen sense of trends, aversion to the status quo, joy in inventing a new order); the **Visionary Artist** (learns the conventions of a field then inverts them — Picasso, Coltrane, Lynch); the **Truth Seeker** (no tolerance for lies and politicking); the **Quiet Pragmatist** (wants only to fix what's broken, with infinite patience); the **Healer** (a knack for what will fulfill and unify people); the **Teacher** (gets people to initiate action and learn from their own mistakes). Played naturally, the style seems to come from beyond you.
2. **Cultivate the third eye — the Vision.** The capacity to see the whole picture behind the fleeting appearances of daily life, trained deliberately rather than waited for. Greene's case is Xenophon: a student of Socrates travelling with ten thousand Greek mercenaries stranded in Persia after their commanders were executed, who — because he had practised that kind of rational, whole-picture thinking for years — was the one who could see a route home while everyone else bemoaned their fate.
3. **Lead from the front — the Tone.** Work as hard as or harder than anyone; set the highest standards for yourself; be consistent and accountable; make any sacrifice first. Members then raise themselves to your level and internalize your values without being lectured. **Set the tone at the beginning** — first impressions are critical and a later attempt looks forced. Show initial toughness: *"if people get the impression early on that they can maneuver you, they will do so mercilessly."*
4. **Focus outwardly — the Attitude.** Reverse the default self-absorption three ways: hone listening until you read between the lines and sense what people are missing (never take smiles and approving looks as reality); **dedicate yourself to earning respect rather than feeling entitled to it**; and treat leadership as a responsibility with the group's welfare hanging on each decision, rather than as a source of attention.
5. **Stir conflicting emotions — the Aura.** Most people adopt one consistent persona and hide the rest. Slightly contrary qualities make people pause before categorizing you, and the more they think about you the larger your presence. The model is a parent: mostly warm, with a clear undertone of intolerance for certain behaviour — *"the child is trapped between affection and a touch of fear, and from that tension comes respect."* Keep anger rare so that when it appears it lands.
6. **Never appear to take, always to give — the Taboo.** Taking away something people assumed was theirs — money, rights, privileges, their own time — creates visceral insecurity and reopens every question about your legitimacy. If sacrifice is unavoidable, make it first and make it real rather than symbolic, frame the loss as temporary, and say precisely when it will be restored. Husband resources like Elizabeth so you can afford generosity — and **don't overpromise**, however good it feels in the moment.
7. **Rejuvenate your authority — Adaptability.** Authority accumulates, which is its own hazard. Held too long it turns rigid; you become the father figure who has monopolized power, and a new generation arrives immune to your aura and sees a relic. You also drift toward intolerance and entitlement without noticing. The defence is the sensitivity Elizabeth kept all her life — reading the moods behind people's words and gauging your effect on newcomers and the young. **Losing that empathy should be your greatest fear**, because it is the point at which you begin to cocoon yourself.

## Application

- **Stop reading the room off the smiles.** Deference is the default behaviour of anyone whose fate you control; assume the true sentiment is somewhere below what you see, and build a channel — skip-levels, anonymous input, a trusted contrarian — that can carry it.
- **Write the vision down and check whether your week matches it.** If your calendar is all tactical reaction, the group can already tell, and that is what erodes respect long before any specific failure.
- **Never plead for support.** If you feel it slipping, the corrective is direction and visible standards, not appeals. Pleading accelerates the loss.
- **Pick your archetype honestly** and lead in that register. A Quiet Pragmatist performing as a Deliverer reads as manipulation.
- **Sequence toughness first.** Set limits and standards at the start of any new leadership relationship; warmth added later is credible, toughness added later is not.
- **Treat any takeaway as a legitimacy event.** Before removing a budget, a perk, or people's time, take the cut yourself first, state its duration, and say what restores it.
- **Audit yourself annually for entitlement.** Are you skipping the boring operational detail? Do you expect loyalty rather than earning it? Have you stopped noticing how new and younger people react to you? Each is the beginning of the rigidity that ends authority.
