# Time Buckets - Plan Experiences Against the Health Curve

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Life-Leadership-and-Wellbeing/Life Leadership and Wellbeing Map|Life, Leadership, and Wellbeing]]

## One-line summary
You die many small deaths before the final one — each life phase closes its own window without announcing the date — so schedule your intended experiences into five- or ten-year buckets against a declining health curve, rather than deferring them into a single undated "someday."

## Context

Perkins watched *Pooh's Heffalump Movie* with his daughters many times. Then one day the younger one, aged ten, simply wasn't interested any more. *"If someone had told me that by this date my kid would stop wanting to watch the Heffalump movie, I probably would have watched it with her a lot more."*

That is the general problem. **The day you die and the day you stop being able to enjoy a given experience are two different dates**, and only the first one gets a funeral. The teenager in you dies, the college student dies, the parent-of-an-infant dies. Each transition is irreversible and none of them is announced in advance.

## Health is the binding constraint, and it is a curve

Three things are required to enjoy an experience — **health, free time, and money** — and they are almost never abundant at the same time:

| Life stage | Abundant | Scarce |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Twenties | health, free time | money |
| Middle years (the *real* golden years) | health (still high), money | free time |
| Retirement | free time, money | health |

The design instruction follows directly: **at every age, trade the abundant resource for the scarce ones.** Young people undersell their free time. People in midlife should be buying time back with money — and the more money they have, the more of it they should be converting. Perkins's canonical example is laundry: two hours a week at $40/hour of net earnings is $80 of your time against a $50 service, and the two hours are worth having even if you spend them doing nothing productive. Research supports it — people who spend money on time-saving purchases report greater life satisfaction *regardless of income*, because reduced time pressure improves daily mood and repeated mood lifts raise overall life satisfaction.

Health declines from a peak in the late teens and twenties. Not every system at the same rate — bone density, lung function, cardiac health, vision, cognition and sense of smell all have distinct curves — but all of them decline. What is under your control is the *slope*, not the direction, and the slope compounds: a small deficit now becomes a large one in fifteen years. (Ten pounds of excess weight is forty pounds of extra force on your knees; cartilage wears; walking becomes painful; more weight follows.) **Health is the multiplier on every future experience**, which is why a small permanent health loss produces a large loss in lifetime fulfillment.

The concrete effect on your money: an ageing skier who once got 20 runs a day now gets 15. Same ticket price, **75% of the experience.** A study of travel constraints found people under 60 are limited mainly by time and money, while people 75 and older are limited by health — *"health problems were increasingly a constraint as age increased."*

## Two ways to price the delay

**Your personal interest rate.** How much someone would have to pay you to postpone an experience by a year. At 20 it is low — you'll still be able to do the thing at 21. At 80 it may exceed 100%. If you are terminally ill it is infinite. **Your personal interest rate rises with age, and most people act as though it were flat.**

**"Would I rather?"** At an 8% real return, $100 becomes ~$216 in ten years — enough for two of the experience instead of one. So ask: *one now, or two in ten years?* At 20 the answer is often "wait" (you'll still have the health, and two is better than one). At 70 it is almost always "now." Two caveats: the experience has to be repeatable — a friend's wedding isn't — and sometimes waiting genuinely buys a better version (*"experiencing Las Vegas at 40 is much better than Las Vegas at 20"*).

## The time-bucket exercise

1. **Draw a timeline** from your current age to your projected death date and divide it into **five- or ten-year intervals.** Each interval is a bucket.
2. **List the experiences you want** — activities and events, large and small, repeatable and once-only. Explicitly **ignore money at this stage**; cost is the excuse that kills the list before it exists.
3. **Drop each item into the bucket where you'd ideally have it**, based on health and life phase.
4. **Then add money** and watch the distribution shift slightly right, toward the years when you can afford better versions — but only slightly.
5. **Re-bucket every five to ten years**, and especially as you approach your net-worth peak.

**Time buckets are not a bucket list.** A bucket list is written late, reactively, by someone confronting mortality and racing the clock. Time buckets are proactive: they distribute a life's worth of intent across the decades *before* the windows close.

What the exercise reveals: experiences don't spread evenly. Ignoring money, they cluster heavily in your twenties and thirties, when health is highest and parenthood hasn't consumed the calendar; with children, a second cluster forms in your thirties and forties. Even after you factor money in, **the vast majority of the experiences you want must happen roughly between ages 20 and 60.** The images in retirement advertising are mostly of things you wanted to do *before* retiring.

The swimming-pool analogy makes the constraint concrete: a resort has a kiddie pool, a teen pool with the waterslide, an adult pool, a seniors' pool. You may use each one fully — but only while you qualify. Miss the waterslide and no amount of later skill or regret gets you back on it.

## Why the exercise makes you happier, not sadder

Contemplating loss reads as morbid; empirically it isn't. Psychologists asked one group of college freshmen to imagine moving away in 30 days and to plan the month accordingly, savoring the campus and people; a control group merely logged their activities. **The savoring group was measurably happier at the end of the month.** We already know how to do this — it's what we do as tourists, treating a week somewhere as the scarce resource it is. We simply stop doing it at home, where things feel abundant.

The regret data points the same way. Bronnie Ware, a palliative nurse, found the most common deathbed regret was *wishing they'd had the courage to live a life true to themselves* rather than the life expected of them; the second — and the top regret among her male patients — was *"I wish I had not worked so hard."* Not the hard work of raising children: the work that cost them *"their children's youth and their partner's companionship."*

## Application

- **Do the exercise at minimum resolution if the full one overwhelms you:** three buckets covering the next 30 years. Add detail later.
- **Name the health-gated items first.** For each, ask: can I still do this in seven years? Perkins decided to wakeboard at 50 on exactly this test — *probably yes now, definitely not at 57, therefore now.*
- **If you have children, find your Heffalump movie.** What is the one experience with them you want more of before this phase of their life ends? That window is measured in months, not decades.
- **Buy time back explicitly.** List the recurring chores you dislike, price them against your net hourly rate, and outsource the ones that lose. This is the highest-leverage move in the middle years.
- **Invest in health as an experience multiplier, not as healthcare.** Preventive fitness raises the quality of every subsequent bucket; end-of-life medical spending raises almost nothing.
- **Run the "would I rather" test on each deferral.** One now versus two later — and be honest about whether the experience is even repeatable.
- **Re-bucket before you scale back work.** Many people reach retirement with a vague plan for the first year and nothing after it, and drift into aimlessness. Decide what the free time is *for* while you're still deciding.
