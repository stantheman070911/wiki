# The Experiencing and Remembering Selves - Duration, Peaks, and Endings

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Life-Leadership-and-Wellbeing/Life Leadership and Wellbeing Map|Life, Leadership, and Wellbeing]]

## One-line summary
An episode has two imperfect measures: its moment-by-moment lived quality and the story retained afterward, which overweights peaks and endings while often neglecting duration.

## Context
People make future choices from remembered experience, but memory does not preserve every minute at equal weight. Daniel Kahneman distinguishes the **experiencing self**, which answers “Does it hurt now?”, from the **remembering self**, which answers “How was it, on the whole?” The first lives the time; the second keeps score and makes many later decisions.

## The evidence

**Colonoscopy study.** In a study designed by Donald Redelmeier and Kahneman, 154 patients rated their pain every 60 seconds during procedures lasting from 4 to 69 minutes. Their later assessments of total pain did not track the sum of those reports. They were predicted largely by the average of the worst moment and the pain near the end.

Kahneman names the two patterns:

- **Peak-end rule:** retrospective evaluation is disproportionately shaped by the most intense moment and the ending.
- **Duration neglect:** elapsed time receives little or no weight in the remembered evaluation.

**Cold-hand experiment.** Participants completed a 60-second immersion in painfully cold water and a 90-second immersion whose final 30 seconds became slightly less painful. Among those who noticed the easing, 80% chose to repeat the longer trial. Its ending produced the better memory even though it contained all the pain of the shorter trial plus 30 unnecessary seconds.

**The scratched symphony.** A listener told Kahneman that a scratch near the end of a long recording had ruined the experience. Kahneman's distinction is that it ruined the memory, not the preceding 40 minutes of enjoyment, which had already occurred.

## The two ledgers

For the experiencing self, an episode is the sum of its moments: when intensity stays equal, twice as much pleasure is better and twice as much pain is worse. For the remembering self, an episode becomes a story organized around selected moments. That story matters because people identify with it, learn from it, and use it to choose what to repeat or avoid.

Neither ledger can replace the other. Memory can recommend a longer painful episode because it ended better, while a purely duration-weighted account omits the narrative people retain and care about. Kahneman concludes that both selves must be considered when their interests diverge, without offering a simple rule for resolving the conflict.

## Application

- **Separate the questions.** Ask both “What was it like while it happened?” and “What story am I carrying now?” A single overall rating answers only the second.
- **Do not let an ending rewrite elapsed time.** A bad final meeting does not make every earlier day bad; a pleasant farewell does not refund a long period of avoidable strain.
- **Design for both selves where possible.** Reduce needless unpleasant duration while also creating humane endings, clear transitions, and occasions for honest reflection.
- **Audit repeat decisions.** Before repeating or avoiding an experience, identify whether the choice follows the lived pattern or one memorable peak or ending.
- **Treat “I will always remember this” as a prediction.** Importance may emerge through later consequences and repeated attention rather than through intensity at the time.
