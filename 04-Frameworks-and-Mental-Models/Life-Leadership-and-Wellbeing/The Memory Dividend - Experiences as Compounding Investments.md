# The Memory Dividend - Experiences as Compounding Investments

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Life-Leadership-and-Wellbeing/Life Leadership and Wellbeing Map|Life, Leadership, and Wellbeing]]

## One-line summary
An experience is not consumption but an investment that pays a **memory dividend** for the rest of your life — which means the standard "start early, start early, start early" investing advice applies to experiences, not just to money.

## Context

The usual accounting treats a trip as spending and a stock purchase as investing. Perkins argues the categories are wrong: an investment is *any mechanism for generating future income*, and the payoff does not have to be financial. Economists already accept this when they call education *"investment in human capital."* Experiences belong in the same class.

The reason is memory. You do not wake up with a blank brain; you wake up preloaded. **Every time you recall an experience you get a fresh, smaller instance of it** — triggered by a song, a smell, a photograph. So buying an experience buys you the experience *plus the sum of all its future recollections.*

## The mechanics

**The original experience is one bar; the dividends are a second stack of bars.** Score any experience in arbitrary "experience points" — how much enjoyment it gave you. The initial event is one bar on the chart. Each act of remembering adds a small bar afterwards. Stacked, the dividend column can equal the original — and sometimes exceed it.

**Dividends compound when shared.** Retelling an experience *is itself an experience*: you're communicating, laughing, bonding, advising, being vulnerable. So an experience makes you someone with more to share, which generates further experiences. *One plus one can be more than two.*

The commercial proof is that platforms monetize this: Facebook's and Google Photos' "On this day 3 years ago" exists because your memory dividend is valuable enough to farm for engagement. Before that, friends and family played that role for free.

**Therefore start early.** The earlier the original experience, the longer the tail of dividends — and the more likely the tail outweighs the head. Perkins's roommate Jason borrowed ~$10,000 from a loan shark at 23 to backpack Europe for three months on an $18,000 salary; Perkins refused to go and finally went at 30, by which point he was *"too old and too bougie to stay in youth hostels."* Jason's verdict decades later: *"Whatever I paid, I feel it was a bargain… I would never have them erased for any amount of money."* Perkins's is regret that grew with time.

**Being cash-poor is not a reason to wait.** Fulfillment is a function of time *and* money, and when you are young, healthy, and unjaded you extract enormous value from cheap or free experiences — free festivals, exploring your own city on foot, hanging out with friends. What you cannot recover later is the youth, not the money.

## You retire on your memories

Perkins's father, too frail at the end of his life for any travel, was given an iPad loaded with the highlight reel of the 1959 Rose Bowl season he'd played in. He laughed, cried, and called it the best gift ever. Too old to acquire new experiences, he could still draw the dividend on old ones.

This inverts the standard retirement story. We are told to save money to fund retirement; Perkins's point is that **the principal you actually retire on is the memory stock**, and money is only the roundabout route to building it. Carson, the butler in *Downton Abbey*: *"The business of life is the acquisition of memories. In the end that's all there is."*

The same reframe applies to conventional investments. When a friend agonized over the financial return on a Central American vacation property, Perkins told him to reject the frame: *"There is nothing special or life-changing about earning 3 percent on foreign real estate."* The question is **return on experience**, not return on equity — how often will you actually be there, and with whom?

## Deliberate, not automatic

The memory dividend only argues for spending *deliberately*, not for spending more. Perkins's own failure mode after his boss told him to stop hoarding was to *"go from autopilot-save to autopilot-spend"* — buying stereos beyond what his ears could hear, pricier restaurants that weren't different. He can't name a single memory dividend from those years. Money spent to be spending buys nothing.

The counter-tool is simple awareness of the trade. On the "latte factor": Perkins does *not* tell you to skip the coffee — *"the last thing I want for you is to finish rich in money and poor in enjoyable experiences."* He tells you to price it: *I could have a round-trip ticket anywhere in the country every few months for what I spend at Starbucks — which do I want?* Choose the lattes if you like. Just don't choose them on autopilot.

## Application

- **Write the list before you price it.** Name the experiences you actually want and how many times you want them, ignoring cost at this stage — money is a distraction from working out what your life should contain.
- **Ask "what would I invest in today?"** — "early" always means now. For each item on the list, ask what the risk is of *not* having it this year.
- **Choose the people, not just the activity.** Dividends compound through sharing, so pick experiences with people you'll keep retelling them with.
- **Farm your own dividends deliberately.** Take the photos, make the album, plan the reunion, compile the video. The dividend is not automatic; you can raise its yield.
- **Convert big purchases from ROE to ROX.** Before a house, a boat, a membership: how many days will you actually use it, with whom, and what memories does that generate? Compare that against the alternative experiences the same money buys — not against an interest rate.
- **Audit your spending for autopilot.** Any recurring expense you can't attach a memory to is a candidate for redeployment — not for cutting to "finish rich."
