# Give While Living - Timing Gifts and Charity for Maximum Impact

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Life-Leadership-and-Wellbeing/Life Leadership and Wellbeing Map|Life, Leadership, and Wellbeing]]

## One-line summary
An inheritance is a random amount of money arriving at a random time for whichever heirs are still alive — so if you actually intend to give, separate that money out and hand it over while it can still change a life, because you cannot be generous when you are dead.

## Context

Every argument for dying with a large estate arrives as *"but what about the kids?"* — often with a moralizing edge, as though planning to spend your own money were selfish. Perkins's response is that the objection is usually hypocritical, not because giving is wrong but because **almost nobody who raises it has actually specified an amount, a recipient, and a date.** His challenge to friends who make it: *"Where's your trust fund for your kids? How much is it set to? When is it going to distribute? Have you even thought about these things?"*

The reframe that dissolves the objection: **money you have allocated to your children is already their money, not yours.** "Die with zero" applies only to *your* money. Separate the two, and the conflict disappears — you can then spend your own share to the hilt without guilt.

## The problem with inheritance is timing, not amount

**The three Rs: random amounts, at a random time, to random people** — random because you don't know which heirs will still be alive, or what they'll need, when you happen to die.

The data on when the money actually lands:

- Across every income group, the **age of inheritance receipt peaks around 60** — the natural product of an 80-year modal lifespan and a 20-year parent-child gap. The distribution is roughly normal, so for every 100 people inheriting at 40, another 100 inherit at 80.
- Between **80% and 90%** of US households receiving a wealth transfer received it as an *inheritance* rather than an in-vivo transfer.
- Economists studying bequests find motives are a mix of intentional and unintentional — and **the two are indistinguishable after the fact.** A deliberate gift and the leftovers of precautionary saving look identical in the data. Perkins's objection is that the givers themselves often can't tell which it was.

The human version: Virginia Colin raised four children alone *"mostly at the edge of poverty."* At 49, by which point she had reached stability, she inherited $130,000 from her mother's estate. *"It just would have been a lot more valuable a lot earlier."* A lifeline arrived as a bonus. Her father, who had lived through the Depression, had been *"afraid of not having enough even when they had more than enough."* She now gives to her own five children and stepchildren early: *"If you get it when you are 30, you can buy a nice house and raise your kids in the environment you want."*

## The peak utility of a gift is roughly 26–35

The same declining-utility curve that governs your own spending governs your recipients'. At the peak, a dollar buys a dollar of enjoyment; by 50 it might take $1.50 for the same effect; by 80 it buys almost nothing. **So a gift's value has an age, and later is not safer — it is weaker.**

Perkins's estimate, based on physiology and maturity, is that **ages 26 to 35 maximize impact** — old enough to be trusted with money, young enough to convert it into life. An informal poll of 3,500+ people agreed: over half chose 26–35; only 12% chose 18–25 (immaturity), only 6% chose 46 or older. Against that, the actual modal receipt age of 60 is a systematic mistiming — and the extreme cases are absurd. Perkins's friend Baird, at 50, is being offered money by his 76-year-old mother who can no longer spend it; he no longer needs it.

Two forces produce the mistiming, the same two that cause oversaving generally: **autopilot** (doing what everyone around you does, because large grants at death are culturally ingrained) and **fear** (precautionary saving against a medical catastrophe that insurance handles better than hoarding).

## Charity has no maturity threshold at all — only urgency

Everything above applies to charitable giving, minus the "too young" concern. *There is an optimal time, and it is never when you're dead.*

Perkins's illustration is deliberately uncomfortable. Sylvia Bloom, a Brooklyn legal secretary, worked 67 years, lived in a rent-controlled apartment, took the subway into her nineties, and left **$8.2 million** to charity — $6.24M to the Henry Street Settlement, $2M to Hunter College. It was called the epitome of selflessness. Perkins declines the label, on a specific ground: **once you're dead the transfer is legally enforced, so it isn't a choice, and generosity requires a choice.** *"You can be generous only when you're alive."*

The substantive charge isn't about the word, though — it's **inefficiency.** People who were needy during her lifetime did not benefit. The returns argument is concrete: worldwide, **social returns to secondary and higher education run above 10% per year.** To justify holding the money and investing it yourself instead of giving it now, you'd have to reliably beat that. And some recipients compound the delay — in 1999 foundations took in over $90 billion and distributed under $25 billion, which is why one analysis concluded donors *"should ask not just how, but how soon, their gifts will be used."*

The counter-model is **"giving while living"**, the term of Chuck Feeney, the Duty Free Shoppers founder who gave away more than $8 billion anonymously, largely by his eighties, while living in a rented apartment on a net worth of about $2 million. Robert F. Smith paying off the Morehouse class of 2019's student loans is the same move: not in the will, done while alive, benefiting people who were about to be indebted rather than people who someday would be.

And the principle scales down. It costs under $500 a year to sponsor a child through organizations like Save the Children or Compassion International. If you don't have money to give, you have time.

## The legacy that isn't money

The final asymmetry: for children, **one of the experiences you are bequeathing is time with you.** Memories of parents have measurable long-run effects — adults recalling higher parental affection show better health and lower rates of depression, and children who received more affection early show better relationships and lower substance abuse. Those are experiences with memory dividends flowing to *them.*

Which produces a genuinely radical conclusion: **once your family's basic needs are met, working more to earn more may be depleting your children's inheritance rather than building it** — and the richer you already are, the more likely that is. The value of an extra hour with your kids behaves like the value of water: worthless when you have fifty gallons, priceless in the desert. A billionaire's marginal work hour is nearly free to the family finances and expensive to the children. A subsistence worker's is the opposite. Most people are in between, and the honest question is: *does this additional hour of work add to my legacy or deplete it?*

Perkins's caution against sentimentality is worth keeping: this is not the "best things in life are free" cliché. *Everything you do takes away from something else you could be doing* — the point is to make the trade explicitly rather than assuming that earning always wins.

## Application

- **Name a number, a person, and a date — today.** Whatever you intend to give, decide the amount and when it lands. An intention you haven't specified is not a plan; it's a bequest by default.
- **Move the money out of your estate and into their name** (a trust, a 529, an outright transfer). Once it's theirs, it stops being part of what you spend down — which is what frees you to spend the rest.
- **Target ages 26–35 for children**, adjusting for maturity. Spreading the giving across several transfers is fine; Perkins's stepson received ~90% of his "inheritance" at 29 to buy a house.
- **Check your will for people older than you.** Perkins found his mother, sister, and brother in his — and asked why they should wait. Anyone in your will who will be less able to enjoy the money later is a candidate for giving now.
- **For charity, give now and ask how soon.** There is no maturity argument, the suffering is present-tense, and the return on early giving (especially education and medical research) is high. Ask the recipient organization whether the money gets deployed or endowed.
- **Don't fund giving out of a future business exit.** Perkins's advice to a friend planning to donate the proceeds of a startup: the charity needs the money now, and a smaller gift today beats a larger hypothetical one later.
- **Price the work-versus-time trade explicitly** rather than assuming more earning benefits your children. Ask what the marginal hour is worth to them — and whether the window on that phase of their childhood is still open.
