# Your Triggers Are Guides - Decoding Anger, Jealousy, Regret, and Resentment

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Life-Leadership-and-Wellbeing/Life Leadership and Wellbeing Map|Life, Leadership, and Wellbeing]]

## One-line summary
Every "negative" emotion carries a message about what you need or want; a trigger is not the problem but the notification, and the reason a challenge becomes a chronic issue is that you shut the feeling down instead of reading it.

## Context

The instinct with a trigger is to treat the thing that triggered you as the problem. It isn't. **The problem is that you don't know what to do with how you feel**, so the emotion gets shut down and stored, and you become sensitive to a world full of things that might bring it back up. *"Our internal guidance systems whisper until they scream"* — and if you never learn to listen, you eventually dissociate from the signal and become its victim.

The reframe that makes this usable: emotional intelligence is not calm or positivity. It is **the ability to interpret the sensations that come up in your body and understand what they are telling you about your life.**

## Decoding the emotions

| Emotion | What it's actually reporting | Its shadow, if unread |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Anger** | Where your boundaries are and what you find unjust. It is an influx of motivation meant to mobilize *you* — *"often the peak state we reach before we truly change our lives."* | **Aggression** — the energy projected onto others instead of used to change your own circumstances. |
| **Sadness** | The correct response to losing something you loved. It releases in waves, not at once. Crying at appropriate times is a **sign of strength**; people who are struggling most are often those who can't release. | Blocked grief — refusing the natural phases. |
| **Guilt** | Usually about what you *didn't* do. Notably, people who commit genuinely heinous acts feel little remorse — so feeling it is itself a decent sign. If it attaches to a specific act: admit, apologize, correct. If it's diffuse, ask who taught you that you were a burden. | Carried childhood guilt projected onto present circumstances. |
| **Embarrassment** | You didn't behave in a way you're proud of. *"Other people can never make us feel as embarrassed as we make ourselves feel"* — when you're confident you're doing your best, the feeling mostly stops. | **Shame** — a passing feeling turned into a verdict on your worth. |
| **Jealousy** | A cover-up emotion: it presents as anger or judgment but is really **sadness and self-dissatisfaction.** *"If you want to know what you truly want out of life, look at the people who you are jealous of."* The anger is that they let themselves pursue it and you didn't. | Condemning the accomplishment — necessary to justify not pursuing it yourself. |
| **Resentment** | Someone failed to match the picture of them in your head. It reads like a demand that *they* change. But *"other people are under no obligation to live up to our ideas of them."* The work is revising the image, not the person. | Projected regret aimed outward. |
| **Regret** | Not a punishment for the past but instructions for the future. Didn't travel young? Regret is telling you to travel now. **Most people regret what they didn't do more than what they did** — which is exactly why the emotion is forward-facing. | Rumination. |
| **Chronic fear** | Usually not a real threat but underdeveloped response systems. Note the tell: **we fear the unlikely thing we can't control** — a likely threat gets responded to, not feared. Holding the thought feels like retaining control; it *is* the loss of control, because you're spending energy on what you can't change instead of the habits that would move you. | A defeating spiral built out of one scary thought. |

The escape from chronic fear is acceptance rather than combat: the moment you can shrug and say *and if that happens, it happens*, the power returns. The fire is fed by the belief that accepting the possibility means inviting it.

## The three-step processing loop

When you can process only half your emotions, you avoid every situation that might produce the other half — which means avoiding the actions that would change your life. And unprocessed feelings don't leave; you sit and dwell in them because you don't know how to make them go.

1. **Get clear on what happened.** Why exactly are you upset? Without this you replay details endlessly without locating the injury.
2. **Validate the feeling.** Anyone in your situation would probably feel the same. Allow the physical release — crying, shaking, journaling, telling a trusted friend.
3. **Determine a course correction.** Only now: what changes in your behaviour or thinking to produce a different outcome next time?

## Controlling versus suppressing

The distinction that decides whether emotional regulation is health or damage:

> **Suppressing is unconscious; controlling is conscious.**

Suppression follows a fixed pattern: deny the reaction → assume it will pass if ignored → find daily life vaguely disrupted → reach a breaking point and produce an outburst you can't control. It behaves like an unconscious bias — you're unaware of it and it's shaping you anyway.

Controlling means you **know** you're angry and are choosing the response. Strictly, you aren't controlling the emotion at all — *"Emotions are temporary, but behaviors are permanent. You are always responsible for how you choose to act."*

The test in the moment: **do you know how you feel?** If yes, and your behaviour is deliberate, that's control. If you can't name the feeling and your behaviour seems to happen to you, that's suppression.

The related redefinition of mental health: not how happy you seem or how unconditionally positive you can be, but — like physical strength, which is really how efficiently the body handles daily tasks and the occasional challenge — **whether you can move through ordinary life and its occasional shocks without being stifled by yourself.**

## Needs are not weaknesses

A frequent driver of suppression is the belief that having needs is insufficiency. Wiest rejects both extremes: codependence leans too far on others, but *"believing that you don't need anyone or anything"* is the mirror image of the same wound — mistrust and inability to connect.

> Your need to feel validated is valid. Your need to feel wanted is valid. Your need to feel secure is valid.

Humans are wired for connection and are most fulfilled serving something beyond themselves. So the need for financial security isn't greed; the need for recognition for your work isn't vanity. **The behaviour you're using to meet the need may be broken — the need itself usually isn't.**

## Peace over happiness

The goal-level version: trade "happiness" for **inner peace.** Happiness is fickle and attaches to achievements, possessions, other people's opinions, and events unfolding a particular way — *"when your goal is happiness, you will always find just behind it a lingering sense of unhappiness."* Achieve the money, the relationship, the promotion, and you return to baseline.

The meditation image: your thoughts are stones dropped in a lake. **You don't force the water still — it stills on its own when you stop interrupting it.** Inner peace isn't created; it's returned to. Camus: *"In the midst of winter, I found there was, within me, an invincible summer."*

## Application

- **When triggered, ask what the emotion is reporting before asking how to stop feeling it.** Anger → which boundary was crossed? Jealousy → what am I not letting myself pursue? Regret → what am I being told to do now?
- **Keep a jealousy list.** Whose life produces the sting? That list is a more honest statement of your goals than anything you'd write deliberately.
- **Convert resentment into a revised expectation.** Ask what you can learn rather than what they should change; the expectation is yours to fix.
- **Run the three-step loop in writing** rather than replaying events. Name the injury, validate it, then decide the one behavioural change — and stop before the third step is done, because clarity without correction is just rumination.
- **Test yourself for suppression:** can you name what you feel right now? If not, you're not regulating, you're storing.
- **Interrogate a chronic fear for its metaphor.** Ask what it would mean if the fear were symbolic, then act on that meaning. If it stays unlikely and uncontrollable, practise the shrug.
- **Distinguish the need from the strategy.** Grant the need outright, then replace the mechanism serving it badly.
