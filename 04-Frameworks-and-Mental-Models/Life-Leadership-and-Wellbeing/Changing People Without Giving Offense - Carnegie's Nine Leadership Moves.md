# Changing People Without Giving Offense - Carnegie's Nine Leadership Moves

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Frameworks and Mental Models Index|Frameworks and Mental Models]] → [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Life-Leadership-and-Wellbeing/Life Leadership and Wellbeing Map|Life, Leadership, and Wellbeing]]

## One-line summary
When correction genuinely is required, nine sequencing moves let a leader change behaviour without producing resentment — the load-bearing one being that praising the slightest improvement outperforms condemning the remaining fault.

## Context
The rest of Carnegie's material is about avoiding criticism. This part answers the obvious objection: a manager's job sometimes *is* changing people's behaviour, and pretending otherwise is not leadership. The nine moves are about the delivery mechanism, not about softening the standard.

## The nine moves

1. **Begin with praise and honest appreciation.** It is easier to hear something unpleasant after hearing something true and good.
2. **Call attention to mistakes indirectly.** Notice the shift from "you did a fine job, *but*…" to "…*and* if you keep improving X." The "but" retroactively converts the praise into a set-up; the "and" leaves it intact.
3. **Talk about your own mistakes first.** Admitting you made the same error at their stage removes the status differential that makes correction sting.
4. **Ask questions instead of giving direct orders.** "What do you think of this?" or "Would this work?" preserves autonomy, invites the person's own improvement to the plan, and lets them save face if the idea is theirs.
5. **Let the other person save face.** Even when you are entirely within your rights, a public unmasking destroys something that took years to build and buys nothing.
6. **Praise the slightest improvement, and praise every improvement.** Abilities wither under criticism and blossom under encouragement.
7. **Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to.** Name the quality you want them to have as though they already have it, and they will strain not to disappoint the assessment.
8. **Use encouragement; make the fault seem easy to correct.** Tell them the thing is learnable and they have the aptitude, and the barrier drops from identity to task.
9. **Make the other person happy about doing what you suggest.** Tie the request to something they already want.

## Why move 6 does most of the work

Animal trainers reward the first flicker of the desired behaviour rather than punishing everything else; managers rarely do the equivalent. Carnegie's case files are biographical: Caruso was told by his first teacher that his voice sounded like wind in shutters and kept singing because his mother praised him; Dickens's whole trajectory turned on one editor's recognition of an unpaid story; H. G. Wells was pulled out of a draper's shop by a schoolmaster who told him he was fit for finer things.

The modern operating form matters more than the anecdotes: **praise must be specific to read as sincere.** A print-shop worker about to be terminated for a "negative attitude" was instead told exactly why one job he had produced was the best work the shop had seen in months. Within days he was a different employee. General flattery would not have done it — the specificity is what proves the praise was earned rather than administered.

## Application

- **Audit your last five pieces of feedback for the word "but."** Rewrite each with "and."
- **Open a correction with your own version of the same mistake.** If you do not have one, that is a signal you may be correcting a difference in style rather than an error.
- **Convert directives into questions for anything where the how is negotiable.** Reserve direct orders for safety, legality, and deadlines — where autonomy is not actually on offer, faking it is worse than being plain.
- **Name the reputation you want them to grow into**, in writing, in a context others can see. This is move 7 and it compounds: the person now has a public identity to defend.
- **Keep a running note of small improvements per direct report** and mention one per week. Without a written prompt you will only notice the failures, because failures interrupt you and improvements do not.

## Limits and cautions

These are delivery mechanisms, not substitutes for a standard. Praising improvement in someone who is in the wrong seat delays a decision that gets more expensive with time — pair this with the right-seat test rather than using encouragement to avoid a hard conversation. And moves 1 and 3 become manipulative theatre if run as a formula; if the praise is not true, omit it and be plain instead.
