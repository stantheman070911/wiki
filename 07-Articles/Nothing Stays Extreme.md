# Nothing Stays Extreme

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

Flight instructors in the Israeli Air Force once told Daniel Kahneman that his advice on training was wrong, and they had the evidence. Praise a cadet for a clean manoeuvre and the next attempt was worse. Scream at one after a bad manoeuvre and the next attempt improved. They had watched it happen many times. They were right about everything they had seen and wrong about all of it.

Instructors praised only unusually good runs and shouted only after unusually bad ones. An unusual performance is followed by a more ordinary one whatever anyone says to the pilot in between. The instructors had observed arithmetic and concluded it was psychology. Kahneman draws out what that arithmetic teaches anyone living inside it: "we are statistically punished for being nice and rewarded for being nasty."

This is not a curiosity about training. It is the ordinary way causal beliefs get manufactured inside an organisation, because interventions are, almost by definition, applied at extremes. Nobody restructures a territory after an average quarter, rewrites the onboarding flow after an unremarkable month, or has the difficult conversation with a salesperson who is doing fine. We act at the trough and at the peak, and then read the next number as a verdict on the action. The next number was going to move toward the middle anyway.

Two things sharpen the trap. The first is sample size. Extreme readings appear more often in small samples than large ones, purely as arithmetic — which is why a two-week A/B test or a five-customer survey reliably produces confident nonsense, and why a monthly growth sprint that diagnoses the weakest metric can spend its whole cycle chasing a fluctuation. The second is that some variation has no cause worth naming at all. In Goldratt's dice-and-match simulation, every station had identical average capacity and downstream output still fell behind while inventory piled up. No station was underperforming. Negative deviations accumulate down a dependent chain and positive ones are capped, so a balanced system produces growing queues on its own. The correct response was protective capacity and buffers — design against variation — not an investigation into who was slow.

Which sits awkwardly beside a large body of advice that says to react to your outliers, and works. Creators are told to find a video with five times their follower count in views and template it, then build each month at seventy percent proven concepts, twenty percent doubling down on their own best performers, ten percent fresh experiments. Investors are told the opposite of averaging: in venture, sixty-five percent of financings lose money and half a percent return fifty times or more, and those few carry the industry. Extremes are not always noise. Sometimes they are the only thing that matters.

The distinction is what you do with the reading. Templatising an outlier is not a prediction that the number will repeat — it won't. It is a claim that one component inside the winner is transferable, followed by running that component again at volume. That is a new sample, and it either survives or it doesn't. Doubling down because the number was high is a conclusion; pulling the hook out and re-testing it is an experiment. The same fork governs the manager. Reacting to a bad month is a conclusion about a person. Watching whether the behaviour recurs is a measurement.

The remedies are unglamorous and mostly procedural. Andrew Grove's rule for appraisals is to test every conclusion against at least two concrete observations and to separate repeated behaviour from a single unusual event — and to allow for time offsets, since today's numbers may be delayed light from a predecessor's work. He once rated a manager highly on excellent sales and profit while turnover signals were already flashing; output collapsed the following year. Kahneman's own correction is arithmetic: start from the baseline for cases of this kind, form your intuitive estimate, then move from the baseline toward the intuition by only the fraction the evidence genuinely predicts. Steven Bartlett's experimentation rule points at incentives — reward the quality of the test, which the team controls, not the result, which it does not. And Morgan Housel's discipline is to judge at the level of the portfolio, never the individual bet, because a hit rate that looks healthy usually means too few shots were taken.

None of this removes the variance. The dice keep rolling, the quarter keeps having a worst month, and the intervention keeps arriving at the exact moment the number was about to move on its own. What can be removed is the habit of measuring a new reading against the worst one. Before crediting the fix or condemning the person, ask what the starting point was. If it was extreme, the next result was always going to drift toward the middle, and the only question worth asking is whether it travelled further than the arithmetic would have carried it unaided.
