# A Gift Can Arrive Too Late

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

For years after her divorce, Virginia Colin raised four children with almost no child support, often near the edge of poverty. By 49, she had remarried, was holding a decent part-time job, and had reached financial stability. Then her mother died and left $130,000 to each of her five children.

The money was welcome. It was no longer transformative. As Colin later put it, it would have been much more valuable earlier. A lifeline had arrived after the rescue.

We usually measure generosity by the size of the gift. But value also depends on timing and on what the recipient can still do with it. In US Federal Reserve data from 2013–2016 cited by Bill Perkins, the probability of receiving an inheritance peaks around age 60. That follows naturally from an 80-year modal lifespan and a typical 20-year parent-child age gap. It is also a poor way to schedule help. The date of a parent's death has no necessary relationship to the years when a child is paying for education, starting a family, or buying a home.

Wills remain necessary: death can be sudden. Nor should anyone give away the reserve that protects health, dependents, and old age, or hand responsibility to someone unprepared for it. The point is not early at any cost. It is delivery when useful. Protect a credible margin, then deliberately schedule what has genuinely been earmarked for others. Earlier should mean designed, not indiscriminate.

Drawing on Joseph Campbell, Ray Dalio describes later life as “returning the boon”: passing on the special knowledge earned through the journey. In his own case, the legacy included money, too. His account also complicates the timing. Dalio found that giving money away well can be as difficult as making it. His family began with direct work in orphanages, created a foundation, and spent years testing causes, measuring results, revising policies, and seeking advice. A late-life responsibility with its own apprenticeship is one more reason to begin before late life.

That is the second cost of posthumous giving. It not only schedules delivery around the giver's death instead of the recipient's need; it removes feedback. A living giver can see whether support widened someone's choices, created dependence, or went unused, then change the next gift. A charity can be asked how quickly funds were deployed. Knowledge can be passed on while its teacher is still available for questions. A bequest gives the original giver no second attempt.

Colin did not merely form an opinion about her parents' delay. She changed the next transfer. She now gives to her five children and stepchildren while she is alive, according to their needs. Money delivered while a family is being built can shape the home those children grow up in; decades later, the same amount may only decorate a settled life. Her inheritance arrived late. The lesson did not have to.

The useful legacy question is not merely, “What will be left?” It is, “When can what I have still change a life?” Intent deserves a date.
