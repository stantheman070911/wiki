# What Could Have Killed This?

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

Two ideas in this wiki appear to contradict each other.

Taleb's Lindy effect holds that for things which do not physically age — books, rules, tools, practices — having lasted a long time is evidence of robustness. Time works as an adversarial filter. It exposes a thing to changing users and unexpected shocks and removes the fragile alternatives, so what still stands after two centuries has crossed more environments than what shipped last year.

*The Sovereign Individual* argues something close to the opposite. Institutions routinely keep their names, offices, and rituals long after the logic that justified them has failed. After the last Western Roman emperor was removed, the Senate went on meeting, offices went on being filled, insignia went on being issued — what the authors call persistent make-believe. The form outlived the function by generations.

Both accounts are correct, and Taleb supplies the reconciliation himself in a line that is easy to read past: Lindy evidence is weak wherever subsidy, coercion, monopoly, or institutional protection prevents failure. Age is not the evidence. Exposure to failure is. Age is only a proxy for it, and the proxy fails at exactly the moment protection appears.

That turns a vague question — this has been around a while, does that mean something? — into a specific one. What could have killed this, and was that mechanism live?

A broad market index shows what a live mechanism looks like. The worst any single holding can do is lose everything; the company then falls below the index's size threshold and disappears. The best has no ceiling. JL Collins notes that of Charles Dow's original twelve stocks from 1896, exactly one remained in the modern Dow. The index is not a fixed basket but a rolling membership that ejects the dying and admits the rising, with no decision required from the owner. Collins attaches a warning: the property belongs to broad-based index funds. Once someone is choosing which members to hold, automatic ejection is replaced by a person's judgment — and charged for.

The same book supplies the counterexample from the same industry. There are more equity mutual funds than there are publicly traded US stocks, and roughly seven percent close each year, quietly, their assets folded into better-performing siblings. Afterward the family can truthfully report that all its funds have strong records. Nothing survived a test. The failures were removed from the record. Survivorship became a marketing instrument.

So there are three ways for something to be old, and only one of them is evidence. It kept passing a live test. It was shielded from the test. Or the failures were deleted, so that what remains merely resembles a survivor.

The third is the hardest to catch, because from the inside it looks exactly like the first. Dalio's answer is a research standard rather than an attitude: a principle qualifies only if it would have held in all past periods and all other countries — which means going back to the periods and places you skipped because the data was inconvenient. "I have never seen that happen" is a statement about the size of your sample, not about the world.

What produces the second case is worth naming plainly, because it is a design flaw and not a moral failing. People insulated from their own errors can preserve a bad theory indefinitely; a system learns only when poor decisions actually remove capital, authority, or future opportunity. A practice with no removal mechanism is not being tested. It is being stored.

The question cuts in both directions, which is the part usually lost. It should make you slower to defer to an old thing — and slower to demolish one. A rule that survived genuine exposure may be carrying a function nobody in the room can articulate, and the useful move is to find out what killed the alternatives before removing it, keeping the protective function even after the original explanation has gone obsolete. Collins' version, for an organization deciding what may change: is this the core, or only a current expression of the core?

And when you suspect a form has outlived its function, arguing about the mandate is the slowest possible route. Ask instead who is performing the work now, who resolves the disputes, where users are already routing around the thing. Functional migration is visible long before anyone announces it.

The question is never how long something has lasted. It is whether anything was ever permitted to end it.
