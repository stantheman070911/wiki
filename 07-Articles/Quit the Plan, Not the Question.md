# Quit the Plan, Not the Question

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

“Never quit” is stirring advice, but it becomes dangerous when it gives every commitment the same status. A person can endure a hard season in the right work, or a year inside the wrong plan. From the inside, both can feel like character.

The error is treating discomfort as a verdict. Difficulty may simply be the price of doing something that matters. But the mirror-image error is just as costly: treating effort already spent as proof that a direction must be right. The longer we defend a choice, the easier it is to mistake loyalty to our past self for evidence about the future.

The useful distinction is between the question and the plan. “Can I build a useful business?” is a question. “Will people buy doggy skateboards?” is a plan. When a specific offer meets real customers and its founding assumption fails, retire the offer—not your capacity to build. Conversely, when the assumptions remain intact and the work is merely slower or harder than expected, a new plan may be an escape disguised as strategy.

Changing direction should neither be a dramatic leap nor a test of toughness. It should be an experiment. Before betting the company, career, or identity, ask what has to be true. Then devise the smallest encounter with reality that can test it. A conversation, a week in the unglamorous work, a small paid project: these are how commitment becomes intelligent.

Elise loved the idea of opening an Italian deli. She opened one, it succeeded, and she became miserable because she had not tested whether she liked operating a café day after day. A few shifts in someone else’s deli might have revealed the difference between loving the concept and loving the work. A prototype isolates one question before the answer becomes expensive.

There is one more discipline: decide in advance what evidence would make you stop, change, or continue. A pre-mortem asks you to imagine the project has failed and name the causes while you can still act on them. Assign an owner, a warning sign, and a response to the risks that matter. That turns doubt from a vague feeling into a design constraint.

Good quitting is persistence aimed at the right level. Keep the larger question alive. Quit the tactic reality has disproved. Test the next answer cheaply enough to learn from it. The goal is to change course for a reason.
