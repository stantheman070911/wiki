# A Business Can Lose Without Being Attacked

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

In 1997, Richard Rumelt rebuilt the economics of Denton's, a twenty-eight-outlet garden-supply chain. Its existing measures mixed retail performance with decades of land appreciation and omitted some costs from individual stores. Rumelt created an adjusted measure he called gain to operating. The best outlet produced $1.05 million. All twenty-eight together produced $0.32 million. One store contributed more than the whole chain.

The losses had not arrived as a dramatic new attack. They had accumulated behind plausible accounting choices. Old properties looked unusually productive because their historical land costs were low. Employee bonuses were excluded from store results and paid from the corporate pool. Strong outlets quietly carried weak ones while the accounting made many weak locations appear defensible in isolation.

Rumelt calls this entropy: not inertia, which is failure to adapt when the world changes, but the loss of order even without a new external shock. Product lines can blur as divisions reach for adjacent price points. Margin can narrow as plausible expenses accumulate. Quality can slip when small defects fail to trigger investigation. None of the decisions need look irrational in isolation; together they can destroy the design.

The same drift appeared at Bridgewater in 2011 after Ray Dalio transferred oversight of client memos. Two senior investment advisers caught minor errors in one memo; with Dalio's prodding, the team investigated and found a wider quality-control breakdown. People had concentrated on completing tasks; the senior staff meant to sample the actual output were occupied elsewhere. Dalio did not attribute the failure to lack of caring. Completion had quietly become a substitute for quality.

Profit follows the pattern. Alex Hormozi relays a billionaire mentor's heuristic that businesses slowly consume their own surplus because people keep finding reasons to spend. This is not a law against investment. Scarce talent, durable brand, and a repaired operation may deserve substantial funding. The danger is a recurring cost added without naming the constraint it removes, the expected return, the accountable owner, or the test for removing it.

Nor is the answer a leader who must personally police every expense. Jim Collins found that discipline imposed by a forceful executive often disappeared with the executive. Abbott Laboratories built a different kind of rigor: every cost, income item, and investment belonged to one accountable person, while managers retained freedom inside a framework. Across Collins's good-to-great companies, stop-doing disciplines removed work that no longer fit so the work that did fit could be fully funded.

Denton's repair combined pruning with cultivation. Five weak outlets closed, while stronger stores supplied practices for layout, merchandising, and selling. After two years, adjusted contribution exceeded $5 million and accounting profit had doubled; roughly half the improvement came from closures and half from transferring better methods.

No breakthrough idea rescued the chain. Management made the whole visible again, then did the unglamorous work of restoring it. A business can lose without being attacked. It can happen when every part defends itself while no one defends the design.
