# Every Admission Changes the Product

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

Alex Hormozi says he has removed members who were paying $3,000 a month from one of his communities. On a subscription dashboard, that looks like throwing away recurring revenue. Inside the room, the arithmetic changes. A member who damages the culture is not simply one bad account. They change the experience every other member is paying to enter.

When someone buys a book, the purchase does not rewrite the copies already sold. A community is different. Each new member can change what behavior feels normal, how much trust the room carries, how much value must come from the host, and which peers are invited next. The customer is also part of the delivery.

That is why “who is here?” belongs beside the curriculum, calls, and tools in the offer. Early on, the host supplies most of the value. In a mature community, members answer one another, model the standard, and attract peers. The product can move from broadcast to network. But that leverage works in reverse when poor-fit members consume attention, weaken trust, participation, and retention.

The first cohort therefore has disproportionate influence. Seth Godin’s smallest viable market is more than a narrow targeting choice. Its early members help establish the culture and determine which peers hear about it next. Repeated admission choices compound by making the next kind of member more or less likely.

This interdependence makes fit part of product design. Retention data cannot show how one member affected another, but it can test whether entry criteria predict who benefits enough to stay. In one AMA example, a service business had 17 percent blended monthly churn. Only six of its forty clients fit a more exact profile. Those clients stayed for more than a year; the others stayed about three months. The average hid two retention patterns. That did not prove why people left. It made qualification a hypothesis worth testing alongside delivery and activation.

None of this justifies exclusivity as theater. Fit should be tied to the promise: the prerequisites needed to benefit, the conduct needed to participate, and the purpose people have gathered to pursue. “Not for this room” need not mean “not good enough.” A new group needs enough participation to become a group; filtering it to emptiness protects nothing. As qualified demand grows beyond real capacity, the bar can rise. Selection is useful when it protects a real experience, not when it performs status.

The next application presents a larger question than whether the applicant will pay. If you admit them, what room will everyone else enter?
