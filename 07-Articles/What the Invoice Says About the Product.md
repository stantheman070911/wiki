# What the Invoice Says About the Product

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

Ignore the total on an invoice for a moment. Read the nouns and dates instead.

Ten consulting hours. Twenty-five software seats. Payment processing: three percent of volume. Monthly access to a course community. Together, they expose two product questions: what unit is the customer buying, and when should its value renew?

This makes the invoice a compact product specification. A landing page can promise an outcome while the billing unit quietly sells something else. An hourly service says time is the product—and earns less on paper as it gets faster at producing the same result. Diagnostic pricing instead scopes the journey from the customer's present condition to a defined result. Time remains a delivery cost, not the unit the buyer is asked to value.

Software makes the same choice. Drawing on Patrick Campbell, Wes Bush distinguishes functional units—users, contacts, videos—from units closer to outcomes, such as views or payments processed. A sound value metric is understandable, tracks value received, and expands as the customer succeeds. Per-user pricing can therefore suppress adoption when another seat raises cost without comparable benefit. Bush treats Slack as a specific exception: collaboration gains value as more people join, and its fair-billing policy charged only for active users.

The date makes a second claim: this value renews now. In an Alex Hormozi AMA, software serving agents who might sell only four houses a year billed monthly. Between transactions, invoices arrived without a fresh occasion for value. Quarterly or annual terms better matched the natural cycle. Longer terms cannot repair a weak product and may delay the churn signal; they can stop an arbitrary calendar from manufacturing cancellation decisions.

Sometimes the value does not renew at all. A skill learned once can be extremely valuable and still make a poor subscription. Hormozi contrasts one-time education with consumable inputs: current deals, timely news, newly tested advertisements. A better-aligned design may charge substantially for the transformation once, then less for the fresh input. Monthly billing cannot turn finite value into continuous value.

A third question belongs beside the unit and date: what evidence shows that value arrived? Bush recommends usage reviews that show work completed or time saved, then restating value when invoicing. His examples include Mailchimp showing emails sent and Wistia showing viewers and watch time. Put the ideas together and the bill can carry evidence from the same period as the charge. An invoice that arrives alone foregrounds cost.

Outcome billing is not always workable. Results may be hard to measure or depend on factors the provider does not control; in those cases, the nearest reliable functional proxy may be more defensible. But remove the price from the invoice and leave its line items, dates, and evidence. Could the customer tell what they bought—and why it is time to buy it again?
