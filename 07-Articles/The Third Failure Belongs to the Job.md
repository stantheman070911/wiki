# The Third Failure Belongs to the Job

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

Not every third failure belongs to the job. By then, however, the job has lost the right to go unexamined.

Peter Drucker proposed a severe rule: if a role defeats two or three people in succession who had performed well in earlier assignments, assume the role is unfit for human beings and redesign it. His point was not leniency. Impossible jobs often look perfectly logical on paper. Such roles are often built around an unusual predecessor and may demand a mixture of temperaments rarely found in one person. Because the job looks logical, the organization keeps trying another proven performer—recruiting for a person who may not exist.

One failure is not enough to establish that pattern. Intel once moved an extremely productive field sales manager into leadership of a factory unit. The jobs were comparable in size and scope, yet his performance deteriorated and he appeared overwhelmed. In time, he learned the unfamiliar environment and returned toward his former level. Andrew Grove's diagnosis was task-relevant maturity: general competence had remained high, but maturity for this particular work had fallen sharply. Low task-relevant maturity calls for precise instruction and monitoring, so the early failure did not establish the manager's ceiling.

Gino Wickman separates the “right person” from the “right seat.” A person may share the organization's values and still not get, want, or have the capacity for a particular role. That is a placement problem. But Wickman insists on drawing the structure before placing names into it. Otherwise affection, the current roster, or the leader's ego quietly writes the job description.

Ray Dalio separates performance trouble into design problems—such as too many responsibilities—and fit or ability problems; the latter may reflect an innate limit or inadequate training. The remedies differ. Train where skill is missing. Reassign where strengths fit another role. Remove someone who lacks an ability a sound role requires. Redesign work that is faulty or, as Drucker warned, demands conflicting kinds of work.

The sequence matters. First state what the role must contribute, who needs that output, and the few results that would show it was useful. Then specify the role's responsibilities, measures, time requirements, and level of support. Only after the seat is visible should the person be judged against it.

When failure recurs, compare patterns across occupants. Did each struggle with a different gap, or did capable people keep breaking at the same seam in the role or its support? Repeated failure does not absolve the individual. It changes the burden of proof. The manager can no longer treat the job description as neutral while treating every new person as the variable.

The third failure may still reveal another bad fit. But hiring a fourth person into an unchanged role is no longer a new experiment. Before replacing the occupant again, make the job prove that it can be done.
