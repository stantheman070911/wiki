# You Are the Guide, Not the Hero

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

Watch what a brand does the moment it gets to introduce itself, and you will see the same reflex almost every time. It reaches for the microphone and starts talking about itself—its history, its craftsmanship, the founder's long climb, the wall of awards. The instinct feels like confidence. It is actually a casting error, and an expensive one.

Every story has a hero, and the hero is the one who is not yet whole. Luke is confused, Katniss is afraid; the hero wants something and cannot yet get it, which is precisely what makes us lean in. The character we trust is not the hero but the guide—the mentor who already made the trip and came back with a map. Donald Miller's point is that your customer is already the hero of their own life, standing at the center of their own story, wanting something and stuck. A brand that grabs for the hero role is competing with its customer for the one part they will never hand over. And people do not rally behind a second hero. They go looking for a guide.

A guide earns the role on two things, and only two. Empathy: *I understand where you are, because I have stood there.* And competence: *I know the way out, and I can show you.* Notice that neither is served by talking about yourself for its own sake. Both point straight back at the customer's problem and the customer's road.

The proof runs in both directions. One of the most successful pieces of advertising ever written—the Wall Street Journal's "Two Young Men," which ran twenty-eight years and drove roughly two billion dollars in subscriptions—tells of two classmates from the same college, one who subscribed and rose, one who did not. The hero is the reader's own better future; the Journal is merely the guide who got him there. Apple, once it stopped reciting processor specs, sold its machines as tools for the creative and the independent—the customer was cast as the one who thinks different, and Apple as the instrument in his hand. Tidal launched the opposite way, leading with a story about paying wealthy artists their due, a plot in which the listener was, at best, the crowd applauding someone else's win. One made the customer the hero. The other asked to be the hero itself.

The stakes are higher than they look, because the hero is the only character who gets to be transformed. People do not really buy the drill; they buy the finished shelf, the pride, the version of themselves who has it handled. Sell a function and you have described a product. Cast the buyer as the hero and you have promised them a transformation—and only a hero is allowed one.

Here the advice seems to double back on itself. The entire founder-and-creator economy tells you the reverse: put your face on it, tell them about the two bankruptcies and the year on the floor, be the hero of your own brand. Is that wrong?

No—but only because of what the story is *for*. Miller, the same man who insists the customer is the hero, also says heroes use their pain while villains are used by it. Your hard chapter is not told so the audience will admire that you survived. It is told to earn the two things a guide needs: that you have stood where they stand, and that you found the way through. Framed that way, your origin story is not you seizing the hero's part—it is you laying down your credentials as a guide. The struggle is the résumé, not the point.

Which leaves one unsentimental test for any story you tell about yourself. A hero's story ends with the hero—his triumph, his vindication, the applause. A guide's story ends with the person it was told to—with a road they can now see and walk. So watch where your story lands. If it finishes on you, you have quietly taken the cape back, and the customer, feeling it, will drift off to find someone else to follow. If it finishes on them, you have done the one generous thing that also happens to work: you have handed the cape to the only person who was ever going to wear it.
