# Growth Can Starve a Profitable Business

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

A business can earn a positive margin on every sale and still run out of money by selling too quickly.

Suppose each order costs $70 to acquire and fulfill, and the customer pays $100 sixty days later. One completed sale contributes $30 before overhead, tax, and financing costs. But one hundred orders begun before the first payment arrives require $7,000 in cash; one thousand require $70,000. Demand and contribution margin have not deteriorated. Growth has simply outrun collection.

This is the missing dimension in otherwise healthy unit economics. Customer acquisition cost and lifetime gross profit tell you whether a customer's eventual gross profit exceeds the cost to acquire them. Payback period tells you when those acquisition dollars return. Sabri Suby describes the danger as a cash trough: a business can spend $100 to acquire $300 of lifetime value and still run short because the $300 arrives in installments. The ratio records the eventual return; it omits the sequence. The bank account must survive that sequence.

Physical inventory makes that sequence visible. Alex Hormozi gives the example of a merchant with ten units, each producing $10 of profit. Even if demand far exceeds those ten units, the merchant can only fund the next batch from cash already available. There is nothing wrong with the product, price, or demand. Growth remains governed by the free cash the last cycle released—and by how far ahead the next inventory must be ordered.

A subscription can hide the same constraint. In another Hormozi example, a customer costs $30 to acquire and produces $10 of monthly gross profit. The lifetime economics work, but month one opens a $20 gap. An immediate upsell adds $20 of average gross profit, closing that gap within thirty days and allowing the acquisition cash to be recycled. The important idea is not the upsell itself. It is changing the order of cash: collect earlier, delay discretionary outflows, shorten the operating cycle, or deliberately finance the interval.

Earlier cash carries its own warning. Hormozi cautions that prepaid offers generate cash quickly but must be managed so the business can still deliver what it promised. The cash may be in the bank; it is not the same as earned profit or cash free of future obligations.

At company scale, Paul Graham turns the cash calendar into a survival test: hold expenses constant, extend recent revenue growth, and ask whether cash lasts until profitability. If not, the company is “default dead.” Slow growth plus too little time to change course creates the “fatal pinch.” Another funding round remains uncertain; Graham advises writing down the fallback and the date it must begin.

The practical model is a calendar, not another ratio. For one customer cohort, mark every acquisition, inventory, and fulfillment payment, then every collection. Calculate the deepest cumulative cash deficit and the day it returns to zero. Stress-test larger cohorts under conservative assumptions for acquisition cost, fulfillment timing, capacity, refunds, and collections. If the projected trough expands faster than the reserve, more demand is not yet the remedy.

A business can show profit on every customer and still die between payments. Until the cash cycle changes, growth scales the trough.
