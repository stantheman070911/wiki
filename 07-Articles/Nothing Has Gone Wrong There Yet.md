# Nothing Has Gone Wrong There Yet

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

Elise opened a deli because she loved imagining one. The counter, the menu, the room — designing it was a genuine pleasure. Then she owned it, and found she disliked operating it. Bill Burnett and Dave Evans note what would have settled the question in advance: a few shifts in somebody else's deli, which would have separated her enthusiasm for creating a deli from the daily work of running one.

Her error was not optimism. It was comparing two things she knew unequally well.

Whatever you are currently inside shows you all of itself. Every friction, every unglamorous repair, every person who is hard to work with. The alternative shows you only the part you have imagined, and imagination is a poor simulator of Tuesday morning. Alex Hormozi's phrasing for this is precise: new ideas start in uninformed optimism, while an existing business exposes its real friction and therefore feels less attractive *precisely because you know more about it*. The gap you feel between here and there is part genuine difference and part unequal information, and the two are almost impossible to tell apart from the inside.

The pattern is easiest to see in other people. A hair-extensions founder wants to build a software product. A CFO-advisory owner wants to launch a course instead of fixing retention in the practice she has. An e-commerce operator running four capped brands goes looking for a fifth. In each case a specific, addressable constraint — churn, supply, retention — was about to be traded for an unknown set of new ones, and what made the new idea appealing was mostly the absence of a known problem. Sabri Suby's version of the diagnosis is that businesses more often die of indigestion than of starvation.

There is also a price, and it falls due before any of the benefit arrives. Hormozi's estimate from his own operating history is that any change to a working process costs roughly twenty percent in the short term while people relearn — even when the change is genuinely better — against an improvement he was typically hoping would be five. So he set a bar: no change unless it clears a risk-adjusted twenty percent, meaning something like a fifty percent chance of a forty percent gain. Everything below the bar goes on a list. It is the same asymmetry expressed in money. The cost of switching is nearly certain; the gain is still a guess.

None of which tells you to stay. It tells you that the desire to leave is not evidence, and raises the question of what would be.

Hormozi's answer is narrow on purpose. Pivot when a founding assumption has been disproven by your actual activity. If every original thesis still holds and the work is merely slower and harder than you wanted, that is not disproof — that is the standard cost of building, and he thinks the more common error runs the other way: "There are mistakes of picking the wrong path, but there are more mistakes of abandoning the right path." Napoleon Hill drew a related line a century earlier from the opposite direction. When something fails, the failure indicts the plan, not the purpose. Hold the destination; treat the vehicle as disposable. Between them they cover the two ways people misread a hard season — as proof the goal was wrong, or as proof nothing needs to change.

And sometimes leaving is simply correct. One founder ran a commercial construction company and had already started an elevator business with better margins and recurring revenue. Both were operating; both had numbers. Hormozi's advice there inverted — count opportunity cost rather than sunk cost, and be willing to exit the smaller game, because the founder is the asset and the skills transfer into the better vehicle. Notice what made that judgment available. He was not comparing a diary to a brochure. He had read both.

Which suggests the actual move, and it is not patience. It is making the unknown cheaper to know. Name the one uncertainty that would decide the question, find the smallest experience that exposes it, and set a time and money budget before you start so sunk cost cannot quietly redefine what you were testing. Elise's few shifts. Then run the same test on where you already are, since the lightest change is usually a redesign in place: Sarah, drained by budgets and scheduling, pitched a partnership with Production Engineering in terms of her manager's outcomes — faster code, fewer handoff problems — and won a three-month trial that permanently moved the administrative work off her desk. Garth learned on his second day that his new job was far worse than advertised, made it workable for eighteen months with scheduled energy breaks and deliberate friendships in Sales, and left on those relationships with his record intact.

Some situations are excluded from all of this. Abusive, unsafe, discriminatory, or illegal ones are not prototypes to run or jobs to redesign; they are places to plan an exit from. And a market can be genuinely capped, in which case the constraint really is structural and no amount of fixing will move it.

But those are verdicts, and most people reaching for the exit have not yet gathered enough to render one. They have a real account of where they are and a sketch of where they might go, and the sketch is winning because nothing has gone wrong in it yet. The useful question is not whether the other thing is better. It is how much of what you believe about it you have actually seen.
