# You Get Bored Before They Remember

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

Ries and Trout recount that Avis lost money for thirteen consecutive years. Then it accepted its position behind Hertz in one sentence: “Avis is only No. 2 in rent-a-cars, so why go with us? We try harder.” The company reported profits for the next three years. Shortly after Avis was sold to ITT, it ran ads proclaiming, “Avis is going to be No. 1.” Ries and Trout argue that the old association persisted: after several later campaigns, they still expected readers to recall the No. 2 line. The message had changed; in their telling, the mental position had not.

The temptation is structural. A team lives inside its own message. It sees the early drafts, debates every word, reviews the designs, sits through the presentations, and watches the campaign reports. By the time an audience member encounters the finished idea, the people who made it may already have encountered it repeatedly. Internal boredom can therefore be mistaken for external saturation, even though the two groups have very different exposure histories.

Seth Godin argues that people retain what they rehearse and see repeatedly. Familiarity can turn a coherent story from random noise into something recognizable, and recognition can support trust. He calls attention to the dangerous interval between the moment the sender grows bored and the moment the message begins to register with its audience. Godin warns that projects are often abandoned inside that interval. A new message may relieve the team while interrupting the recognition the old one had begun to build.

Consistency does not require saying the same sentence forever. Alex Hormozi's practical answer is to preserve the idea while changing its surface: tell it through a new customer, medium, story, or application. A principle explained in a book, demonstrated in a case, discussed in a conversation, and applied to a new setting need not create four different positions. It can give one position more ways to be understood.

Jonah Berger adds a different mechanism. Immediate word of mouth and ongoing word of mouth are different problems. Ideas stay accessible when ordinary cues bring them back to mind, so a durable message can build a habitat in the audience's life: a recurring sight, moment, phrase, or routine that makes the idea relevant again after launch day. Interesting content may win an immediate mention; a recurring cue can help generate later ones.

The useful distinction is between the core and its expression. The core is the association or promise you intend to earn. The expression is the changing supply of proof, stories, formats, and contexts that keeps the promise alive. Refresh the expression when it feels stale. Reconsider the core for evidence-based reasons—because the promise is false, the audience consistently interprets it differently, or the position is no longer strategically worth owning—not because the team is bored.

Frequency cannot rescue a weak product or a broken promise; it may simply make the mismatch more familiar. But boredom inside the building proves none of those things. The hard judgment is deciding whether a familiar message has stopped working or has only begun to work. Boredom cannot answer it.
