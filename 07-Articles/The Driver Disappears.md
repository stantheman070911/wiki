# The Driver Disappears

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

During his college years parking cars at a Los Angeles hotel, Morgan Housel watched Ferraris, Lamborghinis, and Rolls-Royces arrive at the curb. He wanted one because of what such a car seemed to say: the driver had succeeded; the driver mattered. Yet when Housel stared, he was scarcely thinking about the person who owned it. He was imagining himself behind the wheel—and what other people would then think of him.

The signal had arrived. The admiration had been rerouted.

That distinction matters because status symbols are not meaningless. Jonah Berger's account of social currency rests on a simple observation: visible choices help people infer identity. Cars, clothes, badges, and insider access can make taste, achievement, or membership legible. In an analysis of 1.5 million car sales, Berger and his colleagues found that social influence was stronger in cities where vehicles were easier to observe. A car can be an effective advertisement even when it is a poor biography.

Classification is not regard. The owner experiences a purchase from inside, with whatever history and meaning it carries. A passerby may see only the surface and route it through private concerns. A glance that feels like “They admire me” may mean only “I want that” or “What would people think of me if I had it?” The object stays in the center of the frame while the person drops out.

Marcus Aurelius reduced the reputational audience by widening the view. The whole earth is a point; its inhabited portion is smaller; within that, how many people will commend you, and how stable are their judgments? This is not a command to disregard everyone. One practical reading is to name the audience: whose regard are you trying to earn, and what do those people actually respect?

Dale Carnegie approached the same self-absorption from ground level. His practical observation was that people make friends by becoming genuinely interested in others, not by trying to make others interested in them. At a dinner, a botanist judged Carnegie a compelling conversationalist even though Carnegie had said hardly anything and listened intently. Sincere attention supplied what display cannot reliably provide: the experience of mattering.

That leaves a status object with several honest jobs. It may offer beauty, craft, pleasure, utility, or a precise signal to a particular group. Trouble begins when it is assigned a relationship to perform.

At the curb, the driver steps out while the valet imagines himself getting in. If the object would remain worth owning after you knew that this might be the spectator's entire response, then perhaps you want the object. If not, what are you trying to buy?
