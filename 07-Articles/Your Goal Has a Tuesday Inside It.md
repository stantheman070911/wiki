# Your Goal Has a Tuesday Inside It

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

Michael was preparing to abandon civil engineering. He knew the job made him unhappy, but his diagnosis was no more precise than that: perhaps engineering had failed him, or he had failed engineering. He considered finance and business school—large solutions aimed at a problem he had not yet located.

For several weeks, life-design teachers Bill Burnett and Dave Evans asked him to record the moments when he felt engaged or depleted and the exact activity he was doing at the time. The pattern was narrower than his job title. Difficult technical problems energized him. Proposal writing, fee negotiations, administrative work, and difficult personalities drained him. Michael eventually entered a PhD program and found a role centered on complex civil and structural engineering, mostly working alone.

Michael had been choosing among labels when the useful evidence lived in verbs.

Ambitions invite the same mistake. “Build a company,” “make partner,” and “retire early” are visible destinations, easy to admire from a distance. Each also contains an ordinary calendar: who controls nine o’clock, which problems recur, how often attention is interrupted, who is nearby, and what energy remains at dinner. The milestone may last an afternoon. Its Tuesday can repeat for years.

Michael Gerber makes this sequence explicit. Define the life first—what you value, how you want it to feel, and who you wish to be—then define what the business must do to support it. The business succeeds only if it serves the life it was built to support. Morgan Housel reaches the same point through money. He describes control over time as wealth’s highest dividend. During an investment-banking internship, he found the work stimulating and still quit after one month because every waking second belonged to someone else’s schedule. Enjoyable work without control can become an intolerable day.

The daily level matters for meaning as well as happiness. Burnett and Evans argue that abstract goods become available only through particulars: beauty in a birthday cake, honesty in one difficult conversation, unity in a family deciding how to spend a weekend. Meaning cannot be stored inside the achievement; whatever meaning the goal contributes must be available in the particular moments of living it.

Before committing to a goal, describe one uneventful Tuesday after you have attained it. Leave out the launch, promotion, sale, and holiday. Write what you do, with whom, under whose control, and how the day affects your energy. Then compare that imagined Tuesday with several weeks of evidence from your current activities. If the future work is unfamiliar, test the picture by speaking with or shadowing someone already living that schedule.

Michael did not need a more impressive answer to “What should I become?” He needed a more exact answer to “What work is worth repeating?” The PhD came after that answer, not before it.
