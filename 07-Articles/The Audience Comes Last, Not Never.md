# The Audience Comes Last, Not Never

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

The audience can test a promise. It cannot supply a purpose.

That distinction surfaced in an interview with Steve Blank. The host offered a theory of artists: the purest create for themselves, and the audience comes last. Blank pushed back. Work may begin in private conviction, but art meant to be shared must eventually meet another person. He drew the same boundary around startups. Customer development, he argued, is not a giant focus group or a spreadsheet of feature requests. It informs a vision and checks whether the founder is hallucinating.

“Last,” in this sense, describes authority rather than chronology. Potential customers may be present from the earliest test. Their evidence can pressure-test the assumptions inside a vision; responsibility for the vision still belongs to the maker.

Before public results arrive, the work needs an internal judge. Paul Graham treats taste not as unexamined preference but as a capacity that improves through studying excellent work, understanding why it works, solving the right problem, and revising. Rick Rubin places creative success at the moment the maker has brought a work to the highest standard they can recognize and is ready to release it. If a maker cannot say what good means until the metrics arrive, they are renting a standard.

That internal standard still needs the correct yardstick. Bill Burnett and Dave Evans distinguish three returns a work can seek: money, impact, and expression. An expressive work is not necessarily worthless because it did not sell. A commercial product cannot claim success merely because its maker loves it. Most work mixes the three returns, but leaving the mixture unnamed allows every number to become a verdict on everything.

Once a work promises to change something for other people, their experience gains legitimate authority. Seth Godin asks: Who is it for? What is it for? The second question names the intended change. A coherent tool that nobody can use may have passed an internal review while failing the service it claimed to provide.

Even then, feedback requires diagnosis. Sales can test willingness to pay; completed tasks can test utility; comprehension can test clarity. None alone settles artistic quality. Rubin also argues that timing, distribution, cultural mood, competing releases, and prior expectations can affect reception. A weak result may therefore point to the craft, the audience, the promise, or the conditions around release. A strong result can be just as ambiguous.

Before release, name the internal standard and the external promise. The audience reports what happened when the work met the world. Deciding what that result is evidence of remains part of making it.
