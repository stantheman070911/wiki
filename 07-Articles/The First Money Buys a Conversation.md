# The First Money Buys a Conversation

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

JL Collins recounts that, at twenty-five, he was earning $10,000 a year and had saved $5,000 over two years. He liked his first professional job, but wanted four months of unpaid leave to travel through Europe. His boss said no. Collins assumed that ended the matter: an employee asked, an employer answered.

He went home, thought for about a week, and resigned. Then the answer changed. His boss asked him not to do anything rash, spoke with the owner, and returned with six weeks of leave. Collins also negotiated a month of annual vacation going forward.

The $5,000 eventually helped fund the trip, but it did its first important work before Collins spent it. It made the cost of hearing no survivable. Once he could accept the loss of the job, a fixed condition became a conversation.

Negotiator Chris Voss treats leverage as fluid: it shifts toward the party able to withhold a desired gain or absorb a feared loss. Collins did not make a louder request or discover a better argument. Because he could absorb losing the job, he made his alternative credible.

The same threshold appears at company scale. Paul Graham calls it ramen profitability: enough revenue to cover the founders' basic living costs. He recalls Airbnb aiming for $4,000 a month—$3,500 for rent and $500 for food. That would not make the company large. It would let the founders continue without an investor's permission. Funding could move from necessary to optional, changing the negotiation before the company reached conventional success.

Financial independence is often discussed as one distant threshold: enough assets to stop working forever. These cases reveal an earlier one. Enough to retire may end dependence on a paycheck. A smaller liquid reserve can make one particular dependence negotiable.

The useful target is therefore personal, not ceremonial: the essential costs between a credible no and the next realistic option. Each recurring obligation raises that number. Each liquid dollar above near-term needs lengthens the time available to find an acceptable answer.

This is not an argument for theatrical defiance. Bill Burnett and Dave Evans advise testing whether work can be redesigned before leaving it, where conditions are safe—by reframing the role, changing its tasks, or moving internally. Collins liked his job, considered the consequence for a week, and accepted a compromise. The buffer improved the decision because it reduced urgency, not because it rewarded impulsiveness.

The revealing question for a first savings target is not only, “When can I retire?” It is, “Which conversation can I not have because I cannot survive the answer?” Collins's first useful fortune did not buy the rest of his life. It bought six weeks—and changed what he understood work to be.
