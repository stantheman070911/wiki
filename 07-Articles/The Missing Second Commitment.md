# The Missing Second Commitment

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

At MyFitnessPal, marketing had a top-priority goal for personalized email: drive a minimum number of monthly active users to its blog. One key result was increasing email click-throughs. It required engineering work, but engineering had already set its own quarterly priorities, and nobody had made the dependency explicit. By the time the company learned this at its postmortem, the project was already a quarter late.

Nothing about that failure looks dramatic. No explicit conflict had occurred; marketing simply had not informed engineering. The plan asked for work engineering had not included in its quarterly priorities.

This is the quiet defect in much cross-functional work. One team writes an objective and treats another team's attention as a given. The recipient hears a request, perhaps agrees in the meeting, then returns to a different stack of obligations. Everyone leaves with an impression of alignment; nobody has made the same promise.

A cross-team plan needs two commitments. The first belongs to the accountable owner: the outcome, the measure of success, and the date. The second belongs to each contributing team: the specific result it will provide, the person responsible, the capacity it will reserve, and the point at which it must say that the agreement no longer holds. The second commitment is not co-ownership. MyFitnessPal kept a single owner for every OKR precisely to prevent accountability from dissolving into mutual blame. It is an explicit acceptance of a contribution.

That distinction is one element of what separates a target from a strategy. Richard Rumelt defines coherent action as coordinated policies, resource commitments, and actions that carry a chosen policy into the world. A launch plan that quietly assumes another department's time may contain an outcome and a wish, but it lacks a resource commitment. The missing item is not administrative detail. It is part of the action itself.

Peter Drucker makes the same point from the other side of the handoff. A specialist's output is only a fragment until another specialist can use it. The producer therefore has to learn who needs the work, what they need to understand, and in what form and time frame they can use it. In other words, neither side gets to call the connection someone else's coordination problem. The requester must make the needed contribution clear; the contributor must say what can actually be delivered.

Meetings can begin this work, but they cannot substitute for it. Andrew Grove treated meetings as a medium for managerial work, and showed why early clarification has unusual leverage: defining information, responsibilities, and milestones before work begins prevents ambiguity from spreading through many people's work over time. A meeting that ends with only verbal assent has spent the leverage without capturing it.

The record can be short. For every dependency, write the outcome owner, the required contribution, the contributor's named owner and timing, and the signal that forces a renegotiation. Then check that the contributing team has recorded the same commitment in its own plan.

If that second record does not exist, do not call it a dependency. Call it an assumption. The project has not begun yet.
