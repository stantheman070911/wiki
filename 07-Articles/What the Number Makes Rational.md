# What the Number Makes Rational

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

While arguing for a new YouTube metric, Cristos Goodrow posed a choice. A one-minute video teaches someone to tie a bow tie quickly and precisely. A ten-minute version is entertaining, but may not teach the knot at all. Which should rank first?

The answer depended on the number. Google Search favored the efficient answer. YouTube decided its job was engagement, replaced views with watch time as its core measure, and in 2012 launched a watch-time-optimized recommendation system. The metric did not merely report what users watched. It changed what the product considered worth showing.

A target is a compressed instruction. Reviewed repeatedly, it tells people where to spend attention and ingenuity. Andrew Grove's bicycle image is exact: you tend to steer toward what you watch.

YouTube later set a target of one billion hours of daily watch time by the end of 2016. Changes were evaluated for their effect on that number; engineers eventually searched for improvements worth as little as 0.2 percent. The target concentrated years of work and was reached ahead of schedule. It also made particular trade-offs locally rational. Extending existing viewers' sessions was easier than attracting new viewers, even though YouTube's research showed more growth potential in enlarging the user base. A single watch-time total could hide the difference. The number was useful precisely because it narrowed the field. That narrowing was also its risk.

Grove's remedy for a one-sided measure is to pair it with a countermeasure that reveals the primary target's shadow. Monitor inventory alone and people will drive it down until shortages appear; pair inventory with shortage incidence. Count vouchers processed and pair the total with errors. If a number can improve while the intended value deteriorates, it is unsafe by itself.

No pairing makes a score self-interpreting. The same number produces different behavior when used for learning and when used mechanically to determine pay. John Doerr warns that tying goal scores directly to compensation encourages people to sandbag: easier promises become safer than honest ambition. Grove likewise treats objectives as a way for people to pace their work, not as a legal contract that replaces judgment.

Before putting a target into circulation, imagine a capable person pursuing it exactly as written. What will they do more of? What can worsen while the number rises? Which paired measure would expose that damage? Then decide whether the score is meant to guide a conversation or settle a verdict.

YouTube eventually widened its view beyond watch time to include satisfaction and social responsibility. The earlier metric had not become false. The product had outgrown what it made rational.
