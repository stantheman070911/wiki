# Everything You Can't Stop Is Working

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

In 1980, Continental Airlines was still running a piece of software called the Boeing Fleet Planner. It took costs, added a markup, and produced the fares the airline expected to charge. Two years into deregulation it was still reporting that long-haul fares had to rise. They fell twenty-seven percent.

The tool was not broken. It had never been a forecasting tool. It was built for negotiating with a price regulator, back when fares genuinely were cost plus a markup, and at that job it worked. What had changed was the world it was answering. Nobody at Continental caught it, because — as Richard Rumelt argues — a routine does not only shape what an organization does. It shapes what the organization can see. The planner kept producing numbers, and the numbers kept looking like forecasts.

That is the general form of the thing, and it runs at every scale. A behavior that looks irrational is usually a competent answer to a question that is no longer being asked, or to one nobody wants to say out loud.

Carl Jung fell and hit his head as a schoolboy, and had a thought: maybe now I won't have to go back. Fainting spells followed — uncontrollable, and entirely real. He later described neuroses as "substitutes for legitimate suffering." The symptom does the work the person cannot face doing directly. Brianna Wiest builds her whole account of self-sabotage on that observation, stated flatly: if something is still in your life, some part of you wants it there. Disorganization keeps you permanently a few tasks away from having to start. Being busy is distraction wearing the costume of virtue. Perfectionism spares you the repetitions in which you would find out how good you actually are. None of these are failures of will. They are working. That is precisely why they persist.

Which explains why the standard remedy fails. *Stop doing it* is not an executable instruction. The cue survives, the need survives, and suppression keeps the behavior cognitively live — you spend the day not thinking about it, which is a way of thinking about it. Steven Bartlett's father smoked only in the car. What ended it was not resolve but a swap: the reaching motion stayed, the container stayed, and lollipops went where the cigarettes had been.

There is a second reason removal fails, and it is less comfortable. People defend these behaviors. Graham Weaver spent decades treating a harsh internal voice as the engine of his ambition. During the worst loss his firm ever took, that voice ran continuously and produced no idea, no solution, no usable advice — only fear, wearing a coach's whistle. He could see it plainly and still not let it go, because he credited it with his drive. Napoleon Hill noticed the same protectiveness around excuses: people defend their alibis because they made them, and it is human nature to defend what you built.

So the question that opens the problem is not *how do I stop this.* It is *what is this doing for me, and what would do it better.* Wiest's distinction is the one to keep: the strategy may be broken, but the need underneath it usually isn't. The need for security is not greed. The need to be recognized for your work is not vanity. Granting the need outright is what makes it possible to fire the mechanism serving it badly — because you are no longer being asked to give something up for nothing.

The framing sounds like an excuse and is the opposite of one. *I lack discipline* is a verdict with nothing attached to it. *This is buying me something, and I can name what* is a specification. Leila Hormozi's line holds the distinction in place: it's not your fault, but it is still your problem.

One caution from Rumelt, because the principle cuts both ways. Some stuckness is not stuckness. The regional phone companies were slow to sell cheap DSL into markets where they were still selling expensive T1 lines, and that was arithmetic rather than blindness — their most valuable customers were the inert ones. The behavior was profitable right until it wasn't, and when it turned, it turned fast, taking down every competitor who had built a business on the gap staying open.

Worth holding next to your own list. Whatever you can't stop is paying you something. The question is how much longer it can keep up the payments.
