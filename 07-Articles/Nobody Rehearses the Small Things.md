# Nobody Rehearses the Small Things

> **Up:** [[Home|Home]] → [[07-Articles/Articles Index|Articles]]

**Article of the day · ~2 min read**

In 1969 Jeb Magruder came out of an interview with Bob Haldeman impressed by the man's honesty and sharpness. He also watched Haldeman discover that no golf cart was available and turn near-hysterical, insulting the staff over it. Magruder wanted the job, so he treated the interview as the reading and the outburst as a bad moment. It cost him later.

Both scenes were real. Only one of them had been prepared.

Robert Greene's account of how character forms explains why that asymmetry matters. Disposition, early attachment, and the habits of youth lay down a pattern, and then adolescence adds a fourth layer: disguise. Once people notice their own flaws they learn to cover them, so the timid present as outgoing, brimming self-confidence turns out to be an arrogance that cannot listen, and frankness turns out to be boorishness. An interview asks someone to operate that fourth layer for an hour, in a setting they have prepared for, on the subject they know best — their own history. We then read the output as a forecast of the next five years.

Worse, the traits under the heaviest cover are the ones that will end up governing the relationship. Greene's observation is that the weak parts of a character are the durable parts, because they require no maintenance. Generosity, empathy, and resilience have to be practised into habits, and ordinary life erodes them. Compulsive behaviour needs no upkeep at all, which is why people never do a thing only once. They will explain it as losing their head in the moment, and then they will do it again, sometimes against their own interests.

The instinct at this point is to look harder — sharper questions, a longer process, a better read on the room. But polish is precisely the channel that improves under scrutiny. Taleb's hierarchy of evidence puts durable outcomes first, then repeated behaviour with retained downside, then auditable work samples, and places confidence, vocabulary, and presentation at the bottom: useful for screening, never grounds for overruling anything above them. The correction is not a more penetrating gaze. It is a different sample.

Four samples are cheap and largely unrehearsed.

**The small matters.** Someone late on trivial assignments is late on large ones; someone irritated by minor inconvenience tends to come apart under real pressure. Nobody maintains a persona through a fifteen-minute delay.

**The people who cannot affect the outcome.** The golf cart is the archetype — the gap between the presented self and the treatment of anyone with no leverage in the room.

**Power, issued in bounded amounts.** Lincoln's line, "If you want to test a man's character, give him power," describes a measuring device rather than a warning. Lyndon Johnson, as Senate majority leader, made a point of speaking only to the assistants of senators who had crossed him, and would walk off the floor during their speeches so that others followed. Power did not make him that way; it removed his reason to hide it. Which turns a promotion into an instrument, and argues for handing over responsibility in a bounded form before handing it over entirely.

**The finished record.** Greene's Big Talker arrives with impressive ideas and needs a backer; the tell is that nothing in the history was ever completed, and that he withdraws once you are committed. The plainest question in the whole business is what this person has actually finished.

Two deliberate probes belong alongside the four: criticise directly and see whether the criticism gets used or resented, and hand over an assignment with less direction than usual and watch how the thinking gets organised.

None of this makes anyone a reliable reader of people, and the failure mode deserves naming. Othello reads Desdemona's nervousness under questioning as guilt, when the nervousness was produced by his own paranoid interrogation; the error is in the decoding, not the observing. In 1894 Alfred Dreyfus answered questioning in the calm, efficient register of his bureaucratic training, and a public convinced that innocence protests loudly read his composure as proof of the opposite. Blandness is a mask too — Madoff seemed so unremarkable that suspicion never landed on him. A single observation is noise. Ray Dalio's discipline here is arithmetic: build the judgement upward from specific observations, refuse to over-squeeze any one of them, and score the swing rather than the shot, the way you would judge a poker player across many hands instead of one night's results.

The reason to accept this much inconvenience is a cost asymmetry. Values sit at the top of Dalio's screening order because they are close to fixed, while skills are the cheapest gap to close after hiring — which means a values gap has to be caught before the offer goes out. Greene's version is blunter: weak character neutralises every other quality, so a less charming and less clever person of strong character outperforms the impressive one over any long horizon. Jim Collins found the same pattern at the top of companies. Every good-to-great transition was led by someone personally modest and professionally implacable, while the celebrity leaders clustered in the firms whose gains did not hold.

So an interview cannot tell you what someone is like. It can tell you what they would like you to conclude, which is real information about the performance and almost none about the person. The rest is available, but only where nobody is performing: the small delay, the colleague with no leverage, the bounded piece of authority, the list of things genuinely finished. Haldeman handed Magruder both readings on the same day. He kept the one that had been arranged for him.
