# The Principles of Making an Offer

*A dated synthesis generated from the wiki, condensing THE WIKI's offer-construction entries against Alex Hormozi's [[06-Source-Library/Books/2026-07-18_Book_AlexHormozi_100MOffers_RawTranscript|$100M Offers]] archive. Synthesized 2026-08-15.*

## Executive summary

An offer is not a price on a product — it is an engineered package designed so that price stops being the buyer's deciding factor. THE WIKI's offer material, anchored in Hormozi's *$100M Offers* and cross-checked against Sabri Suby's parallel "Godfather Offer" framework, converges on one mechanism: **maximize the buyer's perceived value while minimizing their perceived risk, effort, and delay — then wrap it in scarcity, urgency, bonuses, and a guarantee so the comparison to a competitor becomes structurally impossible.**

The findings below compress that mechanism into a build order: choose the market before touching the offer, run the four-step construction procedure (dream outcome → problems → solutions → delivery vehicles), trim and stack what you generated, price toward the point of resistance rather than away from it, then layer the four enhancers — scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees — and name the result. A second track covers what happens *around* the single offer: how it's sequenced against upsells and continuity, how price is framed and defended, and when the offer needs a salesperson at all.

This report does not replace the entries it draws from — treat it as the fast-read version, and follow any link below back to its source entry for full tactics, examples, and numbers.

## Findings

### 1. The hierarchy: market beats offer beats persuasion

Before engineering anything, verify the market. Hormozi's ordering — **Market > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skill** — means a brilliant offer in a weak market still loses, while a mediocre offer in a starving market still sells. Score a market on four criteria: massive pain, purchasing power, ease of targeting, and growth trajectory. The three evergreen categories are health, wealth, and relationships. Niching down within a strong market — the same program repackaged for a specific sub-audience — routinely multiplies both price and close rate for businesses under $10M/year.
→ [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/The Value Equation and the Grand Slam Offer|The Value Equation and the Grand Slam Offer]]

### 2. The Value Equation is the master formula

Every offer-construction tactic below is, mechanically, a way to move one of four variables:

```
Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) / (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)
```

Increase the numerator by selling status and transformation, not features, and by stacking proof (case studies, guarantees, social proof) that raises perceived likelihood. Decrease the denominator by engineering quick early wins and by making delivery done-for-you rather than effortful. The harder, more defensible move — and the one beginners skip — is compressing the denominator rather than inflating the numerator: reducing *perceived* wait or effort (a countdown clock, a template, automation) usually beats a bigger promise. Perception moves the equation as much as reality does.
→ [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/The Value Equation and the Grand Slam Offer|The Value Equation and the Grand Slam Offer]]

### 3. The construction procedure: four steps, divergent before convergent

Building the offer itself is a repeatable procedure, not a flash of inspiration. It requires **divergent thinking** (many unknowns, many answers, generate abundantly) before **convergent thinking** (cut to the best few):

1. **Identify the dream outcome** — the destination, not the vehicle. State it with a compressed timeframe.
2. **List every problem** between the buyer and that outcome, sorted against the four value-equation drivers (won't be worth it / won't work for me / too hard / takes too long). Aim for 30–60+ problems — volume is the asset. Every unsolved problem is a lost sale.
3. **Turn each problem into a solution** — mechanically reverse it into "how to [solve it]" language.
4. **Create delivery vehicles** for each solution using the delivery cube: vary *personalization* (1:1 → small group → one-to-many), *effort absorbed* (DIY → done-with-you → done-for-you), medium, and speed/access. The same solution generates many delivery options at different costs and perceived values.

Then **trim and stack**: remove high-cost/low-perceived-value vehicles, keep high-value/low-cost ones, and bundle the survivors into one named package. The construction test: *"What could you do that someone would immediately say, 'All that? Seriously? Yes, I'm in.'"* For a first offer, deliberately overdeliver — even unscalably — to generate sales and feedback; tighten fulfillment only after the offer converts reliably (**create flow → monetize flow → then add friction**).
→ [[03-Tactics-and-Playbooks/Sales-and-Lead-Generation/Engineering a Grand Slam Offer - Problems, Solutions, Trim and Stack|Engineering a Grand Slam Offer]]

### 4. A convergent second method: the Godfather Offer

Suby's independently-derived framework reaches the same destination — value so far above price that comparison breaks down — by a different route: write the most ridiculous, over-the-top version of the offer *first*, before worrying about delivery cost, then negotiate it back to something deliverable. Its seven parts are rationale (a credible "why does this deal exist"), value build (stack against the market's normal price, then anchor with a visible drop), a first sale priced to barely profit or lose money (the backend, not sale one, is where margin lives), payment plans, premiums, a power guarantee, and genuine scarcity. The internal tell of a correctly-built offer is the founder's own "how am I going to deliver on this?" — if it doesn't unsettle the seller, it isn't strong enough to convert the buyer.
→ [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/The Godfather Offer - A Seven-Part Framework for an Irresistible Offer|The Godfather Offer]]

### 5. Price toward resistance, not away from it

Raise price until roughly 20% of prospects object — that friction point is a pricing signal, not a reason to retreat. Higher-paying clients pay more attention, get better results, and produce better testimonials, which justify pricing higher still. This is reinforced by a second, independent framework: **barbell pricing**. Extremes win — sell very expensive to a select few, or very cheap to everyone — and the middle is where businesses die. The counterintuitive early move is to sell your own time 1:1 at a premium, unscalably, because the cash and the anchor it creates fund everything downstream; even buyers who never take the premium absorb its value by association when it's stated plainly and confronted, not softened.
→ [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/The Value Equation and the Grand Slam Offer|The Value Equation and the Grand Slam Offer]], [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/Barbell Pricing and the Unscalable Premium Offer|Barbell Pricing and the Unscalable Premium Offer]]

### 6. Decommoditize before you discount

If the buyer perceives two offers as identical, the cheaper one wins by default — so before touching price, make the difference in outcome legible. List everything that makes the category painful, risky, slow, or annoying; build (and market) the offer around removing those specific pains in plain buyer language, not technical or process language. When a category is easy to compare, differentiate on at least one of three vectors — **faster, less risky, or easier** — and fund that operational difference with the higher price it earns, rather than claiming a difference the delivery doesn't back up.
→ [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/Decommoditize Before You Discount - Make the Difference Obvious|Decommoditize Before You Discount]]

### 7. Lead with a defined end, not an open-ended relationship

Nobody buys "a lifestyle" or "ongoing coaching"; they buy a 6-week challenge, a 21-day program, a specific score. Package the front end as a concrete, time-bound result — the thing the buyer *wants* — and use the trust and momentum earned inside that program to sell the ongoing thing they actually *need*. Timing is part of the offer itself: the right offer pitched at the wrong moment in the buyer's journey is functionally the wrong offer, which is why an overpriced or unproven core offer often converts better once a smaller, defined-end step (an audit, a diagnostic, a workshop) is inserted ahead of it.
→ [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/Give Them What They Want to Get Them What They Need - The Defined-End Offer|The Defined-End Offer]], [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/The Paid Front Door - Warm Cold Traffic Before the Core Offer|The Paid Front Door]]

### 8. The four enhancers stack on top of the core offer

Once the core offer is built, four psychological layers compound its pull without changing the underlying deliverable:

- **Scarcity** (limits *quantity*) and **urgency** (limits *time*) exploit loss aversion — fear of missing out beats desire for gain. Both work best run honestly, against a real capacity ceiling or a real deadline; fake scarcity, once caught, destroys credibility permanently. In a time-boxed campaign, the final hours can account for 50–60% of total sales.
- **Bonuses**: never discount the core offer — add named bonuses instead. Identical content broken into stacked, named parts is perceived as more valuable than the same content delivered as one lump sum.
- **Guarantees** are the single highest-leverage conversion tool, and a guarantee only has teeth when it names a concrete "or-what" (not "you'll get results" but "results in 30 days or you get X"). Match guarantee type to price point and fulfillment cost — unconditional for low-ticket B2C, conditional/service ("I keep working for free until you get the result") for high-ticket or expensive-to-deliver offers, performance-based where outcomes are trackable, and an anti-guarantee ("all sales final") where a guarantee would attract the wrong buyer. Stacking two guarantees compounds perceived safety; the math nearly always favors a stronger guarantee even against higher refund rates.
- **Damaging admissions**: naming an offer's real limitation before the prospect finds it independently increases trust and conversion — the opposite of the intuitive move to hide the weakness.

→ [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Persuasion-and-Influence/Scarcity and Urgency - Engineering Demand with Supply and Deadlines|Scarcity and Urgency]], [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Persuasion-and-Influence/Risk Reversal - The Guarantee Toolkit|Risk Reversal - The Guarantee Toolkit]]

### 9. Name it, and frame the price correctly

A named offer converts better than a described one. Hormozi's MAGIC formula: **M**agnetic reason (why now), **A**vatar (who it's for), **G**oal (the dream outcome), **I**nterval (the timeframe), **C**ontainer (program type). Once named, how the price itself is framed still matters: below $100, lead with the percentage discount; above $100, lead with the dollar amount — whichever produces the bigger-looking number (the Rule of 100). A credible high reference point, shown before the real price, makes an identical price feel like a better deal.
→ [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/The Value Equation and the Grand Slam Offer|The Value Equation and the Grand Slam Offer]], [[04-Frameworks-and-Mental-Models/Persuasion-and-Influence/Practical Value - The Psychology of Deals and the Rule of 100|Practical Value and the Rule of 100]]

### 10. One offer is never the whole business — sequence the set

A single Grand Slam Offer is the unit; a business needs a deliberate sequence of four offer types — **attraction → upsell → downsell → continuity** — built and proven one stage at a time. The governing constraint: 30-day gross profit should exceed roughly twice customer acquisition cost, so every customer funds the acquisition of the next one within a month. Continuity belongs last, once the front end is already cash-positive, because collapsing the whole sequence into one launch breaks the business. A downsell restructures *what* is sold when a buyer says no, rather than discounting the same offer, which protects pricing integrity.
→ [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/The Money Model - Sequencing Offers to Fund Growth|The Money Model]]

### 11. The offer decides whether it even needs a salesperson

A high price does not automatically require a sales call. The deciding variables are buyer qualification (resources, experience, ability to evaluate), the price relative to the buyer's available money, and how much trust the brand has already supplied. High qualification, low relative price, and strong pre-existing trust favor a self-checkout offer page that answers outcome, fit, proof, price, and exclusions without a human; low qualification, high relative price, and weak trust favor an assisted sale. Self-checkout trades some conversion rate for reach, and unassisted buyers are often healthier customers — higher intent, fewer refunds.
→ [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/High-Ticket Self-Checkout - Brand and Qualification Compress the Sale|High-Ticket Self-Checkout]]

## Implications

- **Build order matters more than any single tactic.** Verify the market, then run the four-step construction procedure, then price toward resistance, then layer the four enhancers, then name it. Skipping to enhancers (adding a guarantee or urgency) on top of a weak, uncommoditized, or badly-priced core offer produces a smaller lift than fixing the core.
- **The two "$100M" and "Godfather" frameworks are convergent, not competing.** Where they diverge — Suby's loss-leader-on-sale-one vs. Hormozi's premium-anchor-first — the choice is really about which stage of [[01-Business-Strategy/Pricing-and-Offers/The Money Model - Sequencing Offers to Fund Growth|The Money Model]] the business is in: an attraction offer can afford to lose money on sale one if the backend is designed; a standalone premium offer with no backend cannot.
- **Perception-side moves are underused relative to reality-side moves.** Compressing perceived time delay and effort (the denominator), reframing price (the Rule of 100), and decommoditizing language are cheaper and faster to test than rebuilding the deliverable, and the wiki's examples (the London Underground countdown clock, "SALE" tags at unchanged prices) show they can outperform substantive changes.
- **Everything in this report assumes the market and niche are already validated** (Finding 1). None of the offer-construction, pricing, or enhancer tactics compensate for a weak, shrinking, or hard-to-target market.
